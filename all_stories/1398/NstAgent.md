## Chapter 1: The Hand I Dealt

At nine minutes past eight, Detective Jasper Ace Hartley entered the 7th Precinct as if the building had been erected specifically for his dramatic entrance.

He paused beneath the flickering lobby light, adjusted his burgundy tie, and swept one gloved hand toward the bullpen.

“Good morning, citizens, criminals, and municipal employees.”

The precinct answered with ringing phones, a coughing printer, and Officer Bell shouting that someone had stolen his yogurt.

Ace smiled. Noise was merely applause with poor manners.

He crossed the room in a tailored charcoal suit, polished shoes clicking like punctuation. His reflection followed him in the dark evidence-room window: sharp jaw, swept-back hair, and the knowing expression he had cultivated after solving three burglaries, two disappearances, and one suspicious case of poisoned cannoli. The baker had confessed, technically, but Ace had suspected him immediately. Practically the same thing.

Captain Briggs’s door opened.

“Hartley. If you’re finished admiring yourself in government glass, get in here.”

Captain Miriam Briggs had the square shoulders and exhausted eyes of a woman who had personally argued with every bad decision in the city.

She slid a file across her desk.

“Preston Ashworth. Billionaire. Found dead last night in his penthouse. Single stab wound. Door locked from the inside. No obvious weapon.”

Ace touched the file with two fingers. “At last. A case worthy of my gifts.”

“It’s a homicide, not a theater audition.”

“Those are often the same thing.”

“Work with Detective Mara Chen.”

Ace’s fingers tightened. Chen was competent, observant, and irritatingly good at noticing when he was wrong.

“A janitor found the body,” Briggs continued. “Elroy Mahmoud. Goes by Slick. Interview him before lunch. And Hartley—try not to turn the crime scene into a performance.”

“I bring order to chaos.”

“You bring monologues to chaos.”

By ten o’clock, crime-scene photographs covered Ace’s desk: Ashworth’s marble penthouse, a study lined with unopened books, and the victim collapsed beside a mahogany desk. Near his limp hand sat a small, dusty bottle.

Ace lifted the evidence bag and squinted at the label.

HAND SATANIZER, it read in peeling black letters.

The words were cramped and half-hidden by grime. Ace read them as HAND SANITIZER because the alternative was absurd, and absurdity was something that happened to other people.

Chen was on the phone near the copy machine, her back turned. Briggs was yelling about yogurt. Ace slipped the bottle free.

“A detective’s hands,” he murmured, “must remain clean enough to touch the truth.”

He pressed the pump.

A cold, greasy bead landed in his palm. It smelled of pepper, burnt sugar, and a basement during a thunderstorm.

The first change was his tie.

It shot upward as his neck lengthened, then vanished beneath a burst of blue feathers. His shoulders convulsed. His suit split with expensive-sounding pops. From his spine erupted a fan of green and bronze plumage, scattering photographs and pens.

Ace stared at one feather.

“No.”

His hands became scaled feet. His nose hardened into a beak. His legs jerked straight, and a piercing cry tore from his throat.

“NO!”

It came out as a shriek.

He lurched backward, knocked over his chair, and discovered that his new tail was wider than the workstation. He tried to seize the bottle, but his wing slapped it into a coffee mug. The mug broke.

Ace began strutting in frantic circles.

The bullpen fell silent.

Mara Chen appeared around the partition carrying two coffees. She stopped.

A peacock stood on Ace’s desk wearing one torn sleeve, half a burgundy tie, and an expression of professional betrayal.

“Ace?”

The peacock lowered its head.

“Don’t,” he warned.

It was another squawk.

Chen set down the coffees. “I’m going to ask this as a colleague. Did you just turn into a peacock?”

Ace pointed at the bottle, then himself, then made the universal gesture for this is not what it looks like. Unfortunately, the gesture involved fanning his tail.

“That clears everything up,” Chen said.

Ace charged toward the washroom. His claws clicked over the linoleum, his tail wedged between desks. Officers flattened themselves against filing cabinets. Someone applauded. Someone else began filming.

In the mirror, a magnificent peacock stared back.

Ace screamed at it until the transformation began to unwind. Feathers withdrew with a papery rustle. His beak shrank. His hands returned. He collapsed against the sink wearing a shredded shirt and the dignity of a man who had lost an argument to poultry.

Chen stood in the doorway.

“If you tell anyone,” he rasped, “I will deny everything.”

“I have already texted Briggs. And sent a photograph.”

“There are explanations.”

“Start with the part where you became a bird.”

“The evidence was chemically compromised.”

“Chemically?”

“Possibly spiritually.”

The effect faded after several humiliating minutes. Ace changed into a spare shirt and shoved loose feathers into an old case box. He returned to the photographs with his collar raised and his pride under reconstruction.

Something in Ashworth’s study tugged at him.

He bent close. The peacock’s extraordinary eyesight had seen more than his human eyes managed. Beneath the victim’s desk, almost lost in shadow, gleamed a silver cufflink.

Ace enlarged the photograph.

Stamped into the metal were two letters: IV.

“Chen. Look.”

She leaned over. “Monogrammed cufflink. Could belong to anyone.”

“Or someone named Ingrid Vale.”

“Who is Ingrid Vale?”

“Art consultant to Ashworth’s private collection.” Ace pointed at the image. “Our first beautiful little lie.”

Outside, a mop bucket squeaked past.

Elroy “Slick” Mahmoud paused in the hallway. He was narrow, silver-haired, and sharp-eyed, his janitor’s uniform pressed so neatly it seemed suspicious. He saw the discarded feather protruding from the wastebasket.

Slick’s face tightened with knowing dread.

He glanced at the bottle on Ace’s desk, then at the detective grandly explaining his discovery to Chen. While no one watched, Slick bent, picked up the feather, and slipped it into his pocket.

“God help us,” he whispered. “He used it.”

## Chapter 2: Raccoon in the Evidence Room

By ten the next morning, Detective Jasper Ace Hartley had decided the peacock had been a fluke.

Not a supernatural fluke. Ace did not believe in supernatural flukes. He believed in stress responses, exotic fumes, and regrettable interactions between evidence packaging and an exceptionally sensitive constitution.

He believed this while wearing a hat pulled low over the feathers hidden inside his desk drawer.

“Your constitution is exceptionally dramatic,” Mara Chen said.

Ace looked up from the Ashworth file. “I was poisoned by an industrial cleaning agent.”

“You shrieked and tried to court a coat rack.”

“It had a compelling silhouette.”

Chen slid a requisition form toward him. “The evidence clerk needs your signature. Captain Briggs needs the chain-of-custody report before lunch.”

Ace swept up the form. “A locked-room murder, a suspicious cufflink, and paperwork. The three-headed beast of justice.”

The 7th Precinct roared around them: phones ringing, printers coughing, officers shouting over one another in the crowded bullpen. Ace marched toward the evidence room as though entering a theater built for him. Chen followed, muttering something that sounded like “coat rack.”

The evidence room smelled of cardboard, dust, and old secrets. Ace signed the log and retrieved the Ashworth container. Inside lay photographs, the victim’s watch, a scrap of carpet, and the dusty bottle.

HAND SATANIZER, the label declared in crooked red letters.

Ace stared at it. The peacock incident had left an ache between his shoulder blades and a memory of Chen’s horrified face. But the bottle sat innocently in its bag, gleaming beneath the fluorescent lights.

“Science,” Ace told it, “is repeatable.”

He should have called Chen. Instead he tore open the bag.

The first squeeze had smelled medicinal. The second smelled like burnt sugar and wet stone. A cold prickling raced from his palm up his arm.

“Oh,” he said. “That’s not—”

His fingers shrank. Black claws punched through his nails. Gray-brown fur erupted across his arms, and his spine folded with alarming pops. His nose lengthened. His mustache vanished into a pointed muzzle. A ringed tail burst through his trousers and knocked three evidence boxes from a shelf.

Jasper Ace Hartley landed on all fours.

He was a raccoon.

For one stunned second, he saw himself reflected in a filing cabinet: masked face, twitching whiskers, and an expression of offended genius.

Then the room became a treasure vault.

A silver watch glinted in a tray. A deputy’s gold badge winked from an envelope. Somewhere behind a locked drawer, something metallic whispered of possession. Ace’s heart hammered.

Mine.

He scrambled up the shelves, scattering evidence tags. He wanted the watch, the badge, a rhinestone brooch, and especially the black key card visible through a drawer window. It was sleek and blank except for a tiny silver number: 13.

He clawed at the drawer. The lock resisted. Ace seized a bent paper clip and attacked it with the focused greed of a creature who had never encountered a shiny object he could leave alone.

The drawer sprang open. He snatched the card and stuffed it beneath his chin.

The evidence-room door burst inward.

“Jasper?” Chen called. “I heard something fall.”

The raccoon froze.

Chen stepped inside, saw the toppled boxes, and then saw him. Her hand moved toward her radio.

“Ace?”

Ace hissed.

“Of course,” Chen said faintly. “Why would today be normal?”

He bolted between her boots. Chen lunged and missed his tail by an inch. Ace darted beneath a table, sprang onto a cart, and sent a rack of labeled bags swaying.

“Don’t touch the evidence!” Chen shouted.

Ace hugged the key card tighter.

“Drop whatever you stole!”

He would sooner surrender his badge.

Chen grabbed a collapsible storage cage and swung its door wide. Ace, blinded by the sparkle of a police medallion on the far shelf, charged straight into it. Chen slammed the door and dropped the latch.

The raccoon rattled the bars furiously.

“I’m calling animal control,” Chen said.

Ace pressed his paws to the metal. His claws snagged in the wire. The room tilted. The hunger for every glittering object receded, replaced by hot, human shame.

Fur withdrew. His muzzle collapsed. His tail vanished with a humiliating pop.

Ace landed in the cage wearing a torn shirt, one shoe, and the black key card clenched in his fist.

Chen stared through the bars.

“Before you say anything,” Ace said, straightening as far as the cage allowed, “I have a perfectly rational explanation.”

“Were you a raccoon?”

“Technically, I was investigating the raccoon angle.”

“You stole evidence.”

“I recovered evidence from an unsecured drawer.”

“You broke into a locked drawer.”

“It was a suspicious drawer.”

A mop bucket squeaked in the hallway. Elroy “Slick” Mahmoud appeared, pushing his cart with the solemnity of a man arriving at a funeral he had predicted.

His eyes went directly to the bottle on the floor.

“Put that away,” Slick said.

Chen turned. “You know what it is?”

Slick’s expression tightened. “I know what it does.”

He crouched outside the cage. “Hand Satanizer. Old recipe. Occult nonsense, if you prefer official words. It forces the user to wear the shape of his strongest vice.”

Ace glanced at the stolen card. “My strongest vice is not greed.”

Slick looked at him. “No?”

Chen’s eyes narrowed. “How did your fingerprints get on evidence from Ashworth’s penthouse?”

The janitor’s hand paused on his mop handle.

“I handled the bottle before the police found it,” he said. “Ashworth hired me to move it. Smuggle it, really. He wanted it taken to a vault.”

“And instead?”

“I found him dead. Locked study. Blood on the carpet. Bottle beside him.” Slick swallowed. “I panicked and hid it in the precinct. I thought whoever killed him would come looking.”

“That makes you a suspect,” Chen said.

“Yes,” Slick replied. “It does.”

For once, Ace had no theatrical answer. Slick looked frightened, but not surprised—as though he had been waiting for the bottle to claim another fool.

Chen took the key card and tested it on the evidence-room reader. The panel flashed red, then displayed its encoded route.

ASHWORTH TOWER — FLOOR 13.

Chen frowned. “There is no floor thirteen.”

“Buildings skip it,” Ace said.

“Not in the plans. Not in the elevator registry. Not anywhere.”

Slick stared at the card. “Then Ashworth hid more than a bottle.”

Ace rose, brushing dust from his ruined trousers. His hands trembled, but his voice found its familiar polish.

“Excellent,” he declared. “A secret floor, an occult bottle, a dead billionaire, and a janitor with a confession.”

Chen handed him the card. “You’re not going anywhere near that tower alone.”

Ace looked at the black rectangle, cold and smooth against his palm. Somewhere inside him, the raccoon still wanted to keep it.

“Wouldn’t dream of it,” he said.

From the hallway, Slick watched him pocket the card. His gaze dropped briefly to the feather hidden in his breast pocket, then returned to the bottle.

“Neither,” Slick murmured, “would the thing that’s coming.”

## Chapter 3: Snake in the Tall Grass

Ashworth Tower rose from the financial district like a silver accusation. Taxis snarled around its entrance; above them, a thirteenth floor waited where no thirteenth floor officially existed.

Ace adjusted his borrowed precinct blazer. The raccoon had ruined his tailoring, but not, he felt, his authority.

“Try not to steal anything,” Chen said.

“I temporarily liberated one card.”

“While burgling a drawer.”

He marched through the revolving doors.

They had left Slick at the precinct, available for further questioning. Briggs had authorized a visit to the tower after Chen reported his admission, carefully omitting the zoological circumstances. Ace carried the bottle for identification, sealed in an evidence sleeve in his coat pocket. Chen had expressly forbidden him to touch it.

The lobby smelled of lilies and money.

Lionel Pruitt, the building manager, met them beneath a sculpture of a hand holding a golden apple. His suit looked more expensive than Ace’s monthly rent.

“I understood the police had finished their unpleasant business,” he said.

“The unpleasant business was a homicide,” Chen replied.

“Precisely. Most distressing for the residents.”

Pruitt shook Ace’s hand. His palm was cold and clammy, like something retrieved from beneath a flowerpot.

“Floor thirteen,” Ace said, resisting the urge to wipe his fingers.

“There is no such floor.”

Ace held up the card. Pruitt stopped smiling.

“Mr. Ashworth’s collection is private.”

“And Mr. Ashworth is dead.”

Chen showed him their written authorization to inspect Ashworth’s premises. After a short, bitter phone call, Pruitt escorted them to a service elevator. Ace tapped the card against its reader. Between twelve and fourteen, a previously dark button glowed.

“Mechanical mezzanine,” Pruitt said.

“With excellent taste,” Chen observed when the doors opened.

A carpeted corridor led to a reception room lined with portraits. A glass cabinet displayed ceremonial keys beside an enormous painting of Ashworth. No pipes. No machinery.

Pruitt went to summon the curator.

Ace’s palm still felt damp. While studying the portrait, he reached automatically into his pocket for the travel sanitizer he always carried. His fingers encountered a bottle. He tugged it from its loose sleeve and pressed the pump.

Chen turned at the medicinal smell.

“Ace.”

He looked down at the crooked red lettering.

“Oh, come on.”

His knees buckled. His fingers fused; his arms shortened and disappeared beneath a spreading mosaic of scales. The scream he intended became a long, outraged hiss.

The carpet rushed up.

For a heartbeat he lay inside his collapsed clothes, considering retirement. Then a forked tongue slid out of his mouth without permission.

He was a snake.

Chen shut the elevator doors before anyone downstairs could look up.

“Stay still.”

Ace tried. Unfortunately, his body now contained far more moving parts than his understanding of it did. He coiled, lurched, and lashed his tail through the open pedestal beneath the display cabinet.

The cabinet tipped into Ashworth’s portrait.

Glass shattered. The painting swung sideways; behind it, a panel jolted open.

Chen stared. “That is either excellent detective work or an insurance catastrophe.”

Ace fled through the gap. He preferred excellent detective work.

Beyond lay a windowless gallery. Silver masks watched from velvet-lined cases. A small crown bristled with teeth. A ledger lay open beneath glass, surrounded by photographs of smiling donors—bank presidents, a deputy mayor, a woman whose name adorned the city hospital.

At the center stood an empty pedestal.

HAND SATANIZER, its plaque read.

Ace smelled old wax, dust, and the same burnt sweetness that had risen from the bottle. This was where it belonged. Not a cleaning cupboard. Not a harmless billionaire’s novelty shelf.

Behind him, Chen photographed the room.

“Please stop,” a woman said.

Dr. Ingrid Vale stood in the doorway beside Pruitt. Silver-haired and immaculate, she wore gloves and a charcoal suit. Her gaze passed over the broken cabinet, the snake, and Chen’s badge. Her composure barely shifted.

“I curate this collection,” she said.

“Then explain the empty stand,” Chen replied.

“Preston wanted to destroy the bottle.”

Ace lifted his head.

“He became frightened of certain acquisitions,” Vale continued. “I advised caution.”

“Dr. Vale,” Chen said. “Ingrid Vale?”

“Yes.”

“Do you wear monogrammed cufflinks?”

Vale glanced down at her tailored cuffs. “Sometimes. Preston gave several of us presentation sets. Why?”

“We found one marked IV beneath his desk.”

“I visited him frequently. You may check my appointments.”

Her answer came smoothly, but her eyes moved to Chen’s phone.

Ace tried to demand a closer inspection. He produced a noise like escaping steam.

Pruitt stepped backward.

“Pest problem,” Chen said flatly.

Vale studied the snake. “Your janitor may offer more useful explanations. Security footage shows someone in his uniform leaving the building that evening.”

“You know Slick?”

“Mr. Mahmoud handled errands for Preston. Including objects he should never have touched.”

Ace remembered Slick’s lowered eyes, his carefully limited confession. Had fear made the man helpful—or merely inventive?

A cramp rippled through Ace’s spine. Scales retreated. His body shortened, then expanded in thoroughly objectionable directions. Chen grabbed his fallen coat and threw it over him as he became human behind the pedestal.

Pruitt made a tiny, wounded sound.

“Medical condition,” Ace gasped.

Chen handed him his trousers. “Extremely inconvenient.”

Vale said nothing at all.

While Ace dressed, Chen’s phone rang. She listened, then put the medical examiner on speaker.

“The wound wasn’t the whole story,” the examiner said. “The recovered dagger carried a rare poison. Historical references place it in occult rites. This isn't an ordinary street toxin. Full report follows.”

Chen thanked her and disconnected.

Beyond Vale’s shoulder, the donor photographs smiled in their expensive frames.

Ace had expected an affair, an inheritance, perhaps a spectacularly dishonest accountant. Instead he had an impossible floor, a curated nightmare, and poison from a dead ritual.

Slick knew more than he had told them. So did this woman, who had watched a snake become a detective without asking a single question.

Ace retrieved the bottle with a handkerchief. Chen held open the evidence sleeve.

“This time,” she said, “I carry it.”

He surrendered it without argument. That frightened him almost as much as the scales.

## Chapter 4: Pig at the Gala

The memorial gala for Preston Ashworth occupied the top three floors of the Meridian Hotel, where the chandeliers were large enough to have their own weather systems and every canapé looked as if it had been assembled by a jeweler.

Ace entered beneath a borrowed name, a borrowed tuxedo, and a mustache that had been borrowed from the theater department of the 7th Precinct’s community-relations office.

“Jasper Ace Hartley,” Mara Chen murmured beside him, “if you touch that mustache again, I will arrest you for impersonating a detective.”

“I’m undercover.”

“You’re wearing a gold cummerbund.”

“Undercover magnificently.”

Chen, in a sleek black dress with a police badge hidden beneath her clutch, scanned the ballroom. Ashworth’s wealthy friends glittered beneath the lights: financiers, council members, collectors, heirs, and one retired judge who had apparently mistaken mourning for an opportunity to display a diamond the size of a boiled egg.

At the far end of the room, Dr. Ingrid Vale stood beside a glass case containing Ashworth’s portrait. She wore a silver gown and the expression of a woman attending a funeral for someone who had inconvenienced her.

“Keep her talking,” Chen said. “I’ll find out who had access to the service corridors.”

Ace adjusted his mustache. “Try not to solve the case without me.”

“I’ll do my best to leave a clue or two.”

He approached Vale with the confident glide he reserved for witnesses, cameras, and restaurant hosts who claimed his table was not ready.

“Dr. Vale,” he said. “Detective—”

“Mr. Hartley,” she interrupted, her eyes flicking over his costume. “You’re not very good at being someone else.”

“On the contrary. Most people find me mysterious.”

“Most people find you loud.”

Before Ace could reply, a waiter swept past carrying a silver tray. Beside the champagne stood a small glass atomizer marked HAND SANITIZER.

At last, civilization.

Ace seized it and spritzed his palms. The mist smelled faintly of cinnamon, hot pennies, and something that seemed to whisper his name.

Vale’s eyes widened.

“Wait,” she said.

Ace felt a tremor begin in his fingers. His cuff links shot across the floor. His bones compressed with the brisk efficiency of luggage being shoved into an overhead compartment. His tuxedo ballooned, split, and dropped around him as a pink snout thrust through his shirtfront.

He landed on four stubby legs.

The ballroom froze.

Ace looked up at three hundred horrified faces and saw, reflected in a polished serving platter, a pig wearing half a tuxedo and one expensive shoe.

“This,” he announced, though it came out as a squeal, “is not ideal.”

Then the smell of food hit him.

Roasted figs. Truffle oil. Buttered lobster. Honey-glazed ham.

The ham was a personal insult.

Ace charged.

He shot beneath a waiter’s arm, launched himself onto the buffet, and sent a tower of miniature crab cakes sailing into the orchestra. A violinist shrieked. Someone applauded, apparently assuming the pig was part of the entertainment.

Ace devoured three cream puffs, half a lamb skewer, and an entire decorative apple before Chen caught sight of him.

“Ace!”

He tried to answer. His snout was occupied with a bowl of caviar.

The pig’s nose, however, was not merely hungry. It was magnificent. Beneath the perfume, champagne, and expensive panic, it detected a sharp metallic bitterness threaded through the air.

Poison.

The scent led across the ballroom to Vale. More precisely, to the silk scarf draped over her arm.

Ace abandoned the caviar and barreled toward her.

Vale stepped back. “Get that animal away from me!”

Ace snorted furiously. The same rare poison from Ashworth’s dagger saturated the scarf’s fringe. He knew it with the certainty of a nose that could distinguish truffle from a millionaire’s nervous sweat.

He lunged for the fabric.

Vale twisted aside. Ace’s shoulder struck a pedestal beneath Ashworth’s portrait. The portrait swung crookedly, revealing a narrow compartment in the wall. Inside, a security photograph slid free and fluttered onto the floor.

Chen picked it up.

Her face changed.

The photograph showed Ashworth’s study, timestamped the night of the murder. A figure in a janitor’s uniform stood entering the room. The face was turned away, but the cap, gray jacket, and distinctive brass buttons belonged unmistakably to Slick.

Captain Briggs, who had just arrived through the ballroom doors, took one look at the photograph.

“Where is Mahmoud?”

“Captain,” Chen began, “we need to verify—”

“Verify it at the station. Put him under arrest.”

Ace squealed and stamped his hooves. Everyone mistook it for agreement.

Vale stared down at him. For one brief instant, the elegant mask slipped. She looked not frightened, but calculating.

Ace saw it. Then a security guard saw Ace biting the hem of Vale’s scarf.

The guard grabbed him.

Ace twisted, squealed, and accidentally launched a fountain of cocktail sauce across Briggs’s white shirt.

“Enough!” Briggs roared. “Remove that pig!”

Chen tried to intervene, but two guards had already bundled Ace into a linen cart. Through a gap in the cloth, he saw a woman in a pearl necklace—Celeste Ashworth, Preston’s widow—whispering urgently with Julian Cross, a young man whose hand rested far too familiarly at the small of her back.

“…not here,” Celeste hissed.

“Then when?” Julian answered.

The cart rolled through the service exit and tipped Ace into a dumpster behind the hotel.

For several minutes he thrashed among coffee grounds, wilted flowers, and one alarmingly judgmental lobster shell. The transformation ebbed as suddenly as it had begun. Hooves became hands. Snout became nose. Pink hide became skin.

Ace emerged human, naked except for a torn undershirt, one shoe, and a generous coating of cocktail sauce.

A cook screamed. Ace screamed louder.

By the time Chen found him, he was wrapped in a tablecloth and sitting on an overturned crate.

“Briggs suspended you,” she said. “Effective immediately.”

“Did she mention the poison?”

“She mentioned the sauce.”

Ace pointed toward the hotel. “The photograph is too perfect. Slick’s uniform, the exact time, his face conveniently hidden. Someone wanted it found.”

Chen studied him. “And Vale’s scarf?”

“Poison. Same as the dagger.”

“Can you prove it?”

“Not while dressed as a condiment.”

Behind them, police vehicles roared toward the precinct to arrest Slick. Above, the gala continued in a glittering blur, while Celeste and Julian disappeared through a side door.

Ace watched it close.

The pig had wanted food. The detective wanted answers. For once, the detective’s appetite was worse.

“Chen,” he said, “we have three suspects, a framed janitor, a poisoned scarf, and a widow sneaking around with her husband’s lover.”

“You forgot the cursed bottle.”

Ace looked at the stain on his palm where the mist had touched him.

“No,” he said. “I’m trying very hard not to.”

## Chapter 5: Chameleon in the Precinct

The 7th Precinct had survived a raccoon in Evidence, a peacock in Homicide, and one pig-shaped evacuation of a memorial gala. It did not survive Ace’s return with dignity.

He came through the front doors wrapped in a hotel tablecloth, wearing one shoe, half a torn undershirt, and enough cocktail sauce to qualify as a condiment. Detectives looked up from their desks. A clerk dropped a stapler. Somewhere, a patrolman began laughing and could not stop.

Captain Briggs stood at the center of the bullpen with a phone in one hand and fury in both eyes.

“Do not,” she said, “explain anything until I have finished deciding whether to suspend you or have you examined by a veterinarian.”

“A veterinarian would be intimidated by my complexity.”

“Ace.”

He stopped. The tablecloth sagged.

Chen followed him in, carrying the security photograph. “Captain, the picture may have been planted. The figure’s face is hidden, and Vale’s scarf—”

“Your partner assaulted a buffet, destroyed hotel property, and sprayed cocktail sauce on a captain.”

“It was an investigative maneuver,” Ace said.

“It was a pig.”

“A remarkably perceptive pig.”

Briggs pointed toward the washroom. “Clean yourself up. Then report to my office.”

Ace did both, though not in that order. The washroom smelled of bleach, wet paper towels, and the particular despair of municipal plumbing. He scrubbed at his hands beneath a rattling faucet, trying to remove cocktail sauce, humiliation, and the sensation of Vale’s eyes widening when the atomizer had touched him.

Five times, he thought. Five impossible bodies. Five times his own hands had betrayed him.

On the little shelf beside the sink sat a familiar brown bottle.

Hand Sanitizer, the label appeared to say.

Ace stared at it. “No,” he told himself. “Absolutely not.”

He picked it up anyway.

The bottle was warm. Its label shimmered. HAND SATANIZER.

Ace dropped it. It bounced once, rolled in a neat circle, and came to rest against his shoe.

“This is harassment,” he whispered.

He squeezed.

A cool bead spread over his palm. His skin broke into green and yellow scales. His tongue flicked out before he could stop it. His ribs narrowed, his spine folded, and his remaining shoe fell away as four delicate feet gripped the porcelain floor.

Ace became a chameleon.

His first thought was that he had finally transformed into a detective’s ideal witness: observant, patient, and physically incapable of making a dramatic entrance.

His second thought was that the sink was enormous.

He scrambled up the pipes, clinging to the wall as his skin shifted with the paint: institutional beige, water-stained gray, a surprisingly handsome strip of blue where someone had repaired a bullet hole. He found himself nearly invisible beside the door.

Then voices drifted down the hallway.

Dr. Ingrid Vale.

Ace’s golden eye rotated independently toward the crack beneath the door. Vale stood outside, speaking into a phone. Her voice was low and smooth, the voice of a woman ordering tea while arranging a murder.

“The detective is burning through the vial,” she said. “Let him finish the seven; then we take the Hand.”

Ace’s toes tightened around the molding.

A pause.

“No. The janitor took the fall nicely. The photograph was sufficient, and Briggs has already swallowed it.”

Another pause, shorter this time.

“Once the seventh dose is spent, the vessel will be vulnerable. We collect it before he understands what he has become.”

Vale ended the call.

Ace’s tongue shot out in shock and stuck briefly to the wall.

He had to follow her. He had to reveal himself. He had to do something grand and conclusive. Instead, he remained the exact color of the paint while Vale’s heels clicked away.

The transformation faded without ceremony. Scales became skin, feet became hands, and Ace slid down the wall, landing in the mop bucket with a damp, administrative splash.

Chen opened the door.

She looked at the bucket. Then at Ace. Then at the bottle in his hand.

“No,” she said.

“I have information.”

“You have scales on your neck.”

“I also have information.”

He told her everything. The phone call. The seven doses. The Hand. The janitor taking the fall. Chen listened without interrupting, which was more alarming than disbelief.

When Ace finished, Briggs appeared behind her.

“I heard enough,” the captain said. “Hartley, you are suspended until further notice.”

“But Vale confessed to—”

“She did not confess. You were hiding in a wall.”

“A tactically excellent wall.”

“You have created four separate animal incidents in my precinct’s orbit.”

“Five,” Chen said quietly.

Ace turned. “You’re counting this one?”

Chen gave him a flat look. “You were a chameleon.”

Briggs took his badge. “Go home. Be human. Try not to lick anything.”

As Ace left the office, he passed the holding cells. Slick sat alone on the bench, wrists cuffed, his janitor’s uniform rumpled but his expression steady. He looked at Ace’s neck, where one green scale had stubbornly remained.

“You used it again,” Slick said.

Ace gripped the bars. “Tell me what the bottle is.”

Slick glanced toward the officers’ desk. “Not here.”

“There’s nowhere else. I’m suspended, you’re arrested, and I’ve just been a lizard in the captain’s washroom.”

“Chameleon.”

“Do not correct the animal.”

Slick sighed. “I was an apprentice in the Order of the Hand.”

The name seemed to lower the temperature of the corridor.

“It was a society of wealthy families,” Slick continued. “They collected relics tied to human vice. Ashworth belonged to them. Then he wanted out. He stole the Satanizer from the Order and planned to destroy it.”

“Vale killed him.”

“Vale, and whoever gave her permission. The Order does not forgive disobedience. Ashworth was murdered to keep the bottle.”

Ace looked down at his sticky palm. “How many doses?”

“Seven.”

“I’ve used five.”

Slick’s face tightened. “Then you have two choices left, detective. Stop touching it, or discover why the seventh was reserved.”

“Vale said they’ll take the Hand after seven.”

“They will come for you.”

Ace glanced toward the bullpen, where Briggs was ordering someone to remove the cocktail sauce from her paperwork. Chen stood beside the evidence board, studying Vale’s photograph. Slick sat behind bars, framed by fluorescent light like a man waiting for a verdict he had already heard.

“What happens at the final squirt?” Ace asked.

Slick’s knowing eyes met his.

“Everything,” he said. “And nothing about you will stay hidden.”

## Chapter 6: Sloth on the Clock

Ace Hartley had been suspended for forty-three minutes when he discovered that humiliation had a smell.

It smelled like lemon disinfectant, wet mop strings, and the faint cinnamon of Slick’s contraband coffee. He crouched inside the janitor’s closet beneath a shelf of toilet paper, one hand clamped over his stubborn green neck scale and the other gripping the Hand Satanizer.

The bottle seemed to grin at him from its plastic label.

“Seven doses,” he whispered. “Seven opportunities to become a public menace. Six, technically.”

Outside, the precinct thundered on without him. Phones rang. Shoes squeaked. Someone shouted for a warrant. His badge lay in Captain Briggs’s office, probably being interrogated for association.

Ace had come to the closet because it was the only place in the building where nobody expected him to be useful. He had intended to search Slick’s supplies for a spare uniform, or perhaps a mop with which to make a dignified escape. Instead, his elbow struck the shelf.

The bottle jumped.

Ace caught it badly. His thumb landed on the pump.

A cold bead splashed onto his palm.

“No,” he said.

The word took so long to finish that he heard every letter separately.

Nnnnnnnnnno.

His fingers thickened. His wrists softened. The closet expanded around him—not because it grew, but because he began occupying time differently. His knees folded with solemn inevitability. Brown-gray fur rolled over his arms, his face lengthened, and his eyes became enormous, dark pools of patient accusation.

Ace Hartley became a sloth.

The bottle slipped from his paw and landed on the floor. It took him three full seconds to look at it.

Then another three to feel alarm.

Then, with agonizing commitment, he reached for it.

The precinct door opened. Detective Mara Chen leaned in, saw a sloth hanging from the shelf, and closed the door.

It opened again.

“Ace?”

He raised one claw.

Chen stared. “That is not a persuasive gesture.”

He tried to point at the bottle. His arm moved through a philosophical discussion about direction and arrived somewhere near a bucket.

Chen shut her eyes. “I’m going to assume this is the sixth time.”

Ace nodded, eventually.

She sighed, entered the closet, and dragged in a laptop. “Briggs is looking for you. Slick says you’re an idiot. I told him that was not new information.”

Ace wanted to object. His mouth produced a soft, mournful chirp.

“Fine,” Chen said. “You’re a uniquely gifted idiot.”

She opened the security footage from Ashworth Tower. The image showed the corridor outside Preston Ashworth’s study: polished marble, a brass clock, and a figure in a janitor’s uniform passing beneath the camera. The figure’s face was hidden by a cap and mask.

Ace leaned toward the screen.

The footage played at normal speed. The figure entered, paused, and vanished through the study door.

“Nothing,” Chen said. “We’ve watched it twelve times.”

Ace reached for the track controls. His claw moved so slowly that Chen began to say, “What are you doing?” and finished the question before he touched the button.

He slowed the footage to half speed.

The disguised janitor crossed the hall.

Paused.

Not at the door. Beside it.

The figure lifted both hands and adjusted the gloves. The right hand tugged the left glove. Then the left hand tugged the right.

Ace’s great sloth eyes widened.

He rewound. It took him nearly a minute to press the key, and Chen’s impatience had time to become resignation. Again the figure appeared. Again the pause. Again the gloves.

Ace tapped the screen with one claw, then tapped his own left hand.

“Left-handed?” Chen asked.

He nodded with the force of a drifting continent.

Her expression changed. She pulled the image larger. “Slick writes right-handed. He opens jars right-handed. He mops right-handed, which I didn’t think was a thing until now.”

Ace pointed at the figure’s sleeve. The cuff caught the light. A tiny glint—perhaps a pin, perhaps a cufflink—flashed near the wrist.

IV.

Chen went very still. “Vale.”

The sloth inside Ace felt the answer settle into place. The cufflink beneath Ashworth’s desk. The poison on Vale’s scarf. Her convenient accusation against Slick. The uniform. The gloves.

Dr. Ingrid Vale had worn a janitor’s disguise, entered Ashworth’s study, and killed him with her left hand.

Ace tried to leap triumphantly from the shelf. His body declined to participate. He slid down the mop handle and landed in a pile of rags with a sound like a pillow being dropped.

Chen’s mouth twitched. “I’ll celebrate for both of us.”

The transformation began to unwind after what felt like an entire fiscal quarter. Fur receded. His arms shortened. His face snapped painfully back into place. Ace emerged human, sprawled among the rags, wearing one green scale, a crooked necktie, and the expression of a man who had just solved a murder from inside a bucket.

Slick’s voice came through the service door. “You look terrible.”

Ace crawled toward it. “I have identified the killer.”

“Then you look useful terrible.”

Chen opened the door a crack. Slick stood beyond it in handcuffs, escorted by a bored uniformed officer who had apparently been bribed with the promise of not looking too closely. The janitor’s eyes fixed on the bottle.

“Sixth dose,” Slick said. “The seventh completes the vessel.”

“Or breaks it,” Ace replied. “You said that before.”

“I said it could. The old recipe responds to intention. The first six doses drag your worst habits into the open. The seventh asks what remains when you choose someone else over yourself.”

Ace looked at his hands. They had been greedy, vain, suspicious, hungry, slippery, and now catastrophically slow. “And if I choose wrong?”

“Then the Order gets what it wants.”

Chen folded her arms. “Which is?”

Slick glanced toward the corridor. “A living Hand. A vessel they can use to bind the city’s powerful people to their will.”

The precinct noise seemed to recede. Ace imagined Vale’s calm smile, the empty pedestal, and the seven-dose countdown ticking inside a bottle small enough to hide in a pocket.

“We don’t wait for her,” he said.

Chen looked at him. “You’re suspended.”

“I am also the only detective currently qualified to infiltrate a room as a sloth.”

“No.”

“A chameleon?”

“Also no.”

Slick nodded toward Chen. “Vale’s private gallery is where the Order keeps its relics. I know a service entrance.”

Chen took the laptop. “Then we go quietly. No Briggs. No uniforms. No gala.”

Ace picked up the bottle, careful this time. Six drops were gone. One remained.

He wanted to use it. He wanted certainty, glory, a dramatic transformation that would prove he was still the greatest detective alive.

Instead, he tucked it inside his coat.

“Not yet,” he said.

For once, the words did not feel like cowardice. They felt like a plan.

## Chapter 7: The Janitor’s Tale

Slick drew the gallery on the back of a toilet-roll requisition.

“Architects,” he said, sketching a staircase, “always forget that somebody has to empty the bins.”

Beyond the closet door, the Seventh Precinct conducted three arguments, booked a pickpocket, and tried to locate the owner of a loudly ringing telephone. Ace leaned against the shelves, holding his last dose inside a padded rubber glove. The glove’s empty fingers drooped over the bottle like disappointed relatives.

“Start at the beginning,” Chen said.

“The Order of the Hand started with rich people frightened of losing things.” Slick’s handcuffs scraped the paper. “Money. Beauty. Influence. Eventually, death got on the list. They collected seven Vice Vessels, one for each deadly sin.”

“Seven bottles?” Ace asked.

“Seven objects. A mirror. A purse. A cup. Others I never handled. The Satanizer belongs to pride. Makes a man believe even his punishment ought to be spectacular.”

Ace adjusted his tie, then stopped adjusting it.

“Why did I get the whole petting zoo?”

“Pride opens the door. The rest of your failings come through it.”

Chen gave Ace a look he chose not to investigate.

Slick marked a square beneath the staircase. “I was an apprentice. They recruited tradesmen because millionaires don’t know how their own houses work. I carried boxes. Cleaned shrines. Thought they’d make me somebody.”

His pencil stopped.

“Then I saw what somebody cost.”

For a moment, only the precinct’s phones spoke.

“The patrons are bankers, developers, campaign donors,” he continued. “People with enough power to buy a city, but not enough to stop fighting over it. The living Hand is supposed to bind them together. Whoever commands it commands them.”

“Vale,” Chen said.

“Vale would certainly like the position.”

Ace studied the small square. “And Ashworth?”

“He wanted out. Asked me to steal the bottle back from the Order. I did. Then he hired me to move it to his private vault while he worked out how to destroy it.”

“You told us you were smuggling it for him.”

“I was. I left out who I’d stolen it from.” Slick met his eyes. “I thought if I kept the Order out of your report, I might live long enough to explain. When I reached his study, he was already dead.”

Ace remembered the feather Slick had watched fall, the terror he had mistaken for evasiveness.

“Next time,” he said, “try the full confession before I become wildlife.”

Slick pushed the plan toward him. “Next time, read the label. And don’t waste that last squirt.”

***

Getting Slick out required Chen’s badge, an interview-transfer form, and a lie that made her jaw tighten. The escort surrendered him at the garage, where Chen signed for custody herself.

“This is an investigative interview at a secondary location,” she told Ace.

“With burglary?”

“With consequences. Don’t make me list yours.”

She removed Slick’s cuffs inside the car. He rubbed his wrists without thanking her, which somehow made the trust between them more visible.

Vale’s gallery occupied a former bank between a boutique hotel and a restaurant where the menus had no prices. Through tall windows, Ace saw guests lifting champagne beneath a banner announcing a private auction.

“Useful,” he whispered. “Everyone’s already bidding on incriminating objects.”

At the service entrance, Slick produced a brass key.

“You kept that?” Chen asked.

“They never asked for it back.”

They entered through a kitchen smelling of hot pastry and expensive restraint. Slick took a trolley; Chen carried folded linen. Ace acquired an empty ice bucket and tried to look underpaid rather than legendary.

Beyond a curtain, an auctioneer described a silver compact as “an exceptional instrument of personal renewal.” A woman raised a paddle without asking what it cost.

Ace kept walking.

Under the staircase, Slick pressed a recessed catch behind a fire-extinguisher bracket. A narrow door opened onto stone steps.

“No cameras?” Chen whispered.

“Not when I worked here.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“No,” Slick admitted. “It wasn’t.”

Below them, the old bank vault stood open. Its steel door framed cabinets of objects that made Ace’s teeth ache: a tarnished mirror turned toward the wall, a purse bulging around nothing, a cup whose dark interior seemed deeper than its bowl.

At the center waited a stone hand, palm upward. A rectangular hollow had been cut into it, precisely the size of the bottle inside Ace’s glove.

He disliked being expected.

Chen opened a binder on a nearby desk. Each divider bore a name, a photograph, and a column of payments. Bank directors. A deputy commissioner. Two property magnates whose advertisements promised a better city.

“Membership,” she murmured, photographing the pages. “And leverage.”

Ace found Ashworth’s sheet. Across his portrait, someone had written WITHDRAWAL DENIED in neat black capitals.

Then Chen turned another page.

The binder’s metal spine lifted off a small pressure switch.

A bell struck once overhead.

Slick looked up. “That’s new.”

The vault door began to close.

Chen lunged for the threshold. A guard outside caught her wrist and twisted her against the steel. Slick shoved his trolley toward the man, but a second guard stepped from behind the door with a pistol.

“Hands.”

Slick raised his. The guard cuffed him, then Chen, with brisk familiarity.

Ace stood beside the stone shrine, the ice bucket dangling from his fingers. There was nowhere useful to throw it.

Dr. Ingrid Vale descended the stairs, applauding softly.

“Detective Hartley. Almost punctual.”

“You killed Ashworth,” he said.

“He refused to surrender the bottle.” Her eyes settled on his padded glove. “He thought ownership entitled him to destroy something greater than himself.”

“So you stabbed him.”

“Yes. Must we pretend you came here for the sculpture?”

Chen strained against the guard. His pistol moved beneath her jaw.

Ace set down the bucket very carefully.

Vale smiled at that small surrender. “Put the final dose on your hand.”

His fingers tightened around the rubber glove. For once, he could think of nothing impressive to say.

“Use it,” she said, “or watch your friends die.”

## Chapter 8: The Gallery of Vice

The pistol beneath Mara Chen’s jaw was small, black, and perfectly steady.

Ace found that steadiness insulting. His own hands, by contrast, had become independent citizens. One trembled against the padded rubber glove containing the Satanizer. The other hovered uselessly at his side, as if waiting for applause.

“Detective Hartley,” said Dr. Ingrid Vale, “you have reached the final room of the final lesson.”

The vault beneath her gallery smelled of wax, old stone, and money that had never met a tax inspector. Around them stood the Order’s collection: a silver crown with teeth, a mirror that reflected strangers, a golden scale holding a single black feather. At the center waited an empty shrine shaped like an enormous open hand.

Slick knelt beside Chen, his wrists cuffed behind him. Two guards held him down. His janitor’s cap had fallen over one eye, giving him the look of a disgruntled bird.

“Could we skip the lesson?” Ace asked. “I’ve always learned best under moderate pressure.”

Vale smiled. “You have used six doses. Pride, greed, envy, gluttony, deceit, and sloth—each one peeling away another layer of you. The seventh will make the vessel complete.”

“A vessel for what?” Chen demanded.

Vale turned toward the empty shrine. “For the Hand. A living instrument bound to the Order’s will. Once the ritual is complete, every patron who has paid for a place in this room will share its dominion.”

Upstairs, beyond the vault ceiling, music thudded faintly through the stone. The private auction continued. Champagne glasses chimed. The city’s richest predators were bidding on curses while ordinary people rode crowded trains beneath them.

“Dominion over what?” Ace asked, though he already disliked the answer.

“The city,” Vale said. “Its courts. Its contracts. Its police. Its elections. Desire travels upward through money, Detective. We merely intend to give it a hand.”

Chen’s eyes narrowed. “Ashworth refused.”

“Ashworth became sentimental.” Vale’s smile thinned. “He stole the Satanizer from the Order’s vault and planned to destroy it. He believed one man’s conscience outweighed generations of power.”

“And you stabbed him,” Ace said.

“I corrected him.”

The words landed harder than the threat of the gun. Ace remembered the photograph: Ashworth’s desk, the hidden cufflink, the locked room. He remembered the poisoned dagger and the neat trail leading to Slick. He remembered Vale’s gloved left hand adjusting itself on the tower footage.

“You framed Slick to keep the police looking at the mop bucket instead of the museum,” Ace said.

Vale inclined her head. “His uniform was convenient. His history made him believable. And he had already been foolish enough to touch the bottle.”

Slick gave a low grunt. “You always did mistake quiet for stupid.”

One guard laughed. The other pressed a pistol against Slick’s temple.

Ace’s gaze dropped to the cuffs. They were standard precinct issue, dull steel, double-locked. Slick’s fingers moved behind his back. Not much. A twitch, a pause, another twitch.

Ace knew that expression. It was the same one Slick wore when studying a clogged drain.

“What are you doing?” Chen whispered.

“Thinking about maintenance,” Slick murmured.

Vale raised her pistol toward Ace. “The glove. Remove it.”

Ace tightened his grip around the padded bundle.

“You want me to transform?”

“I want you to finish becoming useful.”

“And if I decline?”

“Then Chen dies first. Slick second. You last, if the Order is feeling theatrical.”

Ace glanced at Chen. Her jaw was tight, but she met his eyes. No plea. No panic. Just a clear, furious instruction: do not let Vale choose the terms.

Something struck stone.

Slick had kicked a display case.

It toppled with a tremendous crash, spilling brass instruments and crystal bottles across the floor. One guard spun toward the noise. The other flinched. Slick twisted his wrists, and the cuffs sprang open with a tiny, triumphant click.

“Janitor’s trick,” he said, lunging sideways. “Never trust a lock near a maintenance corridor.”

He seized a fallen velvet rope and yanked. A stand collapsed. The mirror shattered. Reflections burst across the floor in silver triangles.

Chen drove her shoulder into the guard beside her. His pistol skittered away. Vale fired. The shot cracked through the vault, punching a hole in the golden scale.

Ace moved.

Not elegantly. Not with the balletic precision he had imagined for his heroic moments. He stumbled behind the shrine, slipped on a glass stopper, and collided with the guard’s knees. The man went down swearing. Ace snatched the dropped pistol, then immediately lost it when a crystal skull bounced off his wrist.

But his gloved hand found the bottle.

The Satanizer was warm.

Its label—HAND SATANIZER, the letters crooked and black—seemed to pulse beneath the glass. Inside, a final bead of dark liquid clung to the bottom like a tiny, patient eye.

Vale stood on the other side of the wreckage, pistol aimed at Chen now. “Go on,” she said. “Use it.”

Ace held the bottle up.

“You expect me to become what? A tiger? A rhinoceros? Something with antlers and a surprisingly poor sense of indoor etiquette?”

“I expect you to choose yourself.”

The accusation found its mark because it was not entirely false. Ace had chosen himself every time: his reputation, his instincts, his magnificent theories. Even his transformations had become performances in which he remained the star.

Slick, crouched beside Chen, looked at him through the drifting dust.

“Humility isn’t thinking less of yourself,” Slick had said in the closet. “It’s thinking of yourself less.”

Ace looked at Chen. At Slick. At the broken artifacts, the empty shrine, and the city hidden above them, full of people who would never know how close a jeweled hand had come to closing around their throats.

His fear remained. So did his vanity, his panic, and the green scale at his neck.

But beneath them, something steadier appeared.

Vale’s smile faltered. “Hartley.”

Ace pulled the stopper.

“I have a purpose,” he said. “It isn’t escape.”

He removed the glove and squeezed the last drop onto his bare palm.

“It isn’t glory.”

The liquid struck his skin like ice, then fire.

“It’s them.”

For one impossible second, every sound in the vault vanished. The guards froze. Chen’s breath stopped. Slick’s broken handcuff swung silently from his wrist.

The dark drop spread across Ace’s palm in the shape of five tiny branching lines.

Then the gallery screamed.

## Chapter 9: The Seventh Squirt

The final drop landed in Ace Hartley’s palm.

For one heartbeat, nothing happened.

Then the vault screamed.

The sound came from the walls—from the black stone beneath the shrine, from the brass hands carved into the floor, from every artifact trembling in its case. Dust leapt from the ceiling. The empty pedestal split down the middle.

Ace’s fingers locked.

A fan of blue-green feathers burst across his shoulders. He heard himself shriek like a peacock and saw his reflection in a fallen silver tray: one eye enormous, his neck iridescent, his expression offended beyond language.

The feathers vanished.

A ring-tailed mask swallowed his face. His nose twitched. His hands curled toward a glittering reliquary, and some furious, furry part of him wanted to steal it, bite it, and hide it.

The mask snapped away.

Scales flashed over his cheeks. His tongue forked between his teeth. The room narrowed into heat, scent, and the pistol in Vale’s hand.

Then came the pig: pink nose, bristling ears, an overwhelming perfume of truffles, gun oil, and fear.

The chameleon followed, skin changing color with the fractured light. The sloth dragged itself through his face, making every second enormous and heavy.

Peacock. Raccoon. Snake. Pig. Chameleon. Sloth.

The six masks flickered faster until they became a storm of borrowed faces. Each offered its old weakness like a familiar coat. Strut. Snatch. Strike. Charge. Hide. Wait.

Ace clung to one thought.

Protect them.

Not Ace Hartley, brilliant detective. Not the man who would get headlines or a statue with an aggressively heroic jaw. Chen. Slick. The people outside this vault who had no idea what waited beneath the gallery.

The storm stopped.

Ace stood in the settling dust, entirely human.

His suit was torn. Feathers dissolved when he touched them. A single green scale slid from his neck and struck the floor like glass.

Vale stared. “What are you?”

“Currently?” Ace looked at his ordinary, shaking hands. “A detective.”

The bottle in his palm went cold. Its label, Hand Satanizer, curled inward as if ashamed.

Suddenly the case was a diagram, every line lit from within.

The cufflink beneath Ashworth’s desk: IV. Not a random initial, but Vale’s deliberate signature, placed where only a patient investigator—or a peacock with excellent eyesight—would find it.

The disguised janitor on the security footage. The pause at the door. The gloves adjusted with the left hand.

Slick was right-handed. Vale was not.

The black key card had been planted to point toward Slick and the impossible thirteenth floor. Vale knew police would chase the mystery, and that Slick’s fingerprints on the bottle would make him look guilty. She had made the frame so elaborate it resembled truth.

Ace turned toward her. “You entered Ashworth’s study dressed as Slick. You used the left hand he never would have used. You stabbed Ashworth with the ritual dagger, then planted the key card to frame him.”

Vale’s face tightened.

“And the poison?”

Ace smelled it again—not with a pig’s nose, but with its memory. Bitter flowers. Copper. A cold medicinal sweetness.

“On your scarf,” he said. “The same rare poison from the dagger. You wore the evidence to the gala because you thought no one would recognize it.”

For the first time, Vale looked frightened.

Then she lunged.

Her pistol swung toward Chen. Ace moved before thought could dress the movement in drama. He caught Vale’s wrist, turned his shoulder, and stepped aside. No flourish, no shouted declaration—just weight and timing. Vale hit the floor hard enough to rattle the brass hands.

Ace pinned her wrists with one knee.

“You have no idea what you’ve destroyed!” she hissed.

“Actually,” Ace said, breathing hard, “I’m beginning to.”

Behind him, Slick hooked a guard’s ankles with a mop handle. Chen finished the rest, wrenching the man’s arm behind him and securing it with display cord.

The second guard raised his weapon. Slick threw a bucket. It struck the guard’s knees. Chen caught the gun, and Slick sat on the man’s chest.

“Occupational hazard,” he said.

The vault doors boomed. Voices shouted. Boots pounded down the gallery stairs. Vale twisted toward the entrance, hope flaring in her eyes.

“Those are my people,” she hissed.

“No,” said Chen, checking the captured pistol. “Those are ours.”

The doors burst open. Captain Briggs led uniformed officers into the vault, her weapon drawn and her expression sharp. Behind her came Internal Affairs—and Deputy Commissioner Harlan, whose name Chen had photographed in the Order’s binder.

Briggs saw Vale pinned beneath Ace. “Hartley.”

“Captain.”

“You’re suspended.”

“Yes.”

“You’re also sitting on Dr. Vale.”

“She lunged.”

“That does seem to happen around you.”

Officers took Vale. Chen handed over the pistol. Slick produced the membership photographs. The final proof came from Vale’s scarf, still carrying the poison’s residue. The binder named her patrons, Ashworth’s withdrawal denial explained the motive, and Vale’s confession—recorded on Chen’s phone—left no escape.

As officers arrested the wealthy names upstairs, the Hand Satanizer gave a metallic sigh.

Ace looked down.

The bottle crumbled in his hand. Glass became gray powder, then blew away across the broken vault.

No pain followed. No feathers, scales, claws, or sudden urge to hide a badge.

Only silence.

Slick studied him. “How do you feel?”

Ace flexed his fingers. “Clean.”

Chen smiled tiredly. “That’s a first.”

He looked at the wrecked shrine and Vale being led away. He had lost the curse, the badge, and perhaps the last shreds of his old reputation.

But Chen was alive. Slick was free. Ashworth’s killer was exposed.

For once, that was enough.

Ace glanced at Briggs. “Captain, I believe I have a statement.”

Briggs sighed. “Make it short.”

Ace nodded. “I’ll try.”

## Chapter 10: Clean Hands

The morning after the vault exploded into police paperwork, the 7th Precinct smelled like wet wool, burnt coffee, and victory trying to disguise itself as bureaucracy.

Uniformed officers carried boxes through the lobby. Accountants from Internal Affairs carried larger boxes. Three impeccably dressed men who had never before touched a precinct doorknob sat handcuffed on a bench beneath a sign reading PLEASE DO NOT LEAN ON THE GLASS. One of them was a deputy commissioner. He leaned on the glass anyway.

The Order of the Hand had not survived the night. The membership binder, Chen’s photographs, Vale’s recorded confession, and the poison residue on her scarf had turned a secret society into a parade of indictments. Warrants were being served at towers, clubs, museums, and one yacht whose captain had insisted that the vessel was technically registered as a “floating cultural foundation.”

In the center of the commotion, Elroy “Slick” Mahmoud pushed a mop across the marble floor.

He wore his usual gray coveralls. His mustache was combed. His expression suggested that the collapse of a criminal empire was, at most, an inconvenient spill.

Ace watched him from the edge of the squad room, where he had been instructed to remain until Captain Briggs finished reading his statement for the fourth time. Ace had no badge, no gun, and no official reason to be there. He did, however, have a borrowed necktie and a bandage on his knuckle.

Slick glanced up. “You’re blocking the clean part.”

“I’m observing,” Ace said.

“You’re standing.”

“Detectively.”

Slick leaned on the mop. “There’s something you should know before you start giving speeches about my criminal past.”

Ace’s eyebrows rose. “I have never given a speech about anyone’s criminal past without a dramatic pause.”

“Ashworth didn’t hire me merely to smuggle the bottle. I was his agent inside the Order.”

Ace looked toward the boxes, then back. “You were undercover?”

“Underpaid.”

Slick explained that Ashworth had recruited him years earlier, knowing Slick’s apprenticeship in the Order gave him access no police detective could obtain. He had copied ledgers, photographed ritual rooms, and quietly passed warnings to Ashworth. When Ashworth decided to destroy the Satanizer, Slick was meant to help him disappear from the Order’s reach. Vale had discovered the plan first.

“I found him after she killed him,” Slick said. “Then I panicked. Not because I was guilty. Because I knew exactly who would be blamed.”

“A janitor with fingerprints on an occult murder weapon,” Ace said.

“And poor posture.”

“Your posture is excellent.”

Slick gave him a quiet wink and resumed mopping. “The police only notice the floor when it’s dirty.”

Ace watched the mop glide over the marble, erasing footprints left by officers, lawyers, and men who had believed themselves untouchable. For once, he did not mistake invisibility for insignificance.

By noon, Briggs summoned him into her office. Chen was already there, seated beside a stack of evidence folders. She had her pistol returned, her recorder in an evidence bag, and the calm expression of someone who had slept for eleven minutes and considered it luxurious.

Briggs held up Ace’s badge between two fingers.

“You were suspended,” she said.

“Yes, Captain.”

“You trespassed into a private gallery.”

“Yes, Captain.”

“You used an occult cleaning product seven times, became six different animals, destroyed property, interfered with an arrest, and tackled a suspect while technically unarmed.”

Ace glanced at Chen. “The tackling was technically elegant.”

“It was not,” Chen said.

Briggs set the badge on the desk. “You also identified the killer, protected two officers, recovered evidence, and gave a coherent statement for the first time in your career.”

Ace waited.

“Your suspension is lifted,” Briggs said. “Your badge is restored. Your weapon comes back after psychological evaluation.”

“Reasonable.”

“Do not sound disappointed.”

“I’m working on a new relationship with reason.”

Chen smiled despite herself. Briggs did not, though one corner of her mouth appeared to consider the possibility.

The press conference took place in the lobby because the mayor’s office had decided the precinct’s cracked tile made the victory look “authentic.” Reporters crowded behind the yellow line. Cameras blinked. Somewhere, a microphone squealed like a trapped animal, and Ace felt an old reflex rise in him—the urge to step forward, lift his chin, and let the story become a statue of Jasper Ace Hartley.

Then he saw Chen beside him. He saw Slick behind the cameras, polishing a brass rail. He remembered the vault, the pistol, the terrible quiet before the final drop. He remembered choosing protection over applause.

Ace stepped to the microphone.

“This case was solved by Detective Mara Chen,” he said, “whose evidence work and recording established the Order’s crimes; by Elroy Mahmoud, who risked his life as Preston Ashworth’s agent and helped expose the organization; and by Captain Briggs, who kept the rest of us from making things worse.”

Briggs’s eyes narrowed. “A difficult task.”

The reporters laughed. Ace let them. It felt better than hearing his own name.

A journalist shouted, “Detective Hartley, what happened to the animal transformations? Are you still dangerous?”

“Only around buffets,” Ace said.

Another called, “Where is the old peacock?”

Ace looked down at his hands. They were ordinary hands now: nicked knuckles, soap-dry skin, a faint crescent of grime beneath one fingernail.

“I washed my hands of him,” he said.

This time, the laughter came softer. Chen heard the meaning beneath it. So did Slick.

That evening, after the last reporters left, Ace returned to his desk. The precinct had quieted to its nighttime rhythm: ringing phones, humming lights, the distant groan of plumbing. Slick had mopped the squad room until the floor reflected the ceiling.

Ace opened the bottom drawer beside the Ashworth case files. From an evidence envelope, he removed one blue-green peacock feather—the feather Slick had found months ago and later returned to him with no explanation beyond, “You dropped it.”

Ace placed it in the drawer.

Not a trophy. Not evidence. A reminder.

He closed the file, reached for the bottle beside his typewriter, and froze.

HAND SANITIZER, the label read.

Ace turned it over. Checked the cap. Read the ingredients. Held it up to the light.

From the doorway, Chen said, “It’s ordinary sanitizer.”

“I know.”

“You’ve read the label three times.”

“Four. I’m being thorough.”

He pressed the pump. A clean, sharp scent filled the air. Ace rubbed the gel into his palms and laughed—not loudly, not theatrically, but with the startled relief of a man discovering that his hands belonged to him again.

Outside, the city glittered in its towers and traffic. Inside, beneath the shine, grime gathered in corners, under nails, behind polished names. Ace understood now that truth rarely lived where people displayed it. It hid in the mop water, the locked drawer, the overlooked witness, the hand reaching left when everyone expected right.

Chen picked up her coat. “Coming?”

Ace looked once at the drawer.

“After you,” he said.

And for the first time, he meant it.
