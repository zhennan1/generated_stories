## Chapter 1: The Hand I Dealt

The Hand I Dealt

At nine seventeen on Tuesday morning, Detective Jasper Ace Hartley entered the 7th Precinct as if the building had been constructed to frame his silhouette.

He wore a charcoal suit, a crimson tie, and a fedora tilted at what he considered a dangerous angle. His shoes clicked across the marble floor. Officers looked up. A clerk muttered, “Oh, good. The peacock’s here.”

Ace stopped beneath the brass precinct seal and gave the room a gracious nod.

“Please,” he said. “No applause before the arrest.”

Detective Mara Chen passed him with three folders balanced against one hip. “That must be why nobody started.”

“You’re welcome to learn from my composure.”

“I’m trying to learn how you maintain it while wearing two different socks.”

Ace glanced down. One was black. The other had tiny yellow ducks.

“A deliberate psychological tactic,” he said. “It unsettles suspects.”

“Mostly me.”

Captain Briggs emerged from his office carrying a thick file. “Ashworth.”

The room seemed to tighten around the name. Preston Ashworth owned half the city’s glass towers and had been found dead in the locked penthouse of Ashworth Tower just before dawn.

Briggs handed Ace the file. “You’re lead.”

“At last.”

“You’re lead because everyone else is busy. Victim was stabbed once. No forced entry. There’s a bottle beside the body nobody can identify. Review the scene photos, interview the staff, and refrain from doing anything theatrical.”

“Theatricality is merely competence with better posture.”

“Try not to contaminate the evidence.”

Briggs vanished into his office. Ace carried the file to his desk and opened it while the precinct roared around him: ringing phones, chattering printers, officers shouting over one another, and an ancient radiator hissing in protest.

The crime-scene photographs showed Ashworth sprawled beside his library desk, one hand curled toward the dagger wound in his chest. Beyond the windows, the city glittered and crawled beneath a gray morning sky.

The bottle appeared in three photographs.

It was squat and brown, with a black cap and a faded white label. Dust filmed its shoulders. HAND SATANIZER was printed in crooked red letters beneath a crude drawing of a horned hand.

Ace lifted it from the evidence bag.

“Novelty sanitizer,” he murmured. “Ashworth had a sense of humor, allegedly.”

The plastic felt cold. There were no ingredients or manufacturer’s name, only a faded symbol resembling an eye.

Ace glanced around. Chen was on the phone by the windows. Briggs remained in his office. Nobody was watching.

His hands, he decided, were filthy from the subway railing.

He squeezed the bottle.

A drop landed in his palm.

For three seconds, nothing happened.

Ace smirked. “See? The supernatural remains—”

Heat shot up his arm. His fingers snapped together. His shoulders convulsed.

Feathers burst through his shirt in a blue-green explosion. His sleeves tore. His neck stretched with a wet pop. His nose hardened into a beak.

Ace stared at his reflection in the dark computer monitor.

A peacock stared back.

Then Ace screamed.

The sound came out shrill and furious, sending three officers ducking beneath their desks. The peacock flapped onto the desktop, scattering papers. His tail unfurled behind him and knocked over a mug labeled WORLD’S OKAYEST DETECTIVE.

Chen spun around. “Ace?”

“Kraaa!”

Her eyes widened. “Ace?”

He pecked at the evidence bag, launched it into the air, and skidded across a stack of subpoenas. Then he leaped onto a filing cabinet, caught his tail on the ceiling fan, and began circling the squad room like a furious feathered chandelier.

When the transformation ended, it ended all at once. Feathers sank into skin, beak became nose, and claws became fingers. Ace dropped from the fan and landed in a heap of paperwork, naked from the waist up.

Chen stood over him.

“I’m going to ask a question,” she said, “and I need you to answer carefully.”

Ace grabbed a desk blotter and covered himself. “The bottle is defective.”

“You became a peacock.”

“A highly trained investigative bird.”

“You screamed at Officer Dugan.”

“He was threatening me with a stapler.”

“He was holding a stapler.”

“Exactly.”

Chen looked toward the watching officers. “Everyone back to work.”

They turned away with the unnatural obedience of people who intended to discuss nothing else for years.

Ace pulled on his jacket, trying to recover his dignity. “The label is clearly a typo.”

“Hand Satanizer?”

“Obviously.”

He reached for the crime-scene photographs, then stopped.

During the transformation, the room had sharpened. Colors had blazed. Every movement had seemed precise. He had seen the photographs from a height no human eye could manage.

One image returned to him: Ashworth’s desk, its carved legs and shadowed underside.

“Wait,” he said.

Chen picked up the photograph. Ace leaned close.

Under the desk, nearly hidden in shadow, lay a silver cufflink. Its face bore two engraved letters.

I.V.

Chen’s expression changed. “Ingrid Vale?”

“Art consultant,” Ace said. “She curated Ashworth’s private collection.”

“Could be initials.”

“Could be coincidence.”

“Do you believe in coincidence?”

Ace straightened, wrapping his torn jacket around himself. “I believe coincidence is what lesser detectives call evidence before I explain it to them.”

“You’re going to interview her.”

“I’m going to solve this.”

He set the photograph beside the Satanizer. His hand was still trembling, though he refused to acknowledge it.

In the hallway, a mop bucket squeaked.

Elroy “Slick” Mahmoud pushed it past the squad room in blue coveralls and rubber gloves. At the doorway, he paused.

A single peacock feather shimmered beneath Ace’s desk.

Slick bent and picked it up. He glanced at Ace, who was attempting to knot his shredded tie while pretending the entire precinct had not witnessed his metamorphosis.

The janitor slipped the feather into his pocket.

His mild amusement had vanished.

“Hand Satanizer,” he whispered.

Then he pushed his bucket onward, leaving behind the scent of lemon disinfectant—and a look of knowing dread.

## Chapter 2: Raccoon in the Evidence Room

By noon, Detective Jasper “Ace” Hartley had decided the peacock incident was behind him.

“The human mind,” he told Detective Mara Chen, “is a magnificent machine. Occasionally, under stress, it misfires.”

Chen looked up from the Ashworth statements. “You grew feathers.”

“A stress response.”

“You flew onto the commissioner’s filing cabinet.”

“Strategic elevation.”

“And you laid an egg.”

Ace’s jaw tightened. “That remains unconfirmed.”

Phones rang and officers shouted across the bustling 7th Precinct. Ace gathered the case file and marched toward the evidence room, determined to restore his dignity through productive police work.

The evidence room was quieter than the squad floor, its concrete walls humming beneath fluorescent lights. Metal shelves held sealed bags of clothing, weapons, phones, and substances no one wished to identify. At the rear stood a locked cabinet marked ASHWORTH—PENDING REVIEW.

Ace checked the corridor, then unlocked it.

Inside lay the victim’s watch, photographs, a sealed carpet sample, the silver cufflink engraved I.V.—and the brown bottle labeled HAND SATANIZER.

Ace stared at it.

The peacock had been a fluke. A contaminated batch, perhaps. An allergic reaction. Some unusual interaction between stress and excellent bone structure.

He picked up the bottle.

“Science,” he whispered.

He squeezed.

A cold jet struck his palm.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Ace smiled.

Then his spine folded.

His shoulders hunched as gray-brown fur burst across his skin. His arms shortened into nimble paws, his nose thrust forward, and whiskers sprang from his cheeks. His trousers collapsed around his shrinking legs. A striped tail whipped out behind him, knocking evidence boxes from the shelf.

Ace tried to scream.

A furious chitter came out instead.

He looked down at a raccoon’s paws.

His first thought was that this was inconvenient.

His second was that the evidence room contained an astonishing quantity of silver.

He scrambled toward a rack of confiscated jewelry. Diamond earrings glittered through plastic. A watch flashed beneath its tag. Loose coins waited in an open tray.

Ace seized everything.

The sensation overwhelmed him. Every shiny object seemed precious. Every locked drawer concealed treasure. Every item in the room belonged to him.

Then he saw the black key card.

It rested in a locked drawer labeled ASHWORTH FINANCIAL MATERIALS, dark as oil and edged in silver. Ace abandoned the jewelry and attacked the drawer. His claws failed. He found an evidence hook, jammed it into the lock, and pried until the mechanism sprang open.

He emerged clutching the card.

The evidence-room door opened.

Chen stood there.

She stared at the raccoon. The raccoon stared back.

“Ace?” she said.

Ace tucked the card beneath his belly.

Chen’s expression hardened. “Oh, no.”

He bolted.

The chase tore between the shelves. Chen lunged and caught only his tail, which stretched before slipping free. Ace scrambled up a rack, bounced off a box of confiscated umbrellas, and landed on a rolling cart.

“Stop!” Chen shouted.

Ace chittered and raced toward the rear storage cage.

Thinking quickly, Chen shoved the cart sideways. It struck the cage door, which swung open. Ace dashed inside. Chen slammed it shut.

The raccoon flung itself against the bars.

“Don’t make me call Animal Control,” Chen warned.

Ace attacked the lock.

Then the curse began to fade.

Fur receded in rippling patches. The tail vanished. Paws lengthened into fingers, and his muzzle pulled inward with an alarming snap. A moment later, Jasper Ace Hartley lay curled on the cage floor, naked except for one sock, the key card clenched in his hand.

Chen closed her eyes.

“Say nothing,” Ace ordered.

“I was going to call an ambulance.”

“I am perfectly healthy.”

“You were a raccoon.”

“A highly trained investigative raccoon.”

“You stole evidence.”

Ace examined the card. “I recovered evidence.”

“You also tried to eat a watch.”

“It appeared valuable.”

A lemon-disinfectant scent drifted through the room.

Elroy “Slick” Mahmoud pushed his mop bucket inside. He surveyed the overturned shelves, scattered jewelry, and Ace wrapped in an evidence blanket. Then he saw the brown bottle.

His face went still.

“Hand Satanizer,” he said.

Ace’s amusement vanished. “You recognize it.”

Slick rested both hands on his mop handle. “Old recipe. Older than this city. It makes a person embody whatever sin is strongest in him. Vanity. Greed. Rage.”

Chen looked at Ace.

“It forced me into a criminal mindset,” Ace said. “An investigative technique.”

Slick ignored him. “Ashworth hired me to move that bottle.”

“To where?” Chen asked.

“A vault. He said it had to disappear before the Order found it.” Slick’s gaze dropped. “I went to his penthouse the night he died. Found him on the library floor. I panicked, took the bottle, and hid it here.”

Chen unlocked the cage but kept one hand near her weapon. “Your fingerprints are on the bottle. You were at the penthouse. You knew about the artifact. That makes you a suspect.”

Slick nodded. “I expected as much.”

Ace dressed behind the shelving unit, still holding the black card. “The killer planted it.”

Chen turned. “What?”

Ace lifted the card. A silver numeral gleamed beneath the fluorescent light.

XIII.

“There’s no thirteenth floor at Ashworth Tower,” Chen said.

“Officially,” Slick murmured.

Ace looked from the card to the bottle’s horned-hand symbol. His panic sharpened into something useful.

“Then,” he said, buttoning his shirt with as much dignity as one sock allowed, “we’ve found a floor that doesn’t exist.”

Slick’s mop bucket squeaked as he backed toward the door.

“And somebody,” he said, “who very much does.”

## Chapter 3: Snake in the Tall Grass

Ashworth Tower’s elevator had no button for thirteen.

It did, however, have a slot that accepted the black key card Ace had recovered through what he insisted on calling unconventional fieldwork.

“Stolen,” Chen said.

“Recovered.”

“With tiny criminal hands.”

The elevator chimed. Between twelve and fourteen, a hidden button glowed.

Ace straightened his borrowed tie. After the evidence-room incident, he had acquired trousers from Lost Property and a shirt commemorating the precinct’s charity bowling tournament. The tie was an attempt to restore institutional authority.

“Whatever happens,” Chen said, “don’t touch anything.”

“An unnecessarily broad restriction for a detective.”

“An unusually necessary one for you.”

The doors opened onto marble, discreet lighting, and a man whose suit looked expensive enough to require its own insurance.

“Lionel Voss. Building manager.” He positioned himself across their path. “This is a private floor.”

“Homicide,” Ace said, presenting his badge. “Notoriously public.”

Voss shook his hand with the damp enthusiasm of a refrigerated squid.

“Mr. Ashworth valued discretion.”

“He’s dead. His priorities have shifted.”

Chen stepped between them before Voss could object. “We need access to every room on this floor.”

As she discussed warrants, Ace wiped his palm against his trousers. Nothing improved. He reached into his coat, found a bottle by touch, and pressed the pump.

The label turned toward him.

HAND SATANIZER.

“Oh, you have got to—”

His fingers fused.

The floor rushed upward. His trousers collapsed around him. A hiss escaped where a dignified objection should have been.

For one impossible second, Ace remained upright as a rapidly narrowing column of detective. Then he spilled out of his collar in a length of green scales.

Voss screamed.

Chen shut her eyes briefly.

“I gave you,” she said, “one instruction.”

Ace tried to explain that the bottle was being transported as evidence, that his ordinary sanitizer occupied the same pocket, and that pocket design was a neglected public safety issue.

What emerged was, “Sssss.”

The marble against his belly was horribly cold. Every footstep reached him through the floor. He flicked his tongue and tasted shoe polish, fear, and the lingering metallic trace of Voss’s handshake.

Voss grabbed a brass umbrella stand.

Chen drew her weapon. “Put that down.”

“There’s a snake!”

“He’s a detective.”

Voss stared at her.

“We’re reviewing recruitment standards.”

Ace recoiled from the raised stand and found his body coiling without permission. A furious certainty surged through him: this pompous little man had insulted him, threatened him, and possessed offensively warm ankles.

He lunged.

Voss jumped backward into a pedestal. A glass display case struck the marble with a crack that rang through Ace’s jaw.

The silver ceremonial bowl inside rolled beneath a large portrait of Preston Ashworth. The frame swung outward.

Behind it, a narrow seam opened in the wall.

Everyone went still.

Chen lowered her gun.

“Well,” she said. “That’s almost police work.”

***

Chen sent Voss downstairs to secure the elevator, then pushed the portrait farther open.

Ace followed her through the gap, trying not to think about how easily he fitted beneath a door.

The hidden gallery smelled of old wood and extinguished candles. Glass cabinets held a tarnished goblet, an ivory comb, a purse sewn with gold thread. Each rested alone beneath a small spotlight. On the far wall, a bronze hand extended its fingers around an engraved eye.

Ace knew that symbol.

So did Chen. She photographed it before approaching an empty velvet stand.

The plaque read:

THE HAND SATANIZER  
VESSEL VII  
PROVENANCE RESTRICTED

Below it were seven shallow circles cut into the wood.

Chen photographed those too.

Footsteps sounded outside.

“I see Lionel’s talent for discretion remains theoretical.”

The woman in the doorway wore a charcoal suit and pale gloves. Her gaze passed over the broken case, the open portrait, and finally the snake beside Chen’s shoe.

“Dr. Ingrid Vale,” she said. “Mr. Ashworth’s curator.”

Ace lifted his head.

Vale.

He pictured the silver cufflink under Ashworth’s desk. I.V.

“What an unusual security arrangement,” Vale said.

“Temporary,” Chen replied. “Why isn’t this room on the building plans?”

“Preston collected objects whose owners preferred anonymity.”

“Stolen objects?”

“Not invariably.”

Ace hated her immediately. It was partly professional instinct and partly the way she watched him without moving her feet.

Vale approached the empty stand.

“Then it is gone.”

“What is?”

“A bottle. Brown glass. An unfortunate label.”

Chen’s expression remained flat. “What do you know about it?”

“That Preston intended to destroy it.”

Ace’s tongue flicked.

Slick had said Ashworth wanted it moved to a vault.

“Why?” Chen asked.

“Toward the end, he became frightened of his collection. He thought certain acquisitions had begun making demands.”

“Did you?”

“I thought he needed sleep.”

Vale adjusted one glove. Ace noticed an empty buttonhole at her cuff.

“Did you lose a cufflink recently?” Chen asked.

“A silver one. At Preston’s, last week.” Her answer came promptly. “Have you found it?”

“We’re asking everyone.”

“Then ask his janitor. Elroy Mahmoud. Security footage shows a man in his uniform leaving the service elevator that night.”

Ace held still.

Vale looked down at him again.

“Preston trusted people who knew how to appear useful.”

***

The transformation ended behind a packing screen while Chen kept Vale outside.

Ace dressed with frantic gratitude, checked every pocket, and handed Chen the bottle in its evidence bag.

“Separate compartment,” she said.

“Separate continent.”

Her phone rang.

The medical examiner’s voice carried clearly in the quiet gallery.

“The wound wasn’t the whole story. There’s a rare poison on the dagger. Historical records associate it with occult rites. Not something you buy on a street corner.”

Chen looked toward the cabinets.

“Could it have come from a private antiquities collection?”

“That’s where I’d start.”

After she hung up, Ace studied a photograph displayed beside the bronze hand. Ashworth stood among smiling donors at a museum benefit: a judge, a deputy mayor, two banking chairmen. Vale stood at his shoulder.

Each wore a small pin shaped like a hand.

“Slick knew about the bottle,” Ace said. “He lied about why Ashworth wanted it moved. Now there’s footage.”

“Footage of a uniform,” Chen said. “We haven’t seen the person inside it.”

He nodded reluctantly.

Outside, traffic moved far below the windows, thousands of people going about their afternoon beneath a floor that officially did not exist.

Ace looked back at the smiling faces in the photograph.

“We need names,” he said.

For once, he did not mean his own.

## Chapter 4: Pig at the Gala

The memorial gala for Preston Ashworth occupied the Bellwether Hotel’s top floor, beneath chandeliers large enough to interrogate. Champagne circulated through the city’s richest mourners, each wearing black and calculating how grief might affect an inheritance.

Detective Jasper “Ace” Hartley entered in a borrowed tuxedo and an expression of profound belonging.

“Try not to arrest anyone,” Detective Mara Chen murmured.

“I’m undercover.”

His name tag read: **DETECTIVE J. HARTLEY**.

Chen stared at it. “We’re here to observe Ashworth’s associates. The judge, the deputy mayor, the bankers, Dr. Vale. No grandstanding.”

“I never grandstand.”

“You once announced a burglary investigation through a bullhorn.”

“That was tactical punctuation.”

A string quartet played near a wall of white roses. Hand-shaped pins appeared everywhere among the guests, glittering on lapels and shawls. Ace scanned the crowd until he saw Dr. Ingrid Vale near a marble column. She wore a dark green gown, pale gloves, and a silk scarf. Her empty cuff buttonhole caught the light.

Vale noticed him and smiled. “Detective Hartley. Have you come to pay your respects?”

“I’ve come to ensure the deceased receives justice.”

“Preston would have found that reassuring.”

For an instant, her smile faltered. Then a waiter passed with a tray, and she turned away.

“Observe,” Chen warned.

“I am observing.”

“You’re standing like a man about to leap onto a table.”

Ace retreated toward the restroom corridor. His reflection in the mirrored wall looked magnificent, if slightly tense. Inside the restroom, a clear pump bottle sat beside the sinks, labeled HAND SANITIZER.

He reached for it without looking.

The spray struck his palm.

Nothing happened. Ace smiled at himself in the mirror.

Then his stomach growled.

His fingers thickened. His knuckles swelled. His tuxedo tightened across his shoulders, then everywhere else. His nose pushed forward, his ears folded, and coarse pink bristles erupted along his face.

Ace stared at the mirror.

“No,” he tried to say.

A wet, thunderous snort answered him.

The guest at the next sink screamed. Ace screamed too, though his sounded like a farmyard under attack.

He charged through the restroom door on four short legs.

The gala erupted. A woman dropped her champagne. A banker climbed onto a chair. Someone shouted for security.

Ace smelled the buffet.

The scent struck with supernatural force: roast beef, butter, truffles, smoked salmon, hot pastry, sugared pears, and a cheese with an almost personal appeal. Hunger seized him. He plunged into the buffet.

Plates flew. Oysters collapsed. He swallowed three canapés and emerged from a shrimp bowl wearing cocktail onions like medals.

“Jasper!” Chen shouted.

He turned with a carving knife clenched in his teeth.

“Drop that!”

Ace spat it onto a platter and squealed.

Across the room, Dr. Vale watched him. Not with fear. With calculation.

Then Ace smelled something beneath her perfume and the food: silk, dust, and a sharp mineral bitterness.

The poison from Ashworth’s murder.

Vale’s gloved hand rose to her scarf. Ace followed the scent through the crowd, shoving past a donor and a woman wearing enough diamonds to require structural support.

“Detective,” Vale said, backing away. “Control your animal.”

Ace charged.

He might have reached the scarf, but a waiter stepped backward. Ace struck the man’s legs. The tray flew, and Ace skidded into a display of framed photographs.

The display crashed.

Chen reached the wreckage first. She picked up one photograph and went still.

“Ace.”

He was halfway inside a champagne fountain, bubbles foaming from his snout.

Chen held up the photograph. It showed Ashworth’s study through a security camera. A figure in a janitor’s uniform entered the room.

The timestamp read 11:47 p.m.—the estimated time of Ashworth’s death.

Captain Briggs pushed through the crowd, his face darkening as he took in the ruined buffet, the pig, and the photograph.

“Where is Slick?” he demanded.

“In the precinct,” Chen said.

“Have him arrested.”

Ace wheeled around, knocking over a bowl of cocktail sauce. Red liquid splashed across his white shirtfront.

Briggs pointed toward the service exit. “And get that animal out of here!”

As two guards herded Ace away, he saw Dr. Vale beside the column, one hand pressed over her scarf. Her composure had returned.

Near the far wall, Celeste Ashworth had slipped away from the condolence line. She was whispering fiercely with Julian Cross, the young man from the museum-benefit photograph. Celeste gripped his wrist. Julian looked toward Vale, then toward the fallen security photograph.

Ace noticed everything from beneath a table as the guards closed in.

The photograph was too neat.

Slick’s uniform. The exact time of death. A convenient crash that had exposed it. Vale’s poison-scented scarf. Every clue seemed designed to point in one direction.

A guard grabbed Ace by the hindquarters.

Humiliation overcame hunger.

He launched himself through the service door and down the alley steps, landing in a dumpster filled with wet napkins and yesterday’s flowers. The lid slammed shut.

In the darkness, his snout twitched. He remembered the poison, Vale’s calm eyes, Celeste’s frightened whisper, and Julian watching the photograph fall.

Then his hooves became hands.

His snout receded. The bristles vanished. His torn, sauce-stained tuxedo settled around a thoroughly human Detective Hartley.

He climbed from the dumpster with a lettuce leaf on his shoulder.

Captain Briggs waited at the alley mouth.

“You’re suspended,” he said.

“On what grounds?”

“Animal-related chaos. Destruction of evidence. Assaulting a buffet.”

“The buffet was obstructing justice.”

Briggs stared at him.

Ace wiped cocktail sauce from his cheek and looked toward the glowing gala windows. “Slick didn’t kill Ashworth.”

“And you know that because?”

Ace watched a siren’s red reflection pulse across the hotel glass.

“Because whoever planted that photograph wanted us to stop looking.”

## Chapter 5: Chameleon in the Precinct

By midnight, the Seventh Precinct smelled of burnt coffee, wet wool, and the shrimp cocktail lodged in Detective Jasper Hartley’s left shoe.

“Suspended,” Ace told his reflection in the squad-room washroom. “Pending clarification.”

His reflection, wearing the remains of a rented tuxedo, offered no reassurance.

Chen stood in the doorway holding an evidence bag.

“Briggs said you could collect your things. He did not say you could interrogate the desk sergeant about secret societies.”

“I asked whether he’d noticed any sinister jewelry.”

“You asked him to remove his wedding ring.”

“It had a hand on it.”

“It had his wife’s fingerprint.”

Ace bent over the sink. Tomato-red water ran off his cuffs.

Chen set the evidence bag beside the basin and took out her phone.

“Here’s another problem. This is the original bottle. Seal intact since I bagged it at the tower. So whatever was in that hotel dispenser—”

“Was not an unfortunate coincidence.”

“No. Don’t touch this.”

Her phone vibrated. She glanced at the screen.

“Property clerk. Stay here.”

She stepped into the corridor.

Ace looked at the brown bottle through its plastic wrapping. HAND SATANIZER. Even the typography seemed smug.

He had lost his badge, his reputation, and possibly the deposit on his trousers. Somewhere, a murderer was enjoying the evening.

“You don’t get to be the only one who understands what’s happening,” he told the bottle.

He tore open the bag.

This was not an accident, he would later admit. The accident was believing that made it better.

He pressed the pump and rubbed the cold gel between his hands.

For one hopeful second, nothing happened.

Then his thumbs swung apart.

“Oh, that’s medically upsetting.”

His fingers fused into opposing bundles. His skin prickled green. The sink rose around him as though the entire washroom had begun standing up.

Ace tried to shout for Chen.

What emerged was a tiny, indignant hiss.

His tuxedo collapsed over him.

He struggled free of a sleeve and found himself on the basin’s porcelain lip, gripping it with feet that appeared to have been designed by a committee unfamiliar with feet.

A chameleon.

He could see the door with one eye and his own curled tail with the other.

This was useful, horrifying, and almost certainly against departmental policy.

Footsteps approached. Ace lunged toward the wall, expecting to fall, and discovered that his new hands disagreed. He climbed.

Below him, a probationary officer entered, surveyed the discarded formalwear, and backed out.

“I’m not doing another animal report,” the young man said.

Ace would have applauded his instincts, but his skin had just turned the exact beige of municipal neglect.

He went still.

For the first time all evening, nobody was looking at him.

Through the open doorway, he saw Dr. Ingrid Vale.

She stood beside the drinking fountain in a charcoal coat. The silk scarf was tucked beneath its collar. One pale-gloved hand held a phone.

“Yes, they’ve arrested Mahmoud,” she said quietly.

Ace’s tail tightened.

Vale glanced toward the squad room.

“The janitor took the fall nicely.”

His other eye swung toward her so abruptly that he nearly lost his grip.

“No. Don’t interfere.” Her voice sharpened. “The detective is burning through the vial. Let him finish the seven; then we take the Hand.”

A trolley rattled past, pushed by someone who was not Slick. Vale waited for it to clear.

“He’s vain, frightened, and determined to prove himself. You couldn’t devise a more reliable mechanism.”

Ace opened his mouth.

His tongue shot out and caught a drain fly.

He spent several appalling seconds reconsidering the wisdom of breathing.

When he looked again, Vale was walking toward reception, her phone already put away.

Chen returned carrying a property receipt.

She saw the clothes.

Then the opened bag.

“Hartley.”

From the wall, Ace gave a subdued hiss.

Chen closed her eyes.

“I left you alone for forty seconds.”

***

Reverting behind a mop bucket was an experience Ace would not recommend, particularly without advance warning to his knees.

Chen supplied his emergency gym clothes from his locker and stood guard while he dressed.

“Vale said seven,” he whispered. “And she said Slick took the fall.”

“You heard those exact words?”

He repeated the conversation. Chen typed it into her phone without interrupting.

“She was here giving a supplementary statement,” she said. “Briggs will have her arrival and departure logged.”

“Then we arrest her.”

“We establish evidence she can’t explain away. You being a listening lizard is going to challenge the affidavit.”

She sealed the bottle in a fresh bag.

“This stays out of your hands.”

For once, Ace did not object.

Captain Briggs intercepted them outside his office.

“I told you to go home.”

“Vale is involved.”

“And five minutes ago, an officer reported a lizard wearing your trousers.”

“Near my trousers. There’s an important distinction.”

Briggs held out his hand.

“Badge. Weapon. Now.”

Ace glanced toward Chen. She gave a small, unhappy nod.

The badge felt heavier coming out than it ever had going on.

“Your suspension is now in writing,” Briggs said. “No interviews. No scenes. No more animal-related chaos in my precinct.”

Ace handed over his weapon.

“May I at least apologize to the janitor?”

Briggs looked at the stained tuxedo hanging from Chen’s arm.

“Briefly.”

***

Slick sat in holding with his hands folded between his knees.

Without his mop, he looked older.

“I heard about the pig,” he said.

“Everyone heard about the pig.”

“Not everyone. My sister’s asleep.”

Ace sat opposite the bars. Chen remained by the doorway.

“Vale called it the Hand. She wants me to finish seven.”

Slick’s expression emptied.

“How many?”

“Five.”

The janitor lowered his head.

“Tell me the truth,” Ace said. No flourish. No threat. “Please.”

“I was an apprentice in the Order of the Hand. Before I learned floors were cleaner work.”

“The people with the pins?”

“Wealthy families. Judges. Bankers. People who think owning a city means they ought to own everyone in it.”

“And Ashworth?”

“One of them. Until he wanted out. He hired me because I knew what the bottle was.”

“You said you were taking it to a vault.”

“I was. To keep it from them until he could destroy it.” Slick looked up. “He was afraid they’d kill him first. He was right.”

“Vale?”

“I don’t know whose hand held the knife. I know whose orders mattered.”

Ace looked toward the bag Chen carried.

“Seven doses?”

“Seven. You’ve used five.”

Outside holding, a telephone rang unanswered.

“What happens at seven?”

Slick gripped the bars.

“The final squirt changes everything.”

For once, Ace had nothing clever to say.

## Chapter 6: Sloth on the Clock

Slick’s janitorial closet had three rules posted above the mop sink:

**NO SMOKING.**  
**NO PERSONAL ITEMS.**  
**NO DETECTIVES.**

Ace Hartley had violated the third.

Suspended, disarmed, and wearing gym clothes, he sat on an overturned bucket between a vacuum cleaner and a crate of industrial sponges. The case files lay across the floor: Ashworth’s autopsy, gala photographs, building plans, and the security footage Chen had smuggled him on a thumb drive.

He reached for the ordinary sanitizer on the shelf.

His elbow struck an evidence pouch.

The pouch fell into his lap. Inside it rested the brown bottle with the horned hand and staring eye.

“No,” Ace said.

The bottle rolled. He caught it.

The pump depressed.

A cold drop landed on his palm.

His fingers shortened. His shoulders folded. His spine clicked into a curve, and coarse brown fur spread over his skin. The closet rose around him as his limbs lengthened and his gym shoes became distant objects dangling from his feet.

Ace dropped the bottle.

It took so long to hit the floor that he had time to regret every decision of his adult life.

The closet door opened.

Chen looked in. “Ace?”

He turned his head with painful slowness.

Her eyes narrowed. “You used it again.”

Ace lifted one long, furry arm.

“You’re a sloth.”

He nodded.

Chen stared at him for three seconds. “I’m going to count to three.”

Ace pointed toward the laptop.

His claw moved across the floor at the speed of a drifting continent.

Chen sighed and set the laptop down. “You have fifteen minutes before Briggs notices I’m gone.”

By the time she had connected the drive, Ace had reached the computer. The security footage showed a corridor outside Ashworth’s study. The timestamp read 11:47 p.m.

A janitor entered the frame.

Slick’s uniform. Slick’s cap. Slick’s broad shoulders.

The figure paused at the study door.

“Again,” Ace said.

Chen rewound it.

“Slower.”

She reduced the playback speed. The disguised janitor stopped beside the door and glanced down the corridor. Then the figure raised a hand and tugged a glove tight around the fingers.

The left hand.

Ace’s eyes, now strangely independent, fixed on the detail.

“Freeze it,” he said.

Chen did.

The image showed a pale glove, a narrow wrist, and a small movement of the thumb beneath the cuff.

“Slick is right-handed,” Ace said.

“He could have used his left.”

“He opens every supply closet with his right. Signs with his right. Scratches his left elbow with his right because he says his left hand has the grace of a drunk crab.”

Chen looked at the frozen image. “The killer wore his uniform.”

The figure slid a key card through the reader and entered Ashworth’s study. Minutes later, the figure emerged with something concealed beneath the coat.

Chen replayed the moment. “Vale.”

Ace nodded. “The left hand. The cufflink. The poison on her scarf. She framed Slick.”

The transformation began to fade. Fur receded, claws shortened, and Ace’s limbs returned to their familiar proportions. He collapsed onto the floor, human again except for his gym shorts and a powerful longing for lettuce.

Chen tossed him a cleaning smock.

“Slick will be offended,” Ace said, pulling it on.

“He’ll be offended you used his closet as a laboratory.”

“The laboratory used me.”

They hurried to holding. Slick stood behind the bars, gripping them with both hands.

“Tell me you did not use the bottle again,” he said.

Ace raised one finger.

Slick’s face tightened. “Six.”

“Accidental.”

“Your accidents have become remarkably consistent.”

Chen showed him the frozen footage. Slick studied the gloved hand and closed his eyes.

“Vale,” he said.

“You knew?” Chen asked.

“I knew whose orders mattered. Knowing is not proving.”

“What happens at seven?” Ace asked.

Slick glanced at the security camera before lowering his voice. “The Order calls it completion. The first six transformations strip away the surface sins. The seventh binds them together. The person becomes a vessel for the Hand.”

“The Hand meaning the Order?” Chen asked.

“Their power. Their will. Their claim over everyone they control.”

Ace looked through the bars at the Satanizer, sealed again inside its pouch.

“And if the seventh dose is taken?”

“It completes the curse,” Slick said. “Unless the person uses it with the right intention.”

Chen frowned. “That sounds suspiciously convenient.”

“Old magic is full of loopholes. The final dose can bind the vessel to the Order—or break the chain. It depends whether the user reaches for power or protection.”

Ace’s gaze returned to the footage. Vale’s gloved left hand paused on the screen, calm and deliberate.

“What counts as protection?”

“Using it for someone besides yourself.”

For once, Ace did not answer with a joke.

Chen gathered the files. “We need evidence that will survive court. The footage proves a disguised woman entered the study. It doesn’t prove Vale was inside the uniform.”

“Where does she keep the Order’s records?” Ace asked.

Slick’s expression changed. “Her private gallery. Beneath the public collection. Relics, ledgers, names, and a shrine for the vessels.”

“A shrine,” Chen repeated.

“The Order will be there,” Slick said. “If Vale has been waiting for the seventh dose, she’ll want the bottle in that gallery.”

Chen looked at Ace. “You’re suspended, disarmed, and one squirt away from becoming something worse than a sloth.”

“I’m aware of two of those things.”

“All three.”

Ace picked up the evidence pouch without touching the pump. “Then we don’t waste the last dose.”

Slick gave him a grim smile. “That’s what Ashworth said.”

Chen tucked the thumb drive into her coat. “I can get us inside Vale’s gallery tonight.”

“Us?” Ace asked.

“You are not going alone.”

Slick tapped the bars. “And if you’re entering a private gallery owned by the woman who framed me and murdered Ashworth, you’ll need a janitor.”

“You’ll need someone who knows which doors are real,” Ace said.

Slick nodded.

Outside, the precinct thundered with phones, footsteps, and shouted orders. Inside the holding corridor, the three of them leaned over the case files while the sixth dose faded from Ace’s blood.

The seventh waited in its brown bottle, quiet as a held breath.

## Chapter 7: The Janitor's Tale

Slick waited until the holding-cell corridor emptied before beginning his story.

“Not here,” he said.

Ace glanced at the bars. “You’re in a holding cell.”

“I mean not where the walls have ears.”

“At this precinct, the walls have warrants.”

Slick reached beneath the cot and slid out a folded sheet of yellowing paper. Chen unfolded it across the bench. It was a hand-drawn floor plan of Dr. Vale’s gallery, with corridors twisting beneath the public rooms.

“You had this the whole time?” she asked.

“I had many things. Most were confiscated by people who don’t know the difference between evidence and personal property.”

Ace leaned over the map. “There are three galleries.”

“Three public galleries,” Slick said. “The private one is below them.”

Chen looked at him. “Start at the beginning.”

Slick’s usual slyness vanished.

“The Order of the Hand is older than this city. Seven families founded it around seven objects called Vice Vessels. Each one embodies a deadly sin.”

He nodded toward the bottle in Chen’s evidence bag.

“Hand Satanizer is the seventh. The first six transformations strip away what a person pretends to be. The seventh makes the truth permanent.”

Ace’s stomach tightened. “Permanent animal?”

“Permanent vessel.”

“That sounds worse.”

“It is. The Order believes a completed vessel can command people who surrender to their appetites. Bankers, judges, mayors—anyone with a weakness and something to lose. The city’s elite become handles. The Order holds them.”

Chen studied him. “And Vale wants the bottle for that?”

“Vale wants the bottle because Ashworth refused to give it to her.”

Slick’s scarred hands folded together.

“Ashworth belonged to the Order. Then he learned what they planned. He hired me to retrieve the bottle from a vault in Prague and hide it until he could destroy it.”

Ace blinked. “You were an international thief?”

“I was an apprentice.”

“You’re a janitor.”

“I contain multitudes.”

“Did you steal it?”

“I was supposed to. Ashworth had already taken it. He found me after that and asked for my help.”

“And then Vale killed him,” Chen said.

Slick met her eyes. “Yes.”

“Knowing and proving are different,” Ace said. “One is detective work. The other is paperwork, where justice goes to die.”

Slick tapped the map. “Tonight, Vale holds a private auction. Her guests will bid on art, relics, favors, and secrets. The real business happens below.”

Chen folded the plan. “We get the records and photograph everything.”

“And if we find the dagger?”

“We take it to Briggs and hope he remembers you’re useful.”

“I’m always useful.”

“You became a sloth in a mop closet.”

“An unusually insightful sloth.”

Slick gripped the bars. “Listen to me, Ace. The seventh dose is not a trick. Don’t waste it because you’re frightened. Don’t use it to save yourself. The Hand knows the difference.”

Ace looked at the sealed bottle. Its horned eye seemed to stare through the plastic.

“What if there isn’t another way?”

“There is always another way,” Slick said. “It may simply be more painful and less theatrical.”

By eleven, Vale’s gallery glittered with chandeliers shaped like clasped hands.

Chen wore a black dress borrowed from property control. Ace wore a tuxedo belonging to a detective twice his size. Its sleeves ended above his wrists, and the collar seemed determined to strangle him.

“You look like a waiter who discovered tax fraud,” Chen said.

“I look undercover.”

“You look upholstered.”

They entered through the service door while Slick pushed a cart of champagne toward the kitchen. He gave Ace one quick wink before vanishing behind a curtain.

The auction filled the main gallery. Bronze masks watched from the walls. A judge laughed beside a banker whose lapel pin bore the Order’s symbol.

Ace kept his hands visible. Every jeweled box and silver knife seemed to tug at his fingers. The raccoon itch returned.

Chen touched his elbow. “Focus.”

“I’m focusing on not stealing anything.”

“You’re sweating.”

“I’m dedicated.”

Their cue came when Slick dropped a tray.

Glass shattered. Guests shrieked. Music stuttered.

Chen slipped through a side door, and Ace followed. Behind a black marble panel, she found a concealed latch. The panel opened onto stairs descending beneath the gallery.

The vault smelled of dust, candle wax, and metal.

Shelves held bone masks, sealed jars of black fluid, an iron crown, and a wooden hand with pearl fingernails. On the far wall sat a binder thick enough to stop a bullet.

Chen opened it.

Names filled the pages: judges, council members, police commissioners, banking families, news executives. Beside each were debts, addictions, lovers, and other merciless notes.

“This isn’t a membership list,” she whispered. “It’s leverage.”

At the room’s center stood an empty stone shrine. Seven shallow circles had been carved into its base.

Above it, gold letters read:

**VESSEL VII: A CITY IN THE PALM.**

Ace felt the room tilt.

Then the alarm began.

Red light washed over the vault. Steel shutters slammed across the stairs. Three guards entered through a second doorway, weapons raised.

“Detective Chen,” said the tallest. “Put it down.”

Ace stepped in front of her. “Gentlemen, you’ve mistaken us for—”

Chen kicked his ankle.

He stopped talking.

The guards moved fast. One struck Chen’s wrist, sending her pistol skidding away. Another caught Slick as he came through the service passage and shoved him against a shelf. Bottles rattled.

Ace reached for the Satanizer.

A gun pressed into his ribs.

“Careful,” said a woman’s voice.

Dr. Ingrid Vale descended the stairs in a silver gown. Her empty cufflink hole had been filled with a black stone shaped like an eye.

She looked at the binder, the shrine, and finally Ace.

“Ashworth refused to surrender the bottle,” she said. “He thought destroying it would make him free.”

“You murdered him.”

“I corrected a betrayal.”

“You framed Slick.”

“He was convenient.”

Slick struggled against the guard. “You always confused convenience with loyalty.”

Vale smiled. “And now our detective has arrived with the final dose.”

Ace’s hand remained inches from the bottle.

Vale gestured toward Chen and Slick. “Use it.”

Chen shook her head. “Don’t.”

“Use it,” Vale repeated, raising her chin. “Or watch your friends die before the auction ends.”

The bottle gleamed inside its evidence bag beneath the red alarm light.

Ace heard his heartbeat.

For once, it did not sound like applause.

## Chapter 8: The Gallery of Vice

The vault smelled of dust, gun oil, and money.

Ace stood beneath the inscription **VESSEL VII: A CITY IN THE PALM**, with three guns aimed at him and Dr. Ingrid Vale smiling as if she had won a dull auction.

Chen knelt beside an overturned shelf, her hands bound behind her. Slick sat against a cabinet, wrists cuffed, his janitor’s cap crooked. Between them lay the evidence bag containing the Hand Satanizer.

Ace stared at the bottle.

“Detective,” Vale said, “you have one remaining dose. You know what it does.”

“I know it gives me an unfortunate relationship with wildlife.”

“You have mistaken symptoms for purpose.”

“Peacocks, raccoons, snakes, pigs, chameleons, sloths. That’s a fairly committed symptom.”

Vale’s smile sharpened. “The Order of the Hand has spent three centuries refining the Vessel ritual. The first six transformations strip away a person’s lies. Vanity. Greed. Fury. Appetite. Concealment. Apathy.”

Ace glanced at Slick. “You called them sins.”

“I called them warnings,” Slick said. “There’s a difference.”

Vale gestured toward the binder scattered across the floor. Its pages listed judges, bankers, commissioners, and council members beside their debts, addictions, affairs, and fears.

“The seventh completes the process,” she said. “The vessel becomes an instrument. Every weakness is a handle. Through the Hand Satanizer, the Order can bind the appetites of others and steer them.”

“Give us a city full of handles,” Chen said, “and the city moves when you flex.”

“Precisely.”

Above them, beyond layers of stone and velvet, wealthy guests were bidding on stolen relics.

“And Ashworth?” Ace asked.

“A sentimental fool. He stole the Vessel and intended to destroy it.”

“He hired Slick to hide it,” Ace said.

“He hired a thief.”

“Former thief,” Slick corrected.

Vale ignored him. “Ashworth began contacting prosecutors and journalists. So I corrected his betrayal.”

“Stabbed him with a ritual dagger,” Ace said. “Poisoned him with the same substance on your scarf. Then planted the key card and dressed someone in Slick’s uniform.”

“Your frame job was elegant,” Chen added, “until the glove.”

Vale’s expression tightened.

Ace saw the whole chain: the silver **I.V.** cufflink beneath Ashworth’s desk, the left-handed figure tightening a glove in the corridor footage, the poison on Vale’s scarf, her calm interest in every transformation.

“Ashworth thought the bottle could be destroyed,” Vale said. “It cannot. It can only be completed or broken. Completion requires someone who has passed through all seven gates.”

“You’ve been watching me.”

“Of course. You were wonderfully predictable.”

“I am many things. Predictable is not one of them.”

“You sprayed yourself with the Vessel because you thought it was sanitizer. You used it again because you wanted answers, then proof, then your reputation back.” She tilted her head. “The seventh dose will give you what you have always wanted.”

Ace glanced at his torn tuxedo and chocolate-stained cuff. “A better tailor?”

“Glory. Authority. The right to be unquestioned. Use the Vessel, and the Order will make you indispensable. Refuse, and your friends die.”

Chen met his gaze and gave the smallest shake of her head.

Slick lowered his eyes.

Ace noticed his thumb moving strangely.

Not struggling. Measuring.

A thin metal sliver emerged from beneath Slick’s thumbnail. Ace remembered the janitor’s lesson: the best tools were the ones nobody noticed.

“Last chance,” Vale said. “Pick up the bottle.”

Slick’s hand jerked.

The display case behind him toppled with a spectacular crash. A porcelain mask shattered. Silver relics clanged across the floor. One guard spun toward the noise; another stepped backward onto a bronze bowl.

Chen kicked the bowl into his ankle. He fell.

Slick rolled sideways, snapping one cuff against the cabinet corner. The restraint sprang open.

“Janitorial safety violation,” he announced.

The remaining guard raised his weapon.

Ace slammed into the evidence table. It struck the guard’s knees, sending him into a rack of labeled jars. Black liquid sloshed as an alarm began to shriek.

Vale fired.

The shot punched through the table inches from Ace’s hand.

He dove across the floor and seized the evidence bag. With one desperate pull, he tore it open. The brown bottle gleamed beneath the red emergency lights, its horned hand and eye symbol staring up at him.

His skin prickled. Feathers seemed to itch along his shoulders. Greedy fingers, a snake’s cold patience, a pig’s appetite, a chameleon’s vanishing instinct, and a sloth’s heavy calm stirred beneath his skin.

“Use it,” Vale ordered, aiming at Chen. “Become whatever you need. Escape. Win. Be magnificent.”

For once, the words did not tempt him.

Ace remembered Slick behind the holding-cell bars.

*The Vessel listens for the hand reaching for itself.*

He remembered Chen helping him despite every ridiculous disaster, and Slick pocketing the fallen peacock feather instead of using it against him. He remembered mistaking admiration for usefulness.

The pump waited beneath his thumb.

Ace looked at Chen, then Slick, then the guards struggling among the relics. He did not want glory or authority. He wanted the people in this room alive—and the Order dragged into daylight.

“I’m washing my hands,” Ace said.

He pressed the pump.

A single dark drop fell onto his palm.

The vault went silent.

## Chapter 9: The Seventh Squirt

The drop struck Ace’s palm.

For one breath, nothing happened.

Then the vault inhaled.

The alarm stretched into a single metallic note. Feathers burst around Ace’s face, blue and green beneath the flickering lights. His eyes sharpened, revealing every trembling trigger finger, every bead of sweat, every thread caught on Chen’s jacket.

“Not again!” he shrieked—in a peacock’s voice.

Vale staggered backward. “Yes. Take it. Let the Vessel open.”

Feathers collapsed into fur. Ace’s hands curled into paws, and every glittering object in the vault became irresistible: guns, jeweled masks, a banker’s gold watch. He lunged toward the nearest prize, chittering.

Scales flashed across his cheeks.

His pupils narrowed. The guards became threats, the weapons invitations. Heat filled his throat, and he coiled to strike.

Then he snorted.

His jaw broadened, his body swelled, and the scent of food overwhelmed him. Somewhere above, the auction’s untouched buffet called to him. He wanted to charge, chew, and devour everything.

A guard shouted, “Shoot him!”

Ace’s face rippled green. His features blended into the stone wall. For a moment, hiding seemed perfect. Let the others fight. Let the city burn without him.

Then time slowed.

His thoughts dragged through syrup. The vault moved at a sloth’s pace, giving him time to see what the bottle had shown him: the peacock, raccoon, snake, pig, chameleon, sloth.

Not monsters. Pieces of himself.

Vanity. Greed. Anger. Appetite. Concealment. Apathy.

He had blamed the bottle for every transformation, but it had only forced him to stand inside what he preferred not to see.

The sloth’s heaviness lifted. The masks fell away.

Ace stood human again, his suit torn and his hair singed. His palm bore only a faint red mark shaped like an eye. His mind, however, was clearer than it had ever been.

Vale stared. “What did you do?”

“Refused the promotion.”

The nearest guard raised his pistol.

Ace stepped aside as the shot cracked. He seized the guard’s wrist and drove the man’s elbow against the stone shrine. The gun skidded beneath a cabinet.

A second guard lunged. Ace hooked his ankle and let gravity finish the takedown. The man hit the floor with a grunt.

Slick, still working free of one cuff, smiled. “You can surrender, or I can explain how to remove blood from expensive carpeting.”

The third guard dropped his weapon.

Vale did not.

She snatched a ceremonial blade from the fallen display case and rushed Ace. He noticed everything at once: her weight favoring the right foot, the stiffness in her right shoulder, the knife in her left hand, and the dark residue on her cuff.

The poison.

The murder assembled itself in his mind.

Vale had disguised herself in Slick’s uniform and used the XIII card to reach Ashworth’s hidden floor. She had entered the study, stabbed him from the left with the poisoned ritual dagger, and lost her I.V. cufflink when he fought back. Afterward, she planted the key card to frame Slick, then paused at the door to adjust her gloves with her left hand.

Slick had found the body only after she was gone.

“Your mistake,” Ace said, catching her wrist, “was assuming a frame only needs to look convincing.”

Vale twisted, trying to slash his throat. Ace turned with her momentum, sent her shoulder-first into the floor, and pinned her wrist behind her back.

The takedown was so plain that, for a moment, nobody spoke.

Vale bucked beneath him. “You have no idea what you’ve destroyed! The Order owns judges, banks, half the city—”

“Good,” Ace said. “Then half the city can testify.”

Chen, still bound, gave him an exhausted look. “Could you untie me before the speech?”

Slick freed his remaining cuff and crossed the vault with a ring of keys that seemed to appear from nowhere. He unlocked Chen, then collected the fallen weapons.

A pounding began above them. Shouted commands followed. Police stormed the auction room, and officers poured into the vault. They disarmed the guards, secured the relics, and dragged masked members of the Order from behind their wealth and influence.

Captain Briggs arrived last, staring from the wreckage to Ace kneeling on Dr. Vale.

“Hartley,” he said. “Why am I not surprised?”

“Because I bring closure to every case.”

“You bring property damage.”

“Closure with property damage.”

Chen handed Briggs the evidence binder. Slick followed with the poison-stained scarf, the dagger, and the XIII card, each sealed in a clear bag.

“Try the evidence room this time,” Chen told Ace. “Not his desk.”

Ace looked toward the shrine.

The Satanizer lay on its edge. A crack ran through the brown glass. Then the bottle collapsed inward with a soft sigh, crumbling into gray dust across the seven circles.

The alarm stopped.

Ace listened to his breathing. No hiss. No snort. No chitter. No shriek.

Just breath.

He turned his palm over. No feathers, scales, or fur remained. Only a human hand.

Slick came beside him. “How do you feel?”

“Tired. Bruised. Human.”

“That’ll do.”

Chen, rubbing life back into her wrists, raised an eyebrow. “Don’t get used to it.”

Ace smiled. It was smaller than his old, dazzling grin and lacked its usual demand for admiration.

“No,” he said. “I think I’ll try to keep it.”

## Chapter 10: Clean Hands

By Thursday, the seventh precinct had run out of interview rooms, clean coffee mugs, and people willing to say they had never heard of the Order of the Hand.

A banking chairman occupied the bench usually reserved for drunk tourists. A deputy mayor demanded a private telephone and received a public one with a sticky receiver. Upstairs, a police commissioner’s portrait had been removed from the wall, leaving a pale rectangle that everyone found unexpectedly cheerful.

Ace watched two officers escort a judge past the booking desk.

“Do you know who I am?” the judge demanded.

“Unfortunately,” Chen said, turning a page in her report.

The dismantling was less dramatic than the raid. It involved warrants, duplicated records, and investigators from outside the city arriving with sensible shoes. Chen had insisted on sending copies of the leverage binder to three separate agencies before anyone important could make it disappear.

Ace had once considered paperwork the place where adventure went to die. Now he corrected a date on his statement and kept going.

Across his desk lay the last pieces of Preston Ashworth’s death.

Lionel Voss had surrendered the tower’s maintenance records after discovering that loyalty to rich people did not include legal representation. Ashworth’s study door locked automatically when pulled shut. The supposed internal bolt, visible through the frosted panel, was decorative brass on an antique door.

“Six detectives,” Briggs had said upon learning this. “Not one of you tried the hardware.”

Ace had signed the supplemental report without offering an adjective in his own defense.

The poison had proved to be an old preparation from the Order’s collection, used to weaken victims quickly and give its punishments a supernatural reputation. The dagger had still done the killing. Vale’s scarf, cufflink, confession, and the footage supplied considerably more substance than any curse.

Celeste Ashworth and Julian Cross had been concealing an affair, not a murder. Ace found their embarrassment almost restful.

***

Slick’s exoneration arrived in a padded envelope.

Ashworth’s solicitor brought it personally: payment records, letters, and instructions naming Elroy Mahmoud as the man entrusted to remove the bottle from the Order’s reach.

One letter called him “the only employee in this building who notices what is actually happening.”

Slick read that line twice.

“Could have put it on a reference,” he said.

The letters explained his route through the tower, the bottle beside the dead man when he found him, and his panicked decision to carry it into the precinct rather than trust another wealthy patron. They did not make every choice sensible. They made his innocence clear.

Briggs apologized in his office with the door open.

Slick accepted, then pointed out that the holding-cell sink still leaked.

By Friday morning he was back behind a mop bucket, nudging a wet-floor sign into the path of a councilman’s lawyer.

“You could take a holiday,” Ace said.

“And miss this?”

Slick glanced toward the interview rooms and gave him a quiet wink.

Ace lowered his voice. “The hotel dispenser?”

“Vale’s people managed that gala. She wanted you reaching for the bottle without thinking. Chen found the refill instructions among the catering messages.”

“So I was being cultivated.”

“You were being predictable.”

There was no graceful answer to that. Ace settled for moving his shoes so Slick could mop beneath them.

***

The press conference took place under lights bright enough to interrogate a lighthouse.

Ace stood behind the lectern in a suit that no longer smelled of cocktail sauce. His bruises had faded to the color of elderly bananas. In his pocket, his returned badge felt heavier than before.

Briggs had reinstated him that morning.

“Medical clearance, completed statements, and no more livestock,” he had said, sliding the badge across his desk.

“I’ll do my best.”

“That was not a request for improvisation.”

Now cameras clicked. Ace recognized the familiar pause in which he normally assembled a sentence worth quoting.

He looked instead at Chen, standing beside the steps with a folder beneath her arm. Slick waited near the back, wearing his good work shirt.

“Dr. Ingrid Vale has been charged with Preston Ashworth’s murder,” Ace began. “Further arrests concern conspiracy, coercion, and offenses arising from the Order’s activities. Some people named in its records were victims. Their names will not be offered up for entertainment.”

A reporter raised her hand. “Detective Hartley, is this your biggest case?”

For a moment, he could almost feel feathers gathering between his shoulder blades.

“This case was solved because Detective Mara Chen kept investigating when I became”—he glanced at Briggs—“unreliable.”

Chen’s mouth twitched.

“And because Elroy Mahmoud risked his life to help us, after we had given him very little reason to trust us.”

The cameras swung toward Slick. He looked as though he would rather face armed guards again.

“My colleagues preserved the evidence,” Ace continued. “They protected people. I’m grateful to them.”

No dazzling conclusion arrived. He let the sentence stand.

Afterward, Chen joined him by the water cooler.

“Unreliable?”

“I considered ‘intermittently house-trained.’”

“Save that for your memoir.”

He handed her the revised report. Her name appeared first.

She noticed, but said only, “You missed a signature.”

***

That evening, the precinct resumed its usual noisy argument with the city. Telephones rang. Someone disputed a parking ticket with religious conviction. From the break room came the smell of scorched popcorn.

Briggs paused beside Ace’s desk.

“Where’d the old peacock go?”

Ace looked up. “Washed my hands of him.”

The captain grunted. “Keep the eyesight. Lose the speeches.”

When he had gone, Slick set a narrow paper envelope on Ace’s blotter.

Inside lay a single peacock feather.

“Thought you might want your property back.”

Ace turned it beneath the desk lamp. The blue-green eye caught the light, extravagant and faintly ridiculous. He remembered hiding feathers, inventing excuses, caring desperately who might have seen.

“Thanks,” he said.

Slick nodded and went back to work.

Ace placed the feather in his drawer beside the Ashworth files. Then he reached for the sanitizer Chen had left on his desk.

He stopped.

He read the front, the back, the ingredients, and the manufacturer’s address.

From across the aisle, Chen watched him.

“Satisfied?”

Ace laughed, pressed the pump, and rubbed his hands together.

Beyond the windows, the city glittered. Below that glitter lay leaking pipes, dirty floors, and people who knew which doors really locked.

Tomorrow, he would start there.
