The city precinct never slept; it merely changed noises. Phones shrilled, printers coughed, officers shouted over one another, and somewhere in the evidence room a cart squeaked with the persistence of a guilty conscience. I strode through it all wearing my best trench coat and my most convincing expression of effortless genius.

“Make way,” I announced. “The city’s finest detective is entering the break room.”

Nobody made way. A patrol officer carrying six coffees clipped my shoulder, a clerk wheeled a stack of folders into my knees, and someone’s confiscated parrot shouted, “Liar!” from the reception desk.

I recovered with a graceful spin that I privately classified as tactical. Then I noticed the dispenser beside the break-room sink. Its bottle was black, squat, and crowned with a red cap. The label read **HAND SATANIZER**, in gothic lettering, with the word *Satan* decorated by tiny flames.

I assumed it was an eccentric brand.

“Budget cuts,” I muttered. “Even hygiene has gone underground.”

The department had been told to sanitize before touching the new case files: a string of thefts involving sealed evidence envelopes, each one removed from locked cabinets without a sign of forced entry. I had just dusted my hands with what I believed was ordinary sanitizer when a cold tingle raced up my wrists.

My fingers lengthened.

Not metaphorically. Not in the poetic sense favored by detectives who want their reports to sound impressive. My fingers became long, jointed black claws, each nail gleaming like polished obsidian. I stared at them. The claws stared back, insofar as claws can stare.

I slapped both hands against the sink. “Remain calm, Hand,” I ordered myself. “This is obviously a temporary dermatological misunderstanding.”

A burst of red sparks popped from my knuckles.

My reflection in the steel faucet showed one normal sleeve and, emerging from it, a hand that looked qualified to sign contracts in the underworld. I grabbed a towel. The towel smoked. I dropped it into the sink, where it hissed like an offended cat.

The break-room door swung open.

A janitor stood there, pushing a yellow cart loaded with mops, trash bags, and an alarming number of tiny silver keys. He was a narrow man with a gray mustache, polished shoes, and a serene expression that made it seem his worst prediction had come true.

“Detective Hand,” he said.

I hid my claws behind my back. “You saw nothing.”

“I saw enough to charge admission.”

His name badge read **SATANIZER**. Beneath it, in smaller letters, was *Facilities and Night Operations*. I had questioned him once about the evidence-room keys. He had answered every question with a question, corrected my grammar twice, and somehow left me holding a mop bucket. Naturally, he had been my first suspect.

“You,” I said, pointing at him with a claw. “This is your doing.”

“Technically, it is the bottle’s doing. I only mopped around it.”

“You placed an infernal chemical in a police precinct.”

“I placed a mislabeled bottle in a police precinct. There is a distinction, and a very expensive lawyer would explain it slowly.”

A dispatcher hurried past the open door. I shoved my transformed hand into my coat pocket, but the pocket tore from cuff to hem. The dispatcher glanced over, saw Satanizer, and kept walking. That was the advantage of working in a precinct: nobody investigated a strange noise unless it filed paperwork.

Satanizer rolled his cart inside and locked the door behind him. “How many pumps?”

“One.”

“Then you have perhaps ten minutes before the first change stabilizes.”

“The first change?”

My left hand spasmed. Black fur sprouted over my wrist. I made a noise I would later describe as a tactical cough.

Satanizer opened a drawer in his cart and removed a brass-topped vial. “The bottle was hidden in the old evidence annex. Someone has been using it to alter witnesses, steal objects, and leave impossible fingerprints. I found it behind a false panel while cleaning.”

“You found it and reported it?”

“I reported the false panel. The bottle disappeared before I could report the bottle.”

That was new information, and it landed harder than the transformation. The evidence thefts. The impossible fingerprints. The locked cabinets. I looked at the black liquid clinging to the creases of my hand and remembered the report’s strangest detail: every stolen envelope had been touched by something that left a faint smell of burnt cinnamon.

“Who else knows about it?” I asked.

Satanizer’s eyes flicked toward the ceiling camera. “The person who stole it knows. The person who ordered the thefts may know. And now, unfortunately, you know.”

“Good,” I said, though my voice squeaked as a small horn pushed through my sleeve. “Knowledge is the first step toward arrest.”

“Survival is the first step.”

He uncapped the brass vial and dabbed a clear liquid onto my wrist. The fur vanished. My claws shrank halfway, becoming merely talon-like, which was not the victory I wanted but was an improvement over full demon hand.

I studied him. “You’re helping me.”

“I’m helping myself. If the bottle remains in circulation, every person in this building becomes a possible suspect, victim, or goat.”

“A goat?”

“Wait until the second pump.”

I looked at the black bottle on the sink. Its label had peeled at one corner, revealing writing underneath: a storage number stamped in red—**ANNEX 13**.

I pocketed the bottle with my normal hand, then reconsidered and used a folded evidence bag. “You’re coming with me.”

“As a suspect?”

“As an ally under observation.”

Satanizer smiled and unlocked the door. “That is the nicest promotion I’ve received.”

Together we stepped into the roaring precinct, me concealing one clawed hand beneath my coat and him pushing his suspicious cart toward the evidence annex. Behind us, the bottle gave a soft, bubbling laugh.


The precinct swallowed us the moment we left the break room. Phones rang from every direction, officers hurried beneath buzzing fluorescent lights, and the reception parrot accused three different people of being liars. Detective Hand marched beside my cart with his torn coat flapping and one clawed hand hidden under his sleeve. He moved as though the entire building had been arranged for his dramatic entrance.

“Walk naturally,” I advised.

“I am walking naturally.”

“You are limping in rhythm with the printer.”

“That printer has suspicious timing.”

He stopped beside the printer anyway and leaned toward its paper tray. A sheet emerged bearing a blurry maintenance request. Hand snatched it with his ordinary hand, scanned it, and frowned. The corner of the page carried a reddish smear.

“Burnt cinnamon,” he said.

I took the paper before his claws could puncture it. The smear was faint, but the smell was unmistakable: sweet, scorched, and sharp enough to prickle the back of my throat. Someone had brushed the printer’s service panel recently. The panel was secured with a tiny brass screw, the same kind used on the old evidence cabinets.

“Don’t touch anything else,” I said.

“I am a detective. Touching things is practically a constitutional duty.”

“That explains your current condition.”

He glared at me, though the glare lost force when his little horn bumped the brim of his hat. I pushed my cart onward, steering around a pair of arguing clerks. The evidence annex lay beyond the public records corridor, behind a security door that required both a badge and a rotating access code. I had cleaned that corridor for years. I knew which floor tile rocked, which camera blinked instead of recording, and which security guard accepted pastries in exchange for pretending not to notice unauthorized mops.

Hand knew none of this. He did, however, know how to look offended by it.

At the security door, he produced his badge. The scanner flashed red.

“Try again,” he ordered.

“It recognizes you.”

“Then why is it refusing me?”

“Perhaps it has heard you speak.”

I reached beneath the cart and removed a ring of keys. Hand’s gaze dropped to them immediately. There were eleven keys, though the facilities office had issued me eight. The extra three had come from locks nobody admitted existed.

“You still have those,” he said.

“I never stopped having them.”

“That is not a reassuring answer.”

“It is not intended to be.”

I selected a narrow key and opened the maintenance panel beneath the scanner. Behind it, a bundle of wires had been disturbed. One wire was coated in black residue, glossy as syrup. Hand crouched beside me, his transformed hand hovering inches from the panel.

“Don’t put your fingers in there,” I said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

His claw moved closer.

“I was going to use a pencil.”

“You have no pencil.”

“Then I was going to acquire one.”

I handed him a mop handle. He examined it with the solemnity of a forensic instrument and nudged the residue. The black smear stretched into a thin thread. Beneath it, etched into the metal housing, was a symbol: a crooked circle split by three short lines.

I recognized it from the false panel in the old annex.

Hand noticed my silence. “You’ve seen that mark.”

“I have seen it scratched into a door, beneath a sink, and on the underside of a stolen evidence tray.”

“And you waited until now to mention it?”

“I was waiting for the right detective.”

“You had one standing in front of you.”

“I had one standing in front of me. The distinction matters.”

The security panel clicked. The door unlocked without a beep. I had not entered a code. Hand slowly rose, his eyes fixed on the dark gap beyond the door.

“Someone opened it remotely,” he said.

“Or someone left it ready.”

“Your list of possibilities is irritatingly broad.”

“My list keeps me alive.”

We entered the evidence corridor. The door swung shut behind us, muting the precinct’s clamor to a distant mechanical throb. Rows of sealed cabinets stood beneath red emergency bulbs. Each cabinet bore an inventory number, and every third one had a faint dust line where an envelope had been removed. Hand’s shoulders tightened as he counted them.

“Seven thefts,” he said. “The reports said six.”

“The seventh was never reported.”

“Why?”

“Because the envelope contained a disciplinary file on a lieutenant who retired before anyone noticed it missing. The department prefers mysteries that embarrass other departments.”

He gave me a sideways look. “You know an impressive amount for a janitor.”

“I know an impressive amount because people forget janitors are present.”

At the end of the corridor, a service elevator waited with its doors open. A trail of dark droplets led inside. Hand lifted his sleeve and leaned toward the floor. His horn nearly struck the elevator frame.

“Fresh?” he asked.

“Fresh enough.”

The bottle in its evidence bag began to bubble. The sound echoed in the narrow corridor, soft and wet, like laughter heard through a wall. Hand reached for it, then stopped himself.

That restraint mattered. Until then, I had regarded him as an armed accident wearing a badge. Now I saw someone capable of following instructions when the stakes became personal. Suspicion did not vanish, but it shifted shape. He was no longer merely a danger I had to manage. He was a dangerous ally who might actually listen.

“Annex 13,” he said. “Down.”

I wheeled the cart into the elevator and pressed the unmarked brass button beneath the lowest floor indicator. The doors closed. As the car descended, a second set of claw marks appeared on the inner wall—long, fresh, and still wet with black residue.

Hand stared at them.

I locked the cart’s drawer around the evidence bag.

“Detective,” I said, “whatever is waiting below has already been inside the annex.”

The elevator jolted downward, and somewhere beneath our feet, a lock turned.


The lock turned again beneath us, and the elevator dropped another inch with a stomach-lurching clank. I seized the handrail with my ordinary hand and tried not to look at the fresh claw marks on the wall. They were longer than mine, deeper than mine, and arranged in pairs, as if whatever had made them had been impatient with the elevator’s speed.

“Tell me that is a maintenance feature,” I said.

Satanizer kept one hand on his cart and the other near the ring of suspicious keys at his belt. “It is not a maintenance feature.”

“Excellent. I prefer my threats properly categorized.”

The locked drawer on the cart bubbled. I had secured the bottle inside it, but the sound came through the metal: glug, glug, glug, followed by a tiny laugh. My right hand clenched beneath my sleeve. Black scales had replaced the fur along my knuckles, and a second thumb had begun pushing sideways from my palm.

I stared at it in horror. “No.”

Satanizer glanced over. “What did you touch?”

“Nothing. I merely exist near the bottle.”

“That may be enough now.”

The extra thumb gave an enthusiastic twitch. I slapped it against my coat and nearly lost my balance. “I am Detective Hand,” I announced, mostly to the elevator. “I have solved kidnappings, blackmail rings, and the department’s mysterious shortage of edible break-room pastries. I refuse to be defeated by a bottle with spelling problems.”

“You were defeated by the bottle with spelling problems,” Satanizer said.

“I am currently negotiating a rematch.”

The elevator shuddered, and the overhead light turned red. Through the narrow seam between the doors came a smell of wet stone and burnt cinnamon. I crouched beside the cart and examined the scratches more closely. The lowest mark contained a faint gray smear, unlike the glossy black residue in the security panel. I touched it with the end of Satanizer’s mop handle.

The smear flaked away, revealing a strip of blue paper caught beneath the claw. I pinched it between my normal fingers and held it beneath the emergency light. It was a torn corner from an evidence envelope, stamped with a partial inventory code and the crooked-circle symbol.

“Someone came down here carrying stolen evidence,” I said. “The creature—or person—left the mark while entering the elevator. That means the scratches aren’t random.”

“Nothing in this building is random,” Satanizer replied. “Not even the parrot.”

I turned the paper over. A line of writing had been pressed into its reverse side hard enough to emboss the fibers: **RETURN THE SEVENTH BEFORE THE BOTTLE WAKES**.

My mouth went dry. “You said the seventh theft was a disciplinary file.”

“That is what the inventory ledger says.”

“And what do you think it was?”

“I think someone altered the ledger after removing the envelope.”

“That is a very janitorial answer.”

“It is a very accurate answer.”

The bottle laughed again. This time the sound came from inside the locked drawer. I spun toward the cart. The black bottle had not moved, but a thin red line had appeared beneath its label, glowing like an ember. The letters of *HAND SATANIZER* rearranged themselves for one awful second into *HAND, SATAN’S EYES*, then snapped back.

I reached for the drawer.

Satanizer caught my wrist. “Don’t.”

His grip was firm, not hostile, and he held on until I stopped pulling. I tested him once, then let my arm fall.

“Fine,” I said. “But if it starts singing, I’m throwing it down the shaft.”

“It has already started singing.”

“That was bubbling.”

“That is how it sings.”

The elevator bell rang. The doors opened onto a concrete landing lit by a single swinging bulb. A painted arrow pointed toward **ANNEX 13**, but the arrow had been drawn over an older sign that read **INCINERATOR ACCESS**. Beyond it ran a narrow passage lined with old pipes. Three security seals had been broken, and black footprints crossed the dust toward a steel door.

I stepped out first, because courage was my professional specialty and because Satanizer had put a hand on the cart as though prepared to shove it in front of him. My transformed thumb curled around the evidence envelope fragment. The scales on my wrist began to soften, changing into slick red skin. I felt a horn press against my collar.

“Another change,” I muttered.

Satanizer opened a small vial from his cart. “I can slow it.”

“Can you reverse it?”

“Not without knowing which transformation the bottle is attempting.”

“Then slow it. I need both hands for detective work.”

He dabbed the clear liquid along my wrist. The red skin receded, the horn stopped growing, and the extra thumb folded painfully into the palm. I exhaled through my teeth.

“That helps,” I admitted.

“Do not sound grateful. It encourages me.”

We advanced toward the steel door. At its base lay a neat row of objects: a brass key, a torn evidence envelope, and a precinct coffee mug bearing the initials of a lieutenant who had supposedly retired. The seventh theft had not been a disciplinary file. It had been something—or someone—connected to the annex.

I held up the fragment. “We find the missing envelope, identify who altered the ledger, and locate whatever made these prints.”

“And the bottle?”

I looked toward the locked drawer and heard it bubbling softly behind us.

“We keep it contained,” I said. “Until I learn why it thinks my hands are an ingredient.”

Satanizer selected a key. For the first time, he did not hide it from me.

The steel door clicked open. Inside, something scraped across the floor, and a voice from the darkness whispered my name in my own voice.


The voice whispered Hand’s name again from beyond the steel door, perfectly matching the detective’s rough, offended tone.

“Hand.”

Hand raised his ordinary hand toward the darkness. I caught his sleeve before he crossed the threshold.

“Never answer a voice that knows your name,” I said.

“I wasn’t going to answer.”

“You were lifting your hand.”

“I lift my hand at many things. Criminals. Coffee. Poorly designed signage.”

The door stood open by three inches, revealing a room packed with obsolete incinerator machinery. Pipes crossed the ceiling like rusted ribs. A red emergency lamp swung above a grated floor, turning every shadow into a slow, crawling thing. The voice came again, this time from a brass speaker mounted beside the door.

“Hand.”

Hand looked at the speaker, then at me. “That is either a very elaborate haunting or the precinct’s worst intercom.”

I examined the speaker housing. Its grille had been dusted recently, and a thread of black residue ran from the microphone toward the floor. Beneath it, someone had wedged a folded inventory slip. I pulled it free with the tip of a key. The paper listed three evidence transfers, all stamped with the same time notation: **ANNEX 13—RECEIVED BY HAND**.

Hand’s face tightened. “I never signed for those.”

“No,” I said. “But your name was used.”

“An obvious forgery. My handwriting has more authority.”

“Your handwriting has more exclamation marks.”

He ignored that and crouched beside the threshold. His altered hand hovered above the grate, claws flexing without touching it. Under the red light, his fingers looked almost metallic. I held the clear vial ready in case the scales spread again.

“Don’t apply anything from the black bottle,” I warned.

“I know.”

“You said that before.”

“I am improving.”

A faint hiss rose from the grate. Hand leaned closer. Beneath the metal bars lay a row of transparent plastic bags, each containing an object from the missing evidence inventory: a watch, a silver lighter, a sealed witness statement, and a child’s red marble. The bags were arranged in a perfect circle around a dark stain. On the plastic, fingerprints appeared and vanished in the red light—long spirals, overlapping human whorls, and one unmistakable clawed print.

Hand pointed with the mop handle. “There. Impossible fingerprints.”

“Not impossible,” I said. “Copied.”

He looked at me.

I knelt and inspected the bags without touching them. “A transfer medium. Something soft was pressed against the objects, then peeled away. The fingerprints belonged to several people, but they were all deposited at once. Whoever planted these wanted the evidence to implicate witnesses who never handled it.”

“Altered witnesses, stolen objects, forged signatures.” Hand’s voice sharpened. “The bottle is not transforming me randomly. It is reproducing identities.”

The locked drawer on my cart bubbled. A red glow leaked through the seam.

“Or collecting them,” I said.

The brass speaker crackled. Hand’s voice emerged again, but this time the imitation sounded strained, as if several versions were speaking through one throat.

“Return the seventh.”

Hand took one step toward the machinery. His horn brushed the doorframe, and a flurry of black scales spread across his cheek. He swore, forced open the cart’s locked drawer, seized the bottle, and nearly uncapped it before I slapped his wrist aside.

“What are you doing?” I demanded.

“The transformation is advancing.”

“You cannot use the cause to treat the symptom.”

“I have used worse medical plans.”

He still had the bottle in his hand. The label writhed under his fingers, the letters sliding into **HAND, SATAN’S EYES**. I seized the clear vial, poured a narrow line across his wrist, and watched the scales retreat from his cheek. His claws shortened. The extra thumb folded away with a wet pop.

Hand stared at his restored fingers, then passed the bottle back without argument.

That was the moment our arrangement changed. He no longer treated my warnings as interference, and I no longer treated his recklessness as proof that he was useless. He was still reckless, of course. Trust did not cure a detective of his profession.

A metallic clank sounded inside the room. One of the incinerator’s old access hatches swung open. From the darkness, a narrow maintenance trolley rolled forward by itself and stopped at our feet. On it rested a black ledger, a burnt envelope, and a brass nameplate engraved with the retired lieutenant’s initials.

Hand picked up the nameplate with his ordinary hand. “The seventh theft.”

I opened the ledger. Every missing object had been recorded in a different handwriting, but the final page contained a notation in pencil: **THE BOTTLE WAKES WHEN THE SEVENTH IS REMOVED FROM THE CIRCLE**.

The dark stain beneath the evidence bags began to pulse.

I looked back at the cart. The black bottle had stopped bubbling.

That silence was worse than laughter.

“Put the nameplate down,” I said.

Hand did not move.

From the speaker came one final imitation, softer now and unmistakably mine:

“Cunning janitor.”

The room’s lights went out.


The darkness swallowed the room, the red emergency lamp, and my excellent profile. For three seconds, I heard only the hiss of cooling pipes and the faint metallic tick of the maintenance trolley.

Then something wet clicked beneath the grate.

I drew my revolver with my ordinary hand. My other hand remained clamped around the brass nameplate, its fingers prickling as though the metal had grown teeth. “Satanizer?”

“Still here.”

“Are you holding the bottle?”

“No.”

“Excellent. We have established one fact.”

A match flared near my shoulder. Satanizer had struck it against the sole of his boot, revealing his lined face, his mop, and the little clear vial tucked between two fingers. The flame also revealed the brass speaker. Its grille had opened like a mouth.

“Cunning janitor,” it whispered in Satanizer’s voice.

Satanizer lifted the match toward it. “You flatter me.”

The speaker crackled. “Suspect. Cleaner. Witness. Cooperating investigator.”

“Your categories are untidy,” I said. “I object on professional grounds.”

The flame died, but its brief light had been enough. The dark stain beneath the evidence circle was no longer on the floor. It had spread up the nearest pipe in branching veins, pulsing toward the ceiling. The black bottle sat on the trolley, silent and uncapped, though neither of us had touched it. A bead of liquid trembled at the lip.

My skin tightened along the wrist. Scales began emerging beneath my cuff.

“Oh, no,” I said. “It’s doing the thing.”

“Step away from the bottle.”

“I am stepping away emotionally.”

“Hand.”

“I am also stepping away physically.”

I backed toward the steel door, but my transformed fingers tightened around the brass nameplate. The metal had become hot. In the dark, a line of lettering appeared across its surface, not engraved but glowing through the brass: **ACCESS AUTHORITY—LIEUTENANT VALE**. Beneath it, smaller marks formed a fingerprint.

Not a human fingerprint. The whorls were angular, broken by tiny hooks, and identical to the clawed impression on the evidence bags.

I held the plate close to the match Satanizer relit. “The nameplate wasn’t stolen as evidence. It was used as a key.”

Satanizer looked toward the incinerator hatch. “The lieutenant’s authority opened Annex 13.”

“And someone copied his identity onto the bottle’s collection.”

“Or the bottle copied him.”

The scales reached my elbow. A hot pressure pushed against my knuckles, and two extra joints bulged beneath the skin. I considered grabbing the clear vial myself, then remembered that Satanizer had stopped me from making every medical decision with the black bottle. I held out my ordinary hand.

“Help.”

He passed me the vial without hesitation. His trust was becoming inconveniently useful. I uncapped it, poured a thin line across my wrist, and felt the transformation retreat with a series of tiny pops. The scales melted into skin. The extra joints flattened. My hand returned to its proper number of fingers, which I consider the department standard.

The black bottle shuddered.

Every bag in the evidence circle popped open at once. The watch rolled across the grate. The lighter spun. The sealed witness statement slid toward the stain, dragged by an invisible current. I lunged and caught the statement with my ordinary hand before it vanished between the bars. The paper was cold and damp, and fingerprints bloomed across its surface in rapid succession—first a civilian’s whorl, then a lieutenant’s ridge, then mine.

The final print was Satanizer’s.

He saw it too. His expression tightened, but he did not reach for the statement. “That is not my fingerprint.”

“No,” I said. “It is a copy made from the same medium.”

“Then the bottle has sampled me.”

“Congratulations. You are now evidence.”

The brass speaker emitted a burst of static. A dozen voices poured through it, all recognizable, all overlapping: officers calling for reports, clerks shouting over ringing phones, a dispatcher announcing a suspect at the front desk. The bustling city precinct came rushing into the annex through the speaker, though the sounds were warped and played backward.

Then the speaker used my voice.

“Seventh removed. Identity opened. Witnesses altered.”

I glanced at the ledger. The final page had changed. Beneath the pencil notation, fresh words appeared in wet black ink: **THE HAND WHO SIGNS THE TRANSFER IS THE HAND WHO LEAVES THE ROOM**.

My stomach dropped. “It wants me to sign something.”

Satanizer picked up the burnt envelope with a gloved hand and examined its charred edge. “Not you. Your name.”

He turned the envelope over. Under the soot was a strip of adhesive bearing my precinct identification number. The number had been copied backward, but the final digit was missing.

“The forged transfers,” I said. “They weren’t merely using my name. They were building a replacement identity one stolen object at a time.”

The stain pulsed harder. The incinerator hatch slammed shut. From inside came a scraping sound followed by three deliberate knocks.

I raised my revolver. “We are leaving.”

Satanizer moved to the steel door and checked the corridor through its narrow window. Beyond him, emergency lights had returned, flickering yellow along the concrete passage. Farther away, I could hear the service elevator bell and the confused shouts of precinct personnel responding to the alarm.

He opened the door. “The corridor is clear.”

I tucked the evidence statement into my coat and kept the brass nameplate in my other hand. “We take the ledger, the envelope, and the bottle.”

Satanizer stared at the uncapped black bottle. “You are certain?”

“No. But if it stays here, it can keep collecting identities.”

“And if we carry it upstairs?”

“Then it can collect ours in a more supervised environment.”

The speaker gave a small, mocking cough in my voice.

I stepped through the steel doorway first. My transformation had stopped, but the skin beneath my cuff still felt warm. Satanizer followed with the trolley, one hand gripping the bottle’s drawer and the other holding the clear vial ready.

Behind us, the speaker whispered my name again.

This time, I did not answer.


I pulled the steel door shut behind us, and the three knocks from the incinerator stopped at once. That silence followed us into the Annex 13 corridor like a careful animal. Hand walked ahead with the witness statement tucked inside his coat and Lieutenant Vale’s brass nameplate clenched in his ordinary hand. The other hand remained ordinary for the moment, though he kept flexing it as if expecting claws to spring out and demand a pension.

“Do you hear that?” he asked.

“The alarm?”

“The shouting.”

“That is the precinct.”

Beyond the corridor’s fire door, the city precinct surged around us: telephones ringing, radios squawking, shoes striking tile, and officers arguing over which evacuation route had been printed upside down. We crossed the threshold together, leaving the annex’s red gloom for the hard white lights of the main security passage. I locked the fire door with the manual bar and wedged my mop across it.

Hand glanced back. “Will that hold?”

“No.”

“Your confidence is refreshing.”

“It will hold long enough for someone else to blame the damage on me.”

He almost smiled, but his expression changed when the black bottle rattled inside the locked drawer of my trolley. The sound was faint, like a fingernail tapping glass from underwater. I stopped beside a vending machine while a pair of uniformed officers hurried past us carrying boxes of files. Their eyes slid over the trolley, the mop, and me without stopping. In this building, a janitor pushing anything anywhere was considered part of the architecture.

Hand crouched beside the drawer. “It’s reacting to the nameplate.”

“Everything reacts to that nameplate. It has more authority than the commissioner.”

He held the brass plate above the drawer without touching it. The bottle rattled harder. A thin black line appeared beneath the metal, tracing the missing final digit on the back of the plate. Hand drew in a breath. His fingertips lengthened.

I caught his wrist. “Do not apply the bottle.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were looking at it with the expression of a man about to make a terrible decision.”

“That is my investigative expression.”

“It is your every expression.”

The skin along his wrist roughened into gray scales. He tried to pull free, but I tightened my grip and uncapped the clear vial with my teeth. A small crowd had begun forming at the far end of the passage, attracted by the alarm and by Hand’s increasingly nonstandard anatomy. I poured the stabilizing liquid across his wrist. The scales withdrew, leaving a faint silver sheen behind. His fingers shortened with three dry clicks.

Hand stared at the restored hand, then looked at me. “You’re getting very good at that.”

“I have practice.”

“With me?”

“With disasters that refuse to stand still.”

His gaze softened, and he handed me the nameplate. “Then keep hold of this. I trust you more than I trust my own hands right now.”

The words landed more heavily than the trolley. I slipped the plate into the inner pocket of my work coat, beside my keys and a packet of lemon sweets. “That is a poor choice. I have been a suspect in this investigation since the first mop bucket went missing.”

“You’re still a suspect,” Hand said. “You’re simply a suspect I’m willing to stand behind.”

A service bell chimed near the records junction. The elevator doors opened, revealing a wall of filing cabinets and a reflection that did not belong to either of us. In the polished metal, Hand appeared crowned with black horns, while I appeared to have a second face peering over my shoulder. I spun around. No one stood there. When I looked back, the reflection had corrected itself, except the second face was now smiling.

“Don’t look at it,” I said.

“I already did.”

“Then stop.”

“You say that as if I have command over mirrors.”

I seized the trolley and pushed it toward the records junction. I needed somewhere with cameras, locks, and witnesses before the bottle decided to borrow another identity. Hand kept pace beside me, one hand guarding his coat and the other hovering near my clear vial. The bustling corridor swallowed us: clerks emerged from stairwells, detectives shouted questions, and a printer began spitting out pages nobody had requested.

At the records counter, I stopped before the night supervisor’s empty chair and pulled the emergency chain beneath the desk. Steel shutters dropped over the windows with a clatter, sealing off the junction from the main passage. Hand placed the ledger on the counter and opened it to the final page.

The wet ink had changed again.

**TRANSFER REQUIRES TWO HANDS.**

Beneath the sentence, two blank signature lines appeared. One was labeled **AUTHORITY**. The other was labeled **CLEANING WITNESS**.

Hand looked from the page to me. “It wants us to sign.”

“No,” I said, taking the brass nameplate from my pocket. “It wants to know whether we will.”

The black bottle thumped inside its drawer.

I drew the curtains over the records window, then set the clear vial between us like a tiny glass barricade. “We do not sign. We examine the transfer, identify the copying medium, and find out who has been using Vale’s authority.”

Hand nodded. His shoulder brushed mine, deliberate and steady. “Together?”

“Until you try to drink the evidence.”

“I have never drunk evidence.”

“You licked a cursed fingerprint.”

“That was one time.”

The bottle thumped again. From behind the steel shutters came a distorted chorus of precinct voices, repeating our names. I turned the key in the drawer and pushed the trolley beneath the counter.

“Then let’s give it something it cannot copy,” I said. “A decision made by two people who know exactly what it is trying to steal.”


I examined the two signature lines without touching either. Beyond the shutters, someone demanded a stapler with the desperation usually reserved for hostage negotiations. Radios crackled. A telephone rang steadily enough to suggest it had developed a grievance. The precinct was still functioning, which meant we had perhaps five minutes before somebody needed this counter.

“Something it cannot copy,” I repeated. “Excellent. I have several original deductions.”

“Let us try evidence first,” Satanizer said.

I took that criticism with the grace for which I was famous and searched the counter for a magnifying glass.

There wasn’t one. There was a ruler, a biscuit fossilized inside its wrapper, and a desk lamp attached to the counter by a chain. I swung the lamp over the ledger. The signature lines shone black, but the words above them had a different finish: dull, granular, almost dusty.

“Hold the page up,” I said.

“With what?”

“Professional skepticism. Or the ruler.”

Satanizer slid the ruler beneath the page. Light passed through the paper, revealing an uneven rectangle between this sheet and the next.

A copying sheet.

I had expected ancient hide, possibly something that screamed. Instead, Satanizer lifted a corner with his tweezers and exposed carbon paper.

“The machinery of damnation,” I said, “is distressingly economical.”

“Don’t touch the shiny side.”

Naturally, I had already touched the shiny side.

A black smudge clung to my thumb. I reached automatically for a tissue, stopped, and recognized the smell: burnt cloves beneath cheap lemon fragrance. The same smell I had dismissed when I first grabbed a bottle labeled **Hand Satanizer** instead of hand sanitizer.

It hadn’t been a printing error. My continued survival depended on remembering that.

I rubbed my thumb against my forefinger before I could stop myself.

Both fingers fused into a glossy black pincer.

For a moment I watched it open and close.

Then I made a small, dignified noise.

Satanizer caught my wrist before I could shake the pincer into the ledger. “Keep still.”

“I am remarkably still for a man developing seafood.”

He applied a drop from the clear vial. The pincer separated with a sensation like pulling apart warm taffy. I counted my fingers twice, then tucked that hand beneath my arm.

“The carbon is coated with the bottle’s contents,” I said. “That’s the medium. Skin contact, pressure, then a copy.”

Satanizer held the sheet by its dry margin. Across its surface lay overlapping impressions: fingerprints, fragments of signatures, the hooked ridges we had seen on the evidence bags.

“And the stolen envelopes?” he asked.

“Carried the impressions from evidence to transfer forms. No elaborate break-in required after the first theft. Just paperwork traveling through the building.”

He nodded toward a cabinet beyond the counter. “Then we need the transfer originals.”

The junction’s shutters remained down, but a narrow staff door led into the adjoining records workroom. Satanizer unlocked it, checked inside, and pushed the trolley through. I followed with the ledger balanced on a clean tray, leaving the sealed junction behind us.

The workroom smelled of hot toner and damp coats. Through a wired-glass panel, clerks hurried along the neighboring passage carrying folders. One banged on the glass; I pointed at my tray and mouthed, “Evidence.” She pointed at her folders and mouthed something less respectful.

Satanizer opened a drawer marked TRANSFER CONTROLS without consulting its label.

“You know precisely where everything is,” I observed.

“I clean beneath it.”

“You also sealed the junction, carry an antidote, and have keys to rooms the captain thinks are decorative.”

He paused with his hand on the drawer.

I set the tray down. “Those are observations, not an arrest. Keep helping me.”

He handed me the control register, then moved the bottle’s locked drawer farther from my elbow. It was a considerate act, although the distance suggested he had revised his estimate of my competence.

The register contained the answer to the missing digit.

Each authorization number occupied seven boxes. On the legitimate forms, the seventh held a small rotating control numeral, entered separately by the approving officer. On the forged transfers, six boxes had been impressed through carbon. The seventh was blank.

“It can copy what it collects,” I said. “It can’t supply what wasn’t there. Vale’s nameplate gave it his name and rank, not his current authorization code.”

“And your incomplete number?”

“Same shortcut. It’s been assembling credentials from surfaces instead of people.”

I laid the ledger’s altered transfer page over a clean control form. The two signature lines aligned exactly with the boxes marked RELEASE AUTHORIZATION and DESTRUCTION WITNESS.

Satanizer leaned closer. “Cleaning witness.”

“An alteration. It wants your hand in the place that certifies disposal.”

“Disposal of what?”

I looked at the distorted reflection in the cabinet glass. My ordinary face stared back; beside it, a second smile hovered near Satanizer’s shoulder.

“The originals,” I said.

I covered the glass with a folder before either of us could examine it further.

Then I placed the carbon sheet between two clean evidence boards. Satanizer secured the edges while I photographed the impressions.

“Neither of us signs anything,” I said.

“Even your report?”

“Especially my report.”

From the trolley came a sharp thump.

I turned the register toward the workroom light. Beneath the forged transfer number, an embossed routing code had survived the carbon’s black coating.

Not Annex 13.

The lieutenant’s office.


The embossed routing code pointed toward Lieutenant Vale’s office, which was inconvenient for several reasons, the least serious being that the lieutenant’s office sat across the busiest corridor in the precinct. The serious reasons included the fact that Vale was absent, his authority had been copied, and the black bottle had begun thumping inside my locked trolley drawer with the enthusiasm of a prisoner hearing dinner arrive.

Hand studied the code through the magnifying lens of the desk lamp. “It’s not a destination.”

“No?”

“It’s a shelf classification. Vale’s private transfer originals were never sent to Annex 13. They were filed in his office under restricted administrative material.”

“You sound disappointed.”

“I was hoping for a trapdoor.”

“You have had an unusually generous number of trapdoors.”

He glanced at the trolley. “And a surprisingly uncooperative janitor.”

I slipped the carbon sheet into a fresh evidence sleeve and sealed it with a strip of clean tape. The black bottle thumped again. This time, the drawer lock clicked from the inside.

Hand’s expression sharpened. “It knows where we’re going.”

“Then we should avoid telling it.”

“I’m telling you.”

“That is different. You are not a bottle.”

“Give it time.”

I pushed the trolley toward the staff door. Hand gathered the ledger, the control register, and the photographed forms, keeping each item on the clean tray. He did not touch the contaminated sheet. He did not reach for the bottle. His restraint was becoming almost suspiciously competent, and I found myself revising my estimate of him in the direction of useful rather than merely loud.

We left the records workroom through the narrow staff door, and I pulled it shut behind us before rolling the trolley into the adjoining records passage. That passage opened directly into the main precinct corridor, providing the necessary evidence that we had moved from the workroom toward Lieutenant Vale’s office: a strip of yellow floor paint marked **ADMINISTRATION**, a bank of security cameras, and three clerks nearly colliding with us while carrying folders. The precinct roared around our progress. Phones rang. A radio announced a stolen scooter, then apologized for announcing it on the wrong channel. Somewhere nearby, a printer began producing blank pages with bureaucratic confidence.

A uniformed officer pointed at my trolley. “Is that evidence?”

“No,” I said. “It’s an aggressively mobile cleaning supply.”

Hand lifted the tray. “It is evidence.”

The officer immediately lost interest. In a city precinct, evidence was important, but anything labeled cleaning supply was expected to survive explosions.

Halfway across the corridor, the trolley shuddered. The drawer sprang open by an inch, and a thread of black vapor curled through the gap. Hand stopped so abruptly that the ledger slid forward. I caught it against my chest.

“Do not open that,” he said.

“I was not planning to.”

“You are holding the key.”

“I am always holding a key. I am a janitor.”

The vapor formed a thin, wavering outline above the drawer: a hand, then a claw, then a gloved finger pointing toward the lieutenant’s office. Hand leaned closer before I seized his sleeve.

“Eyes forward.”

“I can see the direction without looking at it.”

“That is how directions become invitations.”

He nodded and obeyed without argument. The tension in his shoulders eased by a fraction, and he kept his gaze on the administration checkpoint. The trust between us had changed shape. It was no longer the temporary convenience of two people trapped beside dangerous paperwork. Hand was learning to let me contain the bottle, and I was learning that he could resist its bait when the investigation mattered more than his pride. It was an alarming development. Mutual competence was the first step toward friendship, and friendship was notoriously difficult to disinfect.

At the administration checkpoint, a brass plaque announced that Lieutenant Vale’s office was temporarily restricted. The door itself stood open three inches. A red security light blinked above the frame. Hand raised the tray toward the camera.

“Restricted access,” he murmured. “We need authorization.”

“We have a routing code.”

“That is not authorization.”

“No,” I said. “It is evidence that someone already used authorization.”

I removed my ring of keys, selected the small square one, and held it up without inserting it. “The office lock was replaced after the envelope thefts. But the filing cabinet inside still uses the old cylinder. If the originals were hidden there, whoever copied them needed access before the replacement.”

Hand looked at the half-open door. “And after.”

A mop bucket rolled out of the office and stopped against my boot.

I stared at it.

Hand stared at me. “You brought that?”

“It did not come from my trolley.”

The bucket’s water was perfectly still despite the corridor traffic. Beneath the cloudy surface lay a brass key stamped with Vale’s initials.

I crouched, lifted the key with clean forceps, and examined the ring. A smear of black residue marked its teeth, but the handle was dry. The bottle had not merely pointed us toward the office. Someone had planted a key where I would find it.

“Still think I’m the suspect?” I asked.

Hand’s mouth tightened. “Yes.”

“Good.”

“But I think someone is trying to use your reputation as cover.”

That was not the answer I expected. I unlocked the office door, then placed myself between Hand and the threshold.

“Stay behind me,” I said.

He adjusted the tray in his arms. “You’re protecting me?”

“I am protecting the evidence from your hands.”

“Of course.”

“And possibly you.”

His eyebrows rose.

“Do not make me repeat it. The corridor has enough witnesses.”

Together we crossed into Lieutenant Vale’s office. I kept one hand on the door and the other near the stabilizing vial while Hand followed with the ledger and control register. Behind us, the bustling precinct swallowed the sound of the latch clicking shut. Inside, the office smelled of dust, cold coffee, and burnt cloves.

On the desk lay three empty evidence envelopes, each bearing a copied impression.

The filing cabinet stood open.

And from the lowest drawer came the unmistakable sound of someone breathing.


The breathing from the lowest drawer continued: inhale, pause, exhale, followed by a faint wet whistle. I raised the ledger like a shield.

“If that is a person,” I whispered, “I want it noted that I was brave until the breathing began.”

Satanizer stepped between me and the cabinet. “It may be a person.”

“That is not the reassuring alternative.”

He studied the drawer without touching it. The office hummed around us: fluorescent lights buzzing overhead, phones ringing beyond the closed door, footsteps passing in the corridor. Through the blinds, I could see the precinct moving at its usual impossible speed. Someone shouted for a translator. Someone else shouted that the translator was in the evidence room. The drawer breathed again.

Satanizer crouched and pointed to the floor. A thin black thread ran from beneath the cabinet toward the wall vent.

“Not a person,” he said.

“You sound certain.”

“I am certain it is a person-shaped problem.”

He took a long-handled dustpan from the trolley, slid its edge beneath the drawer, and pulled. The drawer opened two inches. A rubber bladder inside expanded with a theatrical wheeze. A second thread tightened, tugging a little brass valve mounted behind the cabinet.

I stared at it. “The drawer is breathing.”

“Someone made it breathe.”

“Which is substantially worse.”

Satanizer used the dustpan to pull the drawer wider. Inside lay a folded maintenance apron, a coil of black tubing, and a small hand pump operated by a spring. The apparatus exhaled once more, then collapsed with a sad squeak.

I leaned over the drawer. “That is the least frightening corpse I have ever encountered.”

“It was meant to keep investigators from opening the drawer.”

“By pretending to be a trapped employee?”

“People hesitate before disturbing a trapped employee. They do not hesitate before disturbing paperwork.”

I glanced at him. “You know this because—”

“Because I have cleaned around three false walls, two concealed safes, and one angry raccoon.”

The janitor’s reputation as a suspiciously well-informed suspect had just acquired a fourth category: useful. I trusted him enough to let him open the drawer, which was an alarming development for both of us.

Beneath the breathing apparatus was a narrow metal case. Satanizer lifted it with forceps and placed it on the desk. Its lid bore three copied impressions, each one smeared at the edges like a fingerprint pressed into tar. I kept my hands tucked beneath my elbows.

“Do not touch those,” Satanizer said.

“I was merely admiring them.”

“You admire dangerous objects with your fingers.”

“That was one time.”

“That was this morning.”

He opened the case with a flat key from his ring. Inside were six original transfer forms, a packet of blank authorization cards, and a strip of contaminated carbon paper folded around a small brass stencil. The stencil bore the number seven and a jagged mark resembling a horn.

My pulse jumped. “The missing control numeral.”

“And the source of the copied impressions,” Satanizer said. “The cards were never meant to authorize transfers. They were templates.”

I picked up the magnifying glass from Vale’s desk and examined the forms without touching them. Each original carried a legitimate signature, a complete seven-digit authorization code, and a faint gray line running beneath the final box.

“The carbon copied the first six digits,” I said. “The stencil supplied the seventh.”

Satanizer nodded. “But only after someone pressed the form against it.”

“Which means the forger needed both the envelopes and the originals.”

“And access to this office.”

I looked toward the door. On its inner side, below the handle, a fresh smear of black residue curved like a thumbprint. The culprit had not merely entered Vale’s office. They had used the door after planting the key, after setting the drawer trap, and likely after arranging the envelopes on the desk.

A soft click came from the intercom.

I flinched so hard my elbow struck the chair. “Did you hear that?”

“Yes.”

The speaker crackled. A recorded voice emerged, distorted by static but still recognizable as Lieutenant Vale’s.

“Cleaning witness required for destruction.”

The message repeated.

Satanizer crossed the room and unplugged the intercom. “A loop.”

“Made from Vale’s own office recording?”

“Probably. The copied voice, copied credentials, copied impressions.” He looked at the bottle locked inside the trolley. “Everything here is an imitation except the evidence.”

The bottle thumped once.

I took a careful step backward. “It disagrees.”

“No. It is reacting.”

“To what?”

Satanizer pointed at the stencil. Its jagged mark had begun to glisten. A bead of black liquid formed along one edge and rolled toward my hand.

I reached for a clean evidence cloth, but the bead leapt first. It struck my palm.

My fingers stretched into long, glossy talons.

I made a noise that began as a gasp and ended as an accusation. “I did not apply anything!”

Satanizer seized my wrist and wrapped the hand in the cloth. “The stencil is saturated. Skin contact is enough.”

“My hand is becoming a legal threat!”

“Hold still.”

He uncapped the clear vial. I trusted him now—not abstractly, not because the circumstances encouraged teamwork, but because he had repeatedly caught me before I became furniture. I held still while he spread the antidote across my palm.

The talons softened, shortened, and reluctantly became fingers again.

I flexed them. All present.

Satanizer sealed the stencil inside a glass evidence tube and locked it beside the carbon sheet. Then he gathered the original forms into the clean tray.

“We have the transfer originals,” he said. “We know the stencil completed the forged credentials. And we know someone staged this office to make me look like the only person capable of entering it.”

“You are still the only person who knew where the hidden case was.”

“Yes.”

“Then you remain a suspect.”

“Good.”

I looked at the evidence, then at the janitor guarding it with one hand on the trolley and the other near his stabilizing vial.

“But you are also our ally.”

His expression barely changed. “Do not say that where the mop buckets can hear.”

Outside the office, a key scraped against the lock.

We both turned toward the door.


The key scraped against the lock again, followed by a muttered curse and the unmistakable rattle of someone trying the handle with too much authority. I shoved the trolley between the door and the desk. Hand raised the evidence tray as though six transfer forms could stop a bullet.

“Is that Lieutenant Vale?” he whispered.

“Possibly.”

“Your confidence is not calming.”

“I am a janitor. Calming people is not in my job description.”

The lock turned. I caught the door before it opened more than an inch and looked through the gap. Lieutenant Vale stood in the corridor, one hand on the borrowed key, his uniform jacket crooked and his expression furious. Behind him, the precinct surged and clattered: phones rang, officers hurried past, and a delivery cart squealed around the corner. Vale’s eyes went first to me, then to the trolley, then to Hand’s transformed-but-now-normal fingers.

“Open this door,” he ordered.

“State your business,” I said.

“I am the occupant of this office.”

“That is a claim, not a business.”

Hand leaned toward me. “You are interrogating a lieutenant through his own door.”

“It is a restricted office. Procedures matter.”

Vale slapped his identification against the narrow opening. The photograph, however, had been cut from a photocopy and pasted beneath a fresh plastic laminate. I noticed because the lower corner had lifted. His real badge hung from his belt, and its serial number did not match the number printed on the card in his hand.

Hand noticed too. His eyebrows climbed.

Vale saw us looking and snatched the card back. “Someone has tampered with my credentials.”

“That makes three of us,” Hand said.

Vale’s gaze hardened. “Detective, step away from the evidence.”

Hand did not move. “Which evidence?”

“The materials in that case.”

“Interesting,” I said. “You know about the case.”

“I know what belongs in my office.”

“The case was hidden beneath your lowest drawer,” I said. “The drawer was fitted with a breathing device and a spring pump. Someone expected investigators to be frightened away.”

Vale’s mouth tightened. “You planted it.”

“If I had planted it, I would not have left a maintenance apron with my laundry mark inside.”

That stopped him for half a second. It was enough. I used the dustpan handle to pull the door wider and pointed toward the floor. A faint trail of gray grit led from the corridor’s administration checkpoint to the threshold. The grit was common in the service elevator shaft, not in the polished office passage. Whoever had staged the drawer had entered through the staff route.

Vale glanced down, then back at me. “You were in the service corridor.”

“I work in the building. It would be suspicious if I never used one.”

“You are the only person with access to the maintenance keys.”

“Not the only person who can duplicate them.”

Hand shifted the tray closer to his chest. “The forged authorizations used the first six digits from the carbon sheet and the seventh from the stencil. Someone needed access to the originals, the empty envelopes, and the restricted office. Satanizer had access, but the black residue on the inner door and the copied credentials suggest the room was staged to implicate him.”

Vale looked at him sharply. “You have been contaminating evidence.”

“I have been avoiding contamination. There is a difference.”

“Not to a report.”

“Then write a better report.”

I almost smiled. Hand’s confidence had returned, but it no longer charged ahead blindly. It had acquired brakes, which made it considerably more dangerous and slightly less exhausting. He met my eyes over the tray.

“We need to get these forms somewhere secure,” he said.

“Evidence Annex 13,” I replied. “The service elevator reaches it without crossing the public corridor.”

Vale stepped forward. I blocked the gap with my shoulder.

“Move,” he said.

“No.”

His hand dropped toward his belt. I lowered the dustpan handle until its metal edge rested against the doorframe.

“Lieutenant,” I said, “if that is your real badge, you can explain why your office was staged. If it is not, you can explain why you are carrying a counterfeit. Either way, you are not entering while the originals are exposed.”

A burst of static crackled from the dead intercom behind me. Vale’s expression changed—not fear exactly, but recognition. He looked past my shoulder toward the sealed evidence tube containing the horn-marked stencil.

Hand saw the change. “You know what that mark means.”

“I know nothing about it.”

“Your face disagrees,” I said.

For the first time, Vale looked less like an angry lieutenant and more like a man who had arrived at the wrong door. He glanced down the corridor. Two uniformed officers passed without slowing. The precinct’s noise swallowed the tension between us.

Then a black smear appeared on the counterfeit identification card. It spread beneath the laminate in the shape of a crooked seventh digit.

Vale dropped the card.

Hand made a strangled sound. “That is not normal.”

“Nothing in this office is normal.”

The black mark crawled from the fallen card toward the threshold. The locked trolley shuddered in answer. I snapped the stabilizing clasp over the bottle and grabbed the clean tray.

“Hand, service door.”

He moved immediately. We passed through the narrow door behind the filing cabinet, and I pulled it shut as Vale shouted after us. The passage beyond smelled of bleach, steam, and old paper. A red arrow painted on the wall pointed toward the service elevator, providing clear evidence of our transition out of Lieutenant Vale’s office and into the maintenance corridor.

Hand hurried beside me, clutching the originals. “You realize this makes you look more guilty.”

“I was already a suspect.”

“You are remarkably cheerful about that.”

“I have a plan.”

“Is it a good plan?”

“No. It is a janitor’s plan.”

The elevator doors groaned open. We stepped inside, and I pressed the button marked ANNEX 13. As the doors closed, the trolley gave one final thump. Hand looked at me, his suspicion still evident, but he was no longer carrying it alone.

“I still think you might be involved,” he said.

“I still think you touch evidence when frightened.”

“Fair.”

The elevator began descending beneath the bustling precinct. Between us, the sealed stencil, the original forms, and the restless black bottle traveled toward the place where its storage history had been hidden. Hand stood close enough that his shoulder brushed mine when the elevator jolted. Neither of us stepped away.
