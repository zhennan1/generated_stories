## Chapter 1: The Lament Archive

The archive lights had not gone dark in eighty-three years.

Elian Voss knew this because they had cataloged the maintenance records. Three hundred and sixty replacement panels, seventeen circuit failures, no interval of complete darkness. Even during repairs, auxiliary strips preserved the white illumination required for objective study.

At their worktable, Elian turned a small ceramic vessel beneath a scanner.

“Object forty-seven. Domestic container. Surface pigment: blue. Structural damage consistent with impact.”

A crack divided the painted bird on its side. Someone had repaired it with a yellow substance that shone beneath the light.

“Repair material,” Elian continued. “Probable adhesive.”

The catalog offered a standard interpretation: resource scarcity.

Elian accepted it and moved the vessel to the completed tray.

At the next table, Historian Thorne removed a photograph from its protective sleeve. Five people stood in water up to their knees. Their mouths were open.

“Collective distress?” Thorne asked.

Elian examined the image without touching it.

“No visible threat. Possibly ritualized muscular expression.”

Thorne entered the classification.

At fourteen hundred, a notification appeared on Elian’s console. Excavation personnel had breached the last layer of a sealed chamber beneath the eastern stacks. A senior historian was required to assess its contents before sterilization.

Elian acknowledged the assignment. Their pulse remained at sixty-two.

***

The chamber lay below the archive’s original foundations, down a temporary stair that rang beneath Elian’s boots. Construction lamps exposed brickwork behind the white reinforcement walls. The bricks were uneven. A human thumbprint marked one, preserved from its making.

An excavation technician waited beside an opening.

“No airborne contaminants,” she said. “No active power source detected. One object.”

“Why was the chamber sealed?”

“Unknown.”

She surrendered the survey tablet and withdrew.

Inside, a narrow console stood beneath a collapsed ventilation duct. There were no chairs, storage racks, or human remains. On the console rested a black rectangular case, small enough to hold in one hand.

Elian photographed it in place.

The lid bore an inscription beneath a film of dust.

LAMENT ARCHIVE.

Underneath, in smaller letters:

For whoever still needs us.

Elian read the phrase twice. The pronoun was imprecise. A future recipient could not meaningfully need persons who no longer existed.

They set the survey tablet down and opened the case.

Within lay a translucent wafer, threaded with fine amber lines. Its edges were smooth, without the corrosion typical of pre-Collapse circuitry. As Elian lifted it, the lines brightened.

They stopped moving.

“Contact-activated,” they dictated.

A woman breathed beside their ear.

Elian turned. The chamber was empty.

Then the console illuminated.

The woman appeared above it, rendered in wavering light. Her hair was gray at the temples. She sat on a floor with her back against a cupboard, holding a small red shoe.

For several seconds she said nothing.

Elian searched the console for playback controls.

“I keep thinking,” the woman said, “that if I put it where it belongs, he’ll need it.”

Her voice failed on the last word.

Elian knew the diagnostic signs: disrupted respiration, facial contraction, repetitive attachment to an absent subject. Bereavement fixation. Before stabilization, such episodes had consumed entire populations.

The woman pressed the shoe against her mouth.

Something tightened beneath Elian’s ribs.

They adjusted their stance. The sensation remained.

The image changed.

A man stood in a doorway while rain struck the pavement behind him. Someone outside the frame cried out, and he began to laugh. He covered his face. Two small figures collided with his legs; he dropped to his knees to gather them against him.

Elian’s own mouth moved.

They clamped it shut.

Another recording: a young woman staring across a stretch of dark water.

“I don’t know whether you’ll come,” she said. “I brought enough for two anyway.”

Beside her sat a parcel wrapped in cloth.

There was so little happening. No threat, no instruction, no useful exchange. Yet Elian could not look away from the place she had left empty beside her.

A pressure gathered behind their eyes.

They reached for the wafer, intending to return it to its case.

The projection widened.

A city rose around the woman: terraces, suspended walkways, towers veined with amber light. Across the water, a bridge brightened as a crowd approached. Elian glimpsed a technical overlay—RESPONSIVE GRID, AFFECTIVE INPUT—before it dissolved beneath another voice.

Their training supplied an explanation. Decorative terminology. Ritual superstition. The pre-Collapse tendency to mistake subjective experience for material force.

But the bridge had brightened in time with the voices.

Elian leaned closer.

A drop struck the console.

For a moment they studied it as an external event: a small clear bead, distorting the projected light.

Then they felt the wet track down their cheek.

Their first tear in ten years.

They wiped it away so hard their skin hurt.

“No,” they said.

The sound was startling in the chamber.

They had been stabilized. Tested. Certified. They had worked beside contaminated objects for years without an incident.

The woman with the shoe returned.

Elian wanted to make her stop. They wanted, with equal violence, to take the shoe from her and sit beside her on the floor.

They could not account for either impulse.

Above the doorway, an environmental scanner clicked.

Elian pulled the wafer free. The chamber went silent.

Procedure required them to seal the object, report cognitive disturbance, and remain in place until a correction team arrived.

They opened the report field on the survey tablet.

Their finger hovered over REQUEST ASSISTANCE.

If the recordings were merely symptoms, why did destroying them suddenly seem intolerable?

Elian closed the field. They wrapped the wafer in a clean specimen cloth and slipped it beneath the tablet’s detachable backing. The empty case went into a contamination pouch.

They wiped the console twice.

At the doorway, the scanner’s indicator changed from green to amber.

Elian stood very still.

A line appeared on the survey tablet.

BIOLOGICAL ANOMALY REGISTERED. REVIEW PENDING.

Behind the tablet’s backing, hidden against their palm, the wafer was warm.

## Chapter 2: Contagion

The amber warning followed Elian upstairs.

It traveled as a reflection on the tablet, a pulse beneath their eyelids, a sentence they could still read after the screen had gone dark.

Biological anomaly registered.

At the cleansing station, they held their hands beneath the jets until the skin whitened. Their pulse beat visibly in one wrist. They turned it over.

*The body produces error. The mind provides correction.*

First principle of the Doctrine of Sanity.

They dried their hands.

*Attachment distorts assessment. Distortion precedes disorder.*

Second principle.

Under the tablet’s backing, the wafer pressed against their palm through a thickness of polymer. They could leave it here. Set it beside the basin, notify Preservation, accept whatever treatment followed.

The woman holding the red shoe returned to them.

Elian closed their fingers around the tablet.

***

Their workstation faced an unbroken white wall. Ordinarily this arrangement promoted concentration. Today the wall seemed intolerably near.

They docked the tablet and opened the chamber’s survey record. There were images, measurements, atmospheric readings. The console had also transferred a small packet of recovered text before Elian disconnected it.

They checked the aisle.

Then they opened the packet.

Most of it was corrupted. Between broken characters, a heading remained legible:

**MUNICIPAL AFFECTIVE CONVERSION: OPERATING ABSTRACT**

Elian read twice, waiting for the words to resolve into something less absurd.

The next fragment described patterned neural activity interacting with a responsive crystalline medium. Emotion did not create energy from nothing; it regulated access to a stored potential the surviving text called the reservoir. Collective feeling shaped the field. The grid converted its fluctuations into usable current.

There were output figures.

Transit demand. Water purification. Winter heating.

Not a metaphor. Not the devotional extravagance their instructors had attributed to pre-Collapse engineering.

A maintenance note reported that a district’s generating capacity had fallen after the closure of its communal music halls.

Elian pictured the bridge brightening beneath approaching voices.

Their heartbeat accelerated.

“Your respiration is elevated.”

Thorne stood at the end of the desk.

Elian closed the packet with such force that their fingertip struck the glass.

“Excavation exposure,” they said.

Thorne’s gaze moved from their hand to the tablet.

“I saw the review notice.”

“Then you know it is pending.”

“I know anomalies are not usually referred directly to an inquisitor.”

The air seemed to withdraw from the room.

Thorne adjusted the stack of accession slips they carried. “Shall I take over your chamber inventory?”

It was a reasonable offer. It would also give Thorne access to every discrepancy.

“The assessment is incomplete.”

“Precisely.”

Elian said nothing.

After a moment, Thorne placed the slips on the desk. “You have been looking at a blank screen for eleven seconds.”

They walked away before Elian could decide whether that was concern or evidence.

***

Inquisitor Kaelen Serev arrived without an escort.

Elian recognized their footsteps before they looked up: measured, with a slight delay before the left heel settled. During Elian’s training, those steps had crossed lecture rooms while students struggled to identify the emotional contamination in historical accounts.

Serev had once returned an essay with a single sentence marked.

*You have mistaken suffering for significance.*

Elian had kept the corrected paper for years.

“Historian Voss.”

“Inquisitor.”

“No need to stand.”

Elian had already risen. They sat again, painfully conscious of the wasted movement.

Serev drew the visitor’s chair close enough that their knees nearly touched. Their gray coat bore no ornament except the narrow white bar of Correction.

“A lacrimal event,” they said.

“The scanner registered moisture.”

“On an interior console in a sealed chamber.”

“Yes.”

Serev studied them. Elian remembered that attention from their apprenticeship: the disconcerting sense that Serev could locate an error before Elian knew they had made one.

“What did you encounter?”

“Funerary material. Household objects. A storage case.”

“Recordings?”

The hidden wafer seemed suddenly hot beneath the docked tablet.

This was the moment to give it up. Elian could almost feel the relief: no more watching doorways, no more pulse pounding against their collar. Serev would know what to do with them.

The woman had held the shoe carefully, as though there were still a foot inside it.

“No,” Elian said.

The word entered the space between them.

They had omitted facts before. They had hidden the wafer. But this was a direct question, answered with something they knew was false.

Shame rose so sharply they expected Serev to smell it.

Nothing happened.

The lights remained steady. Somewhere behind the wall, a sorting mechanism clicked into its next cycle.

“Look at me,” Serev said.

Elian did.

“Your eyes are inflamed.”

“Particulate exposure from the vault.”

“You should have reported that.”

“I intended to include it in the assessment.”

Another falsehood. Easier to pronounce, worse to hear.

Serev rested two fingers on Elian’s wrist. Elian fought the urge to pull away.

“You were always susceptible to overidentification with your subjects,” Serev said. “That is why I insisted on your discipline.”

“I remember.”

“Do you? Knowledge of suffering is useful. Participation in it is not.”

Their fingers lifted.

“Empathy is a virulent mental illness. It persuades the patient that another person’s injury must become their own. Eventually no boundary remains.”

Elian thought of the grid, of houses warmed by people gathered to sing.

“Will I require correction?”

Serev watched them for a moment longer.

“Not on the present evidence. Your physiological record will remain under review.”

They stood.

“If anything else occurs, come to me first.”

The familiar instruction nearly undid Elian. They wanted, with humiliating force, to believe it was an offer of safety.

“Yes, Inquisitor.”

***

At the end of shift, Elian went home. They swallowed their prescribed stabilizer, stood beneath the apartment’s inspection light, and waited until their hands stopped trembling.

Then they requested archival access for an unfinished contamination assessment.

Authorization arrived automatically.

There was no night inside the archive, but outside its high windows the city had become a field of identical pale rectangles.

Elian sat at their workstation and detached the tablet’s backing.

For several seconds they could not touch the wafer.

They were afraid of another recording. They were afraid it would remain silent.

Finally, they laid one finger on the amber lines.

“Show me,” they whispered.

## Chapter 3: The Engineers of Feeling

The wafer warmed beneath Elian’s finger.

Light rose from the survey tablet, assembling a room that had not existed for centuries. Its windows stood open. Beyond them, rain fell through sunlight onto a square crowded with people.

Someone was laughing.

Elian looked toward the archive door before remembering that the sound belonged to the recording.

A woman leaned across a table strewn with diagrams. Silver wire encircled her wrists; ink stained the heel of one hand.

“You can’t ask a district to sustain wonder for six hours,” she said. “People have laundry.”

A man outside the image answered, “Then we build for laundry.”

The woman laughed again and turned a diagram toward him. Amber channels branched beneath streets, converging at a structure labeled RESERVOIR INTERFACE.

“Variable intake,” she said. “Redundant pathways. Let the city feel whatever it feels.”

Elian stopped the recording.

They had expected solemn declarations, perhaps ritual invocations around misunderstood machinery. Instead there were calculations, revisions, an argument about ordinary maintenance.

They enlarged the diagram. The conversion architecture matched the operating abstract recovered from the chamber: neural patterns interacting with responsive crystal, regulating the reservoir’s release. Alongside equations were annotations in several hands.

No district should have to earn its heat by being happy.

And beneath it:

Agreed. Baseline provision independent of affective contribution.

Elian read the exchange three times.

The people who had built this system had anticipated coercion. They had argued against it in the margins.

A tremor passed through Elian’s hand. They pressed it flat against the desk.

“Historical material may induce identification,” they whispered. “Identification is not evidence.”

But the equations were evidence. So were the load tables, the repair schedules, the weathered junction photographed beside its schematic. Elian could dismiss the laughter as manipulation only by pretending not to recognize the engineering.

They resumed the sequence.

ARCHITECTS’ CIVIC RECORD, read a heading. SEVENTH REVISION.

A chamber appeared, circular and crowded. At its center, a child read haltingly from a sheet of paper while adults listened. Her poem concerned a tree removed to widen a road. When she finished, a transport planner asked her to show them where it had stood.

The recording skipped.

Now musicians occupied a pumping station. Workers adjusted valves as a low chorus gathered strength. Amber light traveled through the wall conduits; gauges steadied. One worker sang badly and without embarrassment.

Another fragment showed a public council listening to accounts of a winter power failure. An engineer stood beside a woman whose fingers had been damaged by cold. He did not look away while she spoke.

Elian had cataloged the pre-Collapse councils as theatrical and inefficient. The approved histories called their attention to individual suffering a failure of scale.

Here were the records omitted from those histories: revised insulation standards, reserve allocations, signatures. Testimony had become policy. A song had helped steady a pump.

Elian leaned toward the little square of light.

They wanted, absurdly, to enter it.

Not the council chamber, nor the shining streets, but the pumping station. To stand beside the worker who could not sing. To hear a mistake without waiting for someone to report it.

The desire hurt beneath their ribs.

They searched for a classification and found only an old word from prohibited literature.

Homesickness.

Impossible. They had never been there.

The next recording opened on white walls.

Elian recoiled so violently that the chair struck the cabinet behind them.

For an instant the projection vanished beneath another room: a narrow cot, their bare heels against cold metal, something fastened around their wrist. Someone beyond a glass partition held both hands against it.

A face should have been above those hands.

Elian could not recover it.

Only wetness on a cheek. A mouth moving. Their own small voice asking whether they had done something wrong.

Then the archive returned around them.

They were standing. They did not remember rising.

On the tablet, the white room resolved into an early medical workshop. A researcher demonstrated a crystal sensor against her palm. Nothing restrained her. The resemblance had been accidental.

Elian’s pulse battered their throat.

Stabilization memories were unreliable. They knew this from the recovery manuals. Residual distress could attach itself to unrelated stimuli and produce false recollection.

Yet they remembered the cold through the soles of their feet.

A chime sounded overhead.

“Archive sector inspection. Remain at assigned stations.”

Elian extinguished the projection.

Through the frosted partition, two shadows crossed the corridor. Custodians. Their boots sounded together, paused, then separated.

The wafer lay exposed beside the tablet.

Elian reached for the backing, but a fastening tab had bent. It would not close. They pressed harder; plastic clicked sharply.

A shadow stopped outside.

They looked down at the desk’s service recess. Years earlier, a replacement ventilation panel had been fitted over the original casing, leaving a shallow gap behind it. Elian had reported the defect twice.

They caught its edge with a fingernail, pulled it loose, and slid the cloth-wrapped wafer inside.

The door opened before they could reseat the lower corner.

Thorne stood beneath the inspection light.

“You’re still here.”

“Contamination assessment.”

Thorne’s gaze moved to the open tablet.

“At this hour?”

“The chamber is scheduled for sterilization.”

Elian lowered their hand beside their knee. The panel’s corner pressed against their trouser leg.

A Custodian appeared behind Thorne.

“Any unauthorized media?”

Thorne looked at Elian.

For one dreadful moment Elian wanted to explain everything: the woman with ink on her hand, the heating guarantee, the person behind the glass. The need to tell someone felt almost stronger than fear.

“Only inventory equipment,” Elian said.

The Custodian scanned their identification and moved on.

Thorne remained.

“You look unwell.”

“Fatigue.”

“You should leave before they decide otherwise.”

It might have been concern. Elian could no longer tell what concern looked like when spoken in the permitted language.

Thorne stepped back. The door closed.

Elian waited until the footsteps faded, then knelt and secured the panel. Behind the metal lay laughter, equations, a city that had made room for both.

They rested their hand there once before leaving.

## Chapter 4: A Crack in the Mask

Sleep returned the face in pieces.

First a mouth curved above a cup. Then a thumb wiping something sweet from Elian’s chin. A woman leaned across a table, smiling as though Elian had accomplished something extraordinary by spilling their breakfast.

Mother.

The word arrived before the face was whole.

Elian woke with their hand extended into the empty room.

The residential lights had brightened to morning intensity. On the wall, the daily instruction advanced through its familiar sequence.

BALANCE IS THE ABSENCE OF DISTURBANCE.

They lowered their hand beneath the blanket.

There had been another image. A house with fire showing behind its windows. Someone reaching through smoke, fingers spread. Elian could not place that hand beside the woman at the table. They could not even be certain the house was theirs.

Their training supplied an explanation: unregulated associations, generated by exposure to contaminated records.

They repeated it while dressing. They repeated it while standing before the dispenser with their stabilizer cupped in one palm.

The tablet was small, white, scored down the middle.

Elian swallowed it.

They hated the relief of having obeyed.

***

The disturbance was outside the east transit entrance.

At first Elian heard only a scrape that did not belong among the measured footsteps: a heel dragging over pavement. Two Custodians were moving a woman toward a correction vehicle. One held her elbow; the other had twisted her wrist behind her back.

“I only asked where they took him.”

Her voice broke on the last word.

Pedestrians adjusted their routes without looking up. Elian began to do the same.

“Please. He won’t know where I am.”

A thin cord hung from her free hand. At its end swung a child’s mitten, gray with a red darn across the thumb.

The shoe in the recording had been red.

Elian’s stomach contracted. They fixed their gaze on the transit sign and counted its illuminated letters.

Fourteen steps to the entrance. Twelve.

The woman stumbled. Her cheek struck the vehicle door.

Elian turned.

“Has the associated material been logged?”

Both Custodians looked at them.

For an instant, Elian could not understand why they had spoken. Then they saw the woman’s face: surprise beneath the pain, a foolish, dangerous attention.

“This is an expressive-disorder detention,” the nearer Custodian said. “Continue to transit.”

Elian raised their identification.

“Senior Historian Voss. I’m conducting a contamination assessment in the eastern archive sector. That object may be relevant.”

The mitten turned on its cord.

“Relevant how?”

“A recurrent visual motif in proscribed material.”

They heard Serev’s voice in the precision of their own. It made the lie easier to deliver and worse to endure.

The Custodian checked the identification against his wrist reader.

“Your review status is restricted.”

“Then you understand the need to preserve the chain of evidence.”

The woman had stopped struggling. Elian did not look directly at her again.

“Place the textile on the examination ledge,” they said. “Without the detainee handling it further.”

“There’s no ledge.”

“In the transit vestibule. Beside the service corridor.”

Elian knew that corridor. They had used it during a transport inventory: an unmanned passage to the municipal loading court, with a door that opened outward.

The Custodian considered the crowded pavement, then pushed the woman toward the vestibule.

Inside, the second officer remained by the outer doors while the first unclipped a specimen sleeve. Elian set their tablet on the narrow ledge and opened an empty accession form.

“Both hands visible,” they told the woman.

Her wrist was released.

She laid the mitten down carefully.

Elian shifted between her and the outer doors, holding the tablet toward the Custodian.

“Your authorization code.”

As he bent over the screen, Elian glanced at the service passage.

The woman did not move.

Their chest hurt. Perhaps she thought it was a test. Perhaps she had already learned that an offered kindness was another instrument of correction.

“The court,” Elian whispered.

Her breath caught.

Then her shoes whispered over the floor behind them.

The Custodian looked up.

Elian let the tablet fall.

It struck the ledge, then the floor, its casing splitting with a crack. The officer recoiled. Beyond him, the service door opened and shut.

“Detainee moving,” he said into his collar.

Elian crouched beside the tablet. Their hands were shaking so badly they could scarcely gather the pieces.

The officer hauled them upright.

“Did you direct her?”

“I directed her to surrender the object.”

His grip tightened.

That, Elian discovered, was true.

He released them to run after his partner. A detention alert sounded in the vestibule.

Elian remained beside the ledge until their knees could bear walking. The mitten was still there. They put it into the specimen sleeve before they left.

***

By evening, no summons had come.

The absence was not reassurance. It gave Elian room to imagine every camera angle, every word the officers would report. At their workstation they entered the mitten under a provisional accession number. They could not make themselves discard it, though keeping it might turn a suspicion into proof.

The damaged tablet lay beside the keyboard.

Once the sector emptied, Elian loosened the ventilation panel and retrieved the wafer.

They sat with it in their palm before connecting it to the workstation’s inspection port.

A woman appeared on the screen, listening to someone outside the frame. She laughed, reached toward the recorder, and the image tilted toward a sunlit floor.

Nothing momentous. No discovery, no civic achievement.

Elian pressed pause.

Their mother had reached like that.

For one instant they remembered her whole face, bent close, waiting for an answer to a question they could no longer hear. Then it was gone. Elian tried to follow it and found the cold cot instead, the restraint, the glass.

They had called that treatment removal.

But what had been removed could not wake in the night and reach for someone. It could not recognize a stranger’s fear before Elian had decided to recognize it.

The Stabilate had buried it alive.

Elian covered their mouth. They had endangered their work, perhaps their life, for a woman whose name they did not know. They were afraid of her capture, afraid of their own arrest, and ashamed that those fears competed.

Still, if they could return to the vestibule, they would tell her where the door led.

“Compassion,” they said.

The word hurt in their throat.

A notification opened across the paused image.

INQUISITOR KAELEN SEREV  
MANDATORY EVALUATION  
HALL OF CORRECTION  
0700

Beneath it waited a single line.

Acknowledge receipt.

Elian wiped their eyes before touching the key.

## Chapter 5: The Inquisitor's Lesson

The Hall of Correction smelled of warmed metal.

Elian stopped beneath its entrance scanner and remembered, without warning, a small bare foot lifting from a floor too cold to stand on. Their foot. The memory vanished when the scanner instructed them to proceed.

Before leaving the archive, they had disconnected the wafer and slipped it into the split lining of their coat. Concealment on their person was irrational. Leaving it for the sterilization crews had proved impossible.

Now it rested against their ribs as an attendant took their damaged tablet.

“Inquisitor Serev will retain your coat if further examination is required.”

Elian unfastened one button before understanding that this was not yet an instruction.

The attendant watched.

They fastened it again.

***

Serev sat at a white table without restraints. That was the first thing Elian noticed, and the first thing they wished they had not.

“Sit.”

A narrow sensor strip waited beneath their wrists.

“You have been sleeping poorly,” Serev said.

“Intermittently.”

“A distinction without useful difference. Place your hands flat.”

Elian obeyed.

Serev looked unchanged: close-cropped gray hair, immaculate collar, the faint depression beside the mouth that had once seemed a mark of patience.

“Let us begin with first principles. What is suffering?”

“A maladaptive response to adverse conditions.”

“And our obligation?”

“To correct the conditions where feasible. To correct the response where necessary.”

“A citizen grieves for a dead child. Can the condition be corrected?”

“No.”

“Then?”

“The response must be treated.”

The mitten lay in a specimen drawer beneath a label Elian had written. They pictured its red darn with such clarity that they pressed their fingers against the sensor.

Serev glanced down.

“Does treatment wrong the citizen?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because distress impairs judgment.”

“Therefore consent given under distress is unreliable.”

“Yes.”

“And refusal?”

Elian’s mouth had dried.

“Also unreliable.”

Serev folded their hands. “You remember your training.”

Remembering was the danger.

A glass wall. A wrist held still. Someone beyond the glass lifting both hands.

And beside the cot, not an anonymous attendant now, but Serev—young enough that their hair was dark, wearing the same uncreased collar.

You haven’t done anything wrong.

The words arrived with the remembered pressure of a palm against Elian’s forehead.

We are going to save you from this.

“Elian.”

They looked up.

“Where did you go?”

“To the premise.”

“Explain.”

“If refusal is evidence of impairment, there is no response by which a patient may demonstrate competence.”

For a moment the ventilation was the only sound.

“Competence is assessed independently,” Serev said. “That is why we have physicians rather than referendums.”

“Of course.”

“You used to find that reassuring.”

Elian could not tell whether this was a question.

***

Serev touched the table. A speaker rose through a circular opening.

“An auditory assessment. You have undergone these before.”

“Yes.”

“Describe what you hear. Avoid attribution.”

A breath scraped through the speaker. Then another, fast and shallow.

Elian fixed their gaze on the seam around it.

The scream began abruptly, broke, began again.

Their body wanted to rise. There was no thought before the impulse, only the intolerable certainty that someone needed help. Their knees tightened beneath the table.

“Describe,” Serev said.

“An adult voice. Severe respiratory strain. Sustained vocalization.”

“Cause?”

“Insufficient evidence.”

“Probable cause?”

“Pain.”

The word escaped too quickly.

“Distress,” Elian corrected.

In the recording, something rattled. The voice dwindled to a sound Elian could barely bear to hear.

“Relapse patient,” Serev said. “Third admission. They had ceased medication to preserve an attachment.”

The scream returned.

Elian imagined a mouth open until the jaw hurt. They tried to stop imagining it. Their training supplied terms—stimulus, agitation, pathological fixation—and each seemed to move them farther from the person while leaving the pain untouched.

“Would you terminate the recording?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“It has yielded sufficient observational data.”

Serev let it continue.

Elian counted breaths. Their own, not the patient’s. If they counted the patient’s, they would begin waiting for each one.

At last the speaker fell silent.

“The wish to stop a stimulus,” Serev said, “can arise from several motives.”

Elian kept their hands flat.

“Look at me.”

They did.

Serev studied their face for so long that Elian felt the effort of holding it still become another expression.

Then the Inquisitor withdrew the sensor strip.

***

In the corridor beyond the evaluation room, two Custodians waited with field armor.

“A transit structure failed in the western exclusion sector this morning,” Serev said. “A fugitive was present at the control junction. Witnesses describe an expressive episode immediately before the collapse.”

“An episode caused structural failure?”

“That is under investigation.”

Elian heard the distinction. Serev heard them hear it.

“The district contains obsolete civic machinery,” the Inquisitor continued. “Your expertise will be useful.”

One Custodian offered Elian a compact service weapon.

“I am not field-qualified.”

“You retain your civil certification.”

“That was twelve years ago.”

“The operating principle is unchanged.”

The grip settled into their hand, heavier than remembered.

Serev stepped close enough to straighten the weapon’s safety loop around their wrist.

“Treatment is not punishment, Elian.”

The cold cot returned. The hand on their forehead.

They could not make themself answer.

***

By midmorning they were in the Burned District, where roofless buildings interrupted the white city’s distant geometry.

The Custodians searched in silence. Elian followed Serev through a collapsed maintenance station, past ceramic conduits marked with symbols they recognized from the Archive.

Under a fallen stair, dust shifted.

A breath caught in the darkness below.

Serev raised one hand. The others stopped.

“There,” the Inquisitor said softly. “Cover the opening.”

Elian lifted the weapon.

Beyond its sight, between broken steps, a human eye opened.

“Hold,” Serev ordered.

Elian held. Everything in them reached down into the dark.

## Chapter 6: The Burned District

The eye beneath the fallen stair did not blink.

Elian kept the service weapon trained on the opening while dust sifted through the ruined ceiling. Behind them, the Custodians formed a pale line against the Burned District’s soot-black walls. Serev stood apart, immaculate in a gray field coat.

“Identify yourself,” Serev said.

The eye shifted toward the voice.

“Cael.”

“Cael,” Serev repeated. “You are under correctional arrest for expressive sabotage, destruction of civic transit infrastructure, and propagation of unlicensed affective response.”

“I didn’t destroy anything.”

“You caused a transit collapse.”

“I was trying to stop it.”

Serev looked at Elian. “Remove him.”

Two Custodians began shifting the broken concrete. Elian watched through the widening gap. When Cael’s face emerged, he looked younger than expected—perhaps twenty, with unevenly shorn hair and dried blood beside one temple. His maintenance uniform was torn, its district insignia burned away.

A Custodian seized his arm. Cael flinched.

The motion was slight, but Elian felt it in their own wrist: the remembered pressure of a restraint tightening over bone.

“Do not resist,” the Custodian said.

“I’m not.”

They dragged Cael into the open. One leg was bent beneath him. He tried to stand and failed.

“His leg is injured,” Elian said.

Serev glanced at them. “You are observant.”

“It affects transport.”

“It affects nothing.”

Cael looked at Elian. His eyes were fever-bright.

“You have it,” he said.

Elian’s fingers tightened around the weapon. “Have what?”

“The song.”

Serev turned toward him. “What song?”

“I hear it when I sleep.” Cael coughed, spitting dust. “A woman crying. Someone laughing through it. Children counting under the floor. Sometimes there’s a voice telling me to remember the red shoe.”

The Lament Archive lay hidden in the split lining of Elian’s coat, its amber-threaded wafer pressed against their ribs. They had not played it since leaving the archive, but its voices had followed them into sleep.

Cael kept watching them. “It says, ‘For whoever still needs us.’”

Elian forgot to breathe.

Serev noticed.

“Emotional contagion,” the Inquisitor said. “Unremoved and progressing.”

Cael laughed once. “You call it that because you don’t know what else to call being alive.”

A Custodian struck him across the face. Cael dropped to his knees.

Elian stepped forward before they could stop themself. The weapon rose—not at the Custodian, but toward the space between them. The Custodians shifted.

Serev’s attention sharpened.

“Enough,” they said. “The fugitive will serve as a public example. The district assembly begins in forty minutes.”

Cael lifted his head. Blood ran from his lip. “An example of what?”

“Of the consequences of unregulated attachment.”

“I didn’t cause the collapse.” He looked at Elian. “The old system woke up.”

Serev’s expression hardened. “There is no old system.”

“Then why did the lights come on?”

Silence settled over the station.

Elian remembered the ceramic conduits beneath the floor, marked with symbols matching those in the Archive. They remembered the diagrams of responsive crystals and the notes about districts losing capacity when music halls closed. Somewhere beneath the Burned District, something had answered Cael’s distress.

Not destruction.

Recognition.

Serev drew their sidearm.

“Elian,” they said, “you will record the execution.”

The weapon in Elian’s hand became impossibly heavy.

They had recorded thousands of deaths in the archive: dates, causes, procedural compliance. The doctrine insisted that clean language prevented secondary disturbance. But Cael was not an entry. He was a face beneath broken stone, a voice carrying a song from a sealed room.

“Record,” Serev repeated.

Cael’s eyes closed.

The chamber filled with the memory of a scream—not the recording from the evaluation, but the cry of the woman behind glass. A younger Elian had begged to know what they had done wrong.

You haven’t done anything wrong.

Serev’s old voice.

We are going to save you from this.

Elian’s arm did not move.

One second passed.

Then another.

Hesitation was not an action, the doctrine taught. It was merely the absence of decision.

Serev watched them understand that this was a lie.

“Elian.”

Cael opened his eyes. In his bound hands, something glinted: a thin black chip, no larger than a fingernail. He twisted sharply, breaking the Custodian’s grip for half a breath, and shoved it into Elian’s palm.

“Below the scaffold,” he whispered.

Serev fired.

The shot cracked through the station. Cael’s body jerked. A dark mark opened beneath his collarbone, and he slid down the support column, leaving blood across the ash.

Elian stared at him.

A tear slipped free and traveled down their cheek.

Serev lowered the weapon. Their gaze moved from Cael’s body to Elian’s face.

“There,” they said.

The Custodians turned.

Elian closed their hand around the chip. The wafer pressed against their ribs. The dead boy, the Archive, the woman with the mitten, the mother behind the glass—all of it gathered into the single unbearable fact of the tear.

“Arrest Historian Voss,” Serev ordered.

Elian ran.

A Custodian caught their coat, but Elian twisted free, leaving a strip of fabric in the gloved hand. They vaulted the broken rail and plunged into the maze of collapsed streets beyond the station. Boots struck stone behind them. Someone shouted their name.

The Burned District opened in blackened avenues and roofless buildings. Elian ran past dead civic towers and windows filled with ash, no longer trying to regulate their breath. The chip cut into their palm. The tear cooled on their skin.

Ahead, a maintenance scaffold leaned over the chasm where the old transit line had vanished. Elian slipped beneath it and disappeared into the ruins, carrying the forbidden Archive against their heart.

## Chapter 7: Secrets in the Scaffold

The scaffold shook under the weight of the Custodians.

Elian hung beneath it, one foot braced against a broken conduit, both hands gripping a crossbar furred with rust. Below, the transit chasm held no visible bottom. Dust fell past their face.

“Search the western access.”

A light passed over the far wall. Elian pressed their cheek against the metal and held still until the footsteps moved away.

Beside their knee, a maintenance hatch stood open a hand’s breadth. They hooked a heel into it and pulled. The hinge gave a low complaint. Elian froze, then dragged themselves sideways into darkness.

They landed on a narrow shelf, biting their sleeve against the cry. Something warm ran down their shin. Above them, the scaffold rattled again.

The shaft smelled of mineral deposits and old lubricant. Elian drew the hatch almost shut, leaving enough space to hear.

“Historian Voss.”

Serev’s voice, amplified, came from somewhere above the chasm.

“Remain stationary. Your condition is treatable.”

Elian covered their mouth.

Cael had been stationary. On his knees, with one leg twisted beneath him.

They waited until the loudspeaker began repeating the instruction farther away.

Only then did they unclench their hand.

The chip had cut a little black rectangle into their palm.

Their damaged tablet was still in the inner coat pocket. A diagonal fracture crossed its screen; the casing had separated near the access port. Elian disabled its network receiver before waking it fully, then watched the status field until the transmission indicator went dark.

Cael’s chip fitted the port.

For several seconds nothing happened. Then a request appeared beneath the cracked glass.

LOCAL INDEX CORRUPTED. RECONSTRUCT?

Elian selected yes.

A diagram assembled in fragments: conduits, chambers, vertical shafts. Most terminated in blank space. Three points bore a small symbol of two overlapping hands.

Below them appeared a title.

FEELING: WITNESS ROUTES. PARTIAL COPY.

Elian touched the nearest point. A route opened from a maintenance scaffold in the western exclusion sector to a place labeled FOUNDATION ACCESS NINE.

Then text replaced the map.

*If I do not reach you, preserve the records. Do not trust the account of the Collapse.*

Elian sat down hard against the shaft wall.

*Emergency orders preceded the failures. Not followed. The first Stabilators isolated the reservoirs, interrupted the civic bridges, and suppressed the operators. They knew the baseline reserves would last eleven days. They announced rescue on the twelfth.*

There were attachments: dispatch authorizations, reservoir isolation schedules, orders bearing seals Elian knew from the earliest Stabilate collections. One authorization classified the evacuation of a heating district as an unacceptable risk to public compliance.

Elian enlarged the signature.

In the archive, that administrator’s portrait hung above the Hall of Civic Recovery.

Their training supplied objections. A copied seal proved nothing. Documents could be altered. Sequence did not establish intent.

Then they opened the next order.

*Maintain interruption until acceptance of mandatory stabilization exceeds projected resistance.*

Elian read it twice.

For years they had cataloged deaths beneath headings such as Infrastructure Exhaustion and Collective Affective Failure. They had corrected students who attributed agency to catastrophe. The records, they had said, did not support such claims.

The records had been chosen for them.

Below the orders, Cael’s message resumed.

*We have witnesses as well as files. Some of us still maintain the old lines. Some only keep names. Ask before you carry anything farther. People have died because a route was trusted after it stopped being safe.*

The final sentence stood alone.

*If you found the song, let someone else hear it.*

Elian bent forward over the tablet.

They had not asked Cael whom he wanted notified. They had not even lowered the weapon while he tried to explain himself. He had looked at them and seen someone who might help; they had looked at him and searched for a diagnosis.

A sound escaped them, small and ugly.

They tried to stop it. Their throat closed, opened again. Tears struck the broken screen, blurring the map beneath their fingers.

They remembered Serev saying *There*, with the satisfaction of an instructor identifying an error.

Elian struck the wall with the heel of their hand.

Pain flared through the palm holding Cael’s chip. They stopped before striking again. In the dark, they could hear their own breath coming too quickly, and beneath it the quiet drip of water.

They wanted Serev to suffer.

The wish frightened them almost as much as the execution had. They sat with it, unable to make it respectable, unable to make it disappear.

After a while they wiped the tablet on their sleeve.

Inside their coat, the Archive wafer rested against their ribs. They had thought its purpose was proof: evidence to be authenticated, arranged, presented to some authority capable of correction.

But no authority had permitted their first tear.

A stranger’s recorded grief had reached them before they could defend against it. Then they had heard the woman asking for her son and been unable to pass her by. Cael had carried the same voices in his sleep.

The Archive could travel farther than an argument. From recording to listener, from listener to another person. What Serev called contagion might be the means by which isolation ended.

Elian held the coat closed over it. For the first time since the shot, they could imagine doing something beyond surviving.

Metal scraped above them.

A white beam entered through the hatch.

Elian extinguished the tablet and slid off the shelf. Their injured leg nearly folded. They caught a ladder rung and lowered themselves while the hatch opened overhead.

“Access shaft. Checking.”

Light descended after them.

At the next landing, Elian found a narrow inspection slit and forced themselves through sideways. A buckle snagged; they pulled until fabric tore. The beam swept across their empty ladder.

They stayed wedged behind a pipe until the Custodian’s boots climbed away.

Then they brought up the map.

Two turns. A descending passage. Seven sealed service bays.

At the seventh, mineral crust covered the wall. Elian searched beneath it with bleeding fingertips until they found the overlapping hands.

There was no handle.

They pressed where the map indicated and leaned their weight against the stone-faced panel. For a moment nothing moved. Then a latch released deep inside the wall.

Cool air touched their wet face.

Elian looked back once, listening for pursuit, and pulled the hidden door wide enough to enter the foundations beneath the city.

## Chapter 8: The Sympathizer's Network

The door closed behind Elian, taking the last of the municipal light.

“Stay where you are.”

A lamp uncovered itself ahead. Behind it stood a person with a scar running from one corner of their mouth beneath their collar. They held a length of metal pipe.

Elian stopped. Blood moved warmly inside their shoe.

“Who gave you the route?”

“Cael.”

The pipe lowered a little.

“Where is he?”

Elian had rehearsed nothing for this. They had spent their professional life reducing deaths to dates, causes, approved classifications.

“Serev shot him.”

The stranger closed their eyes. In the silence, Elian heard water dripping into a vessel.

“I’m Rhen,” they said at last. “Can you walk another twenty steps?”

***

The room beyond had once housed pressure regulators. Now blankets divided it into narrow sleeping spaces. Six people sat around a table under a shaded lamp. A seventh stood at a stove, stirring something that smelled of onions.

Everyone looked up.

“Cael?” asked the person at the stove.

Rhen shook their head.

A spoon struck the floor. Someone reached across the table and took another person’s hand. Nobody instructed them to compose themselves.

Elian backed against the wall.

“I may have brought pursuit.”

“We’ll check the approaches.” Rhen motioned to a narrow-faced man beside the door. “Davin.”

He left without looking at Elian.

A woman brought a basin and knelt beside their injured leg.

“May I?”

Elian nodded, then flinched when she cut the cloth away.

“I haven’t started yet,” she said gently.

“I know.”

She waited until they nodded again.

Across the room, a child was drawing on the blank reverse of a sanitation notice. Two people stood beneath an enormous yellow circle.

“What is that?” Elian asked.

“The sun.”

“It isn’t that color anymore.”

“It is in my grandmother’s story.”

Elian looked away before the child could see their face change.

Rhen sat nearby. “Cael said someone in the state archive might have found a recording.”

Elian covered the split lining of their coat.

“You don’t have to give it to me.”

“You’re an archivist?”

“Used to be. Municipal oral histories. Before they decided testimony was a particularly unreliable form of illness.”

“What do you preserve now?”

Rhen glanced around the room. “What people can bear to remember. Songs. Names. How to comfort someone without explaining why they should stop crying.”

Elian took out the wafer.

Nobody reached for it.

***

They played only a fragment.

A woman laughed, then apologized for laughing. Another voice told her not to apologize. Behind them came the faint clatter of dishes.

The woman treating Elian’s leg sat back on her heels.

“My father did that,” she whispered. “Always laughed when he was frightened.”

Elian had heard the recording before. Here, among strangers, it became almost unbearable.

“I thought I was losing my judgment,” they said.

“You might be,” Rhen replied. “Being able to feel doesn’t make us right about everything.”

Elian stared at them.

“But it means there’s something to judge besides compliance.”

At the table, an older man began to describe his father’s hands. Nobody hurried him. When he forgot a word, the child offered three improbable alternatives until he laughed.

Elian could not follow the conversation. They were watching how people turned toward whoever spoke, how a silence could be allowed to last.

“The Stabilate’s strongest weapon isn’t the drug,” Rhen said quietly. “It’s having nobody you can tell. You start believing your own life is a symptom.”

“What if telling makes it worse?”

“Sometimes it does. That’s why we ask first.”

Rhen rested a hand on the bench between them.

“Would you like someone to sit closer?”

Elian almost said no. Their body had already leaned toward the offered hand.

Rhen moved slowly. The embrace was awkward, Elian’s injured palm caught between them, their shoulders rigid beneath the torn coat. Then Rhen adjusted, and Elian felt another person’s breathing.

They began to cry.

No console registered it. No one asked them to account for the fluid on Rhen’s sleeve.

***

The warning bell sounded while the soup was being poured.

One sharp note. Then the scrape of the outer door opening.

Rhen stood.

Davin entered first. Behind him came the white faceplate of a Custodian.

For a moment Elian thought he was a prisoner. Then Davin stepped aside.

“They promised relocation,” he said. His eyes stayed on the floor. “My sister is in Correction. They said they’d release her.”

The woman beside Elian rose with a wet bandage still in her hand.

“You knew where my children slept.”

“I didn’t give them your names.”

The Custodian struck her with the weapon’s stock.

Rhen seized Elian’s collar and pulled them backward as the lamp shattered. Someone screamed. Under the table, the child’s yellow sun caught the stove light.

Elian lunged toward it—toward the child—but an older man had already swept the child into his arms.

“Go,” he shouted.

Rhen dragged a metal case from beneath the bench.

“The Archive.”

Elian pressed the wafer into its sleeve and thrust it inside their coat. Behind them came orders to kneel, a shot, the heavy collapse of the table.

Rhen shoved them through a maintenance hatch.

“I can help,” Elian gasped.

“Then hold this.”

The case was heavier than expected. Through its cracked lid Elian saw coils, a folded antenna, hand-soldered connections.

Rhen slammed the hatch. A latch snapped beneath their hand. It would not keep anyone out for long.

They crawled.

***

At the next junction, Rhen stopped to listen. Blood ran from their ear.

“That was everyone?” Elian asked.

“No. There are other rooms. Other routes.”

“Davin knew them?”

Rhen did not answer.

Elian gripped the transmitter’s handle. Alone, they would have only their own mistakes to fear. They could take the case, follow the map, never again sit close enough to someone to be held.

Rhen pointed left. “I can get you out.”

“Why should I trust you?”

“You shouldn’t because I ask.”

Something in Rhen’s expression changed.

“I knew your mother.”

Elian’s hand tightened.

“Before your stabilization,” Rhen said. “I can tell you what I remember. Somewhere we can stop.”

Behind the hatch, metal rang against metal.

Elian wanted proof. They wanted a voice, a name, the impossible return of a hand through smoke.

Instead, they shifted the case between them.

“Take the other handle,” they said.

Together, they lifted it.

## Chapter 9: The Public Testimony

The case struck the wall at every turn. Each impact made Elian think of the lamp shattering, the child lifted into darkness, the woman who had asked permission before touching their wound.

Behind them, metal gave way.

“Here.” Rhen shouldered open a narrow grate. Cold air breathed through it. “They’ll expect us to follow the water lines.”

They dragged the transmitter into a shaft scarcely wide enough for both of them. Rhen pulled the grate shut and guided Elian upward by a ladder slick with condensation. Below, boots passed the opening.

Elian kept climbing. For once, survival felt less like an instinct than a betrayal.

***

They stopped beneath the central tower, where obsolete conduits rose through concrete like roots turned toward the sky.

Rhen bound Elian’s leg with a strip torn from their own shirt. Their hands shook.

“The transmitter needs a carrier,” they said. “This won’t reach the city alone.”

Above them, the synchronization bell sounded its preliminary note.

Every morning, citizens stood at their assigned terminals while implants and public screens delivered the state affirmations. Elian had stood through thousands of them: hands still, breathing measured, grateful for instructions about what they were.

“The tower,” they said.

“The old maintenance channel still feeds its distribution spine.”

“All receivers?”

Rhen nodded.

Elian looked at the case. “We would be doing what they do. Putting something inside people without asking.”

For a moment there was only the ticking of cooling pipes.

“We can open with a warning,” Rhen said. “Tell them how to disconnect. No restraint, no penalty.”

“They may not believe us.”

“No.”

There was no clean instrument waiting beneath the ruins. Only this one, and what they chose to do with it.

Elian took out the wafer.

“My mother,” they said. “Did she want me stabilized?”

Rhen’s hands stopped.

“No. She stood outside the treatment rooms until they carried her away. I was there taking names.”

Elian pressed the wafer’s sleeve between their palms. They wanted details, proof, an account complete enough to live inside. Above them, the second bell began.

“Tell me the rest afterward.”

Rhen nodded once.

***

Their ascent took them through a disused service lift and into the tower’s distribution chamber. Rhen used a mechanical key whose teeth had been filed almost smooth.

Beyond the glass, the city waited in its colorless morning.

The carrier console stood beneath a bank of white indicators.

Serev stood beside it.

Elian’s first thought was absurdly familiar: they were late for an evaluation.

“Set down the case,” he said.

Rhen lowered it without releasing the handle.

“How?” Elian asked.

“You cataloged the city’s surviving pre-Collapse interfaces. I read your reports.”

Of course he had. Their careful work, waiting here ahead of them.

Serev looked at Rhen’s bleeding ear, then at Elian’s bandaged leg.

“You have mistaken injury for understanding.”

“You killed Cael.”

“Yes.”

“Say his name.”

His jaw tightened. “Cael.”

The sound hurt more than Elian expected.

“Did you feel anything?”

Serev’s gaze shifted toward the windows.

“I feel everything you believe you have discovered.”

Elian stared at him.

“No,” they said.

“Did you imagine the people who administered stabilization were exempt? The first treatment cohorts were enforcement personnel. They could not ask us to do this work while leaving us vulnerable to it.”

“And it failed.”

“It requires maintenance.”

Elian remembered his hand adjusting a restraint on their childhood cot. Its steadiness had seemed proof that nothing terrible was happening.

“All those years,” they said. “You knew.”

“I knew what returned when treatment weakened. Panic. Attachment. The inability to make a necessary choice.”

“Pity.”

“Yes.” The word came sharply. “Pity, too.”

Serev opened a small medical case on the console. Inside lay a sealed injector.

“I can stop this. You remember enough to fear the procedure. You need not remain conscious for it.”

Rhen shifted beside the transmitter.

Serev drew his weapon.

“Do not.”

Elian’s body wanted the injector. The desire rose with humiliating force: no more Cael falling, no more wondering who had escaped the refuge, no more reaching toward memories that might never become whole.

Serev saw it.

“You’re exhausted,” he said softly. “Let me help you.”

Elian looked at the familiar hands that had once fastened their wrists.

“You would leave me able to watch you kill someone.”

“I would leave you able to function.”

“That isn’t the same.”

The final synchronization bell began.

Elian stepped between his weapon and Rhen.

“Feelings aren’t the disease,” they said. Their voice shook. “They’re the cure. Not because they make us good. Because without them, I let you tell me suffering didn’t matter.”

Serev seized their coat.

Elian caught his wrist as Rhen threw open the transmitter case.

The shot struck the wall beside the console.

Serev drove Elian backward against the glass. Pain burst through their shoulder. They clung to his weapon hand, smelling the medicinal soap they remembered from instruction rooms.

Behind him, Rhen forced a connector into the maintenance port.

“Don’t make me do this,” Serev said.

For an instant Elian nearly apologized.

Then they struck his wrist against the window frame.

The weapon fell. Serev drew the short correction blade at his belt and thrust it beneath Elian’s ribs.

There was pressure before pain.

Their knees struck the floor.

“Elian!”

“Finish it,” they tried to say.

Rhen was already pressing the wafer into the open reader. Amber light ran through the transmitter’s coils.

The console requested testimony.

Elian saw their own face appear on its small recording screen: bloodless, astonished, nothing like a historian authorized to speak.

“My name is Elian Voss.” Breath caught painfully. “What follows contains emotional recordings and evidence withheld by the Stabilate. You may disconnect. You will have done nothing wrong.”

Serev bent over them, blade lifting again.

Elian raised a hand.

They had meant to stop his arm. Instead their cut palm came to rest against his cheek.

His skin was warm.

He went utterly still.

Elian saw how carefully he held his mouth, how long he must have practiced keeping it from trembling. They knew that effort. They had lived inside it.

“You had nobody to tell,” they whispered.

Behind him, the white indicators turned amber.

The broadcast began.

## Chapter 10: The First Tear

The first sound was not a voice.

It was breathing.

A hundred breaths, a thousand, drawn from old recordings and braided through the tower’s distribution spine until the chamber itself seemed to inhale. The sound passed through the glass, through the console, through the implants of every citizen standing beneath the morning synchronization lamps.

Elian heard it and thought of the cold room.

The breath before a needle. The breath held by the woman behind the glass. Their own small lungs fighting the restraint straps.

Then the Archive spoke.

“My name is Elian Voss,” their recorded voice said, thin but steady. “What follows contains emotional recordings and evidence withheld by the Stabilate. You may disconnect. You will have done nothing wrong.”

Across the city, screens went dark.

Not black. Amber.

In the ration halls, in transit stations, in classrooms where children stood with their hands behind their backs, the Stabilate’s affirmations vanished. The calm white face of civic instruction dissolved into a field of wavering gold.

A woman began to laugh.

It was an old recording, bright with surprise, and the laughter broke apart halfway through into sobs. A child shouted somewhere beneath it. Someone sang three notes, stopped, then tried again. There were voices speaking names, names that had been reduced to errors and symptoms and erased from the civic register.

The city listened.

In the distribution chamber, Rhen crouched over the transmitter, one hand pressed against the coils. Amber light pulsed beneath their fingers.

“It’s carrying,” they said.

Elian could not answer. Blood was spreading hot beneath their shirt, then cooling against the tower floor. Each breath dragged the blade’s wound wider. Their shoulder throbbed. Their leg had gone numb below the bandage.

Serev still stood above them.

The correction blade remained in his hand. Its edge trembled.

On the nearest wall screen, the Archive showed a woman kneading bread. Her hands were flour-white. She sang as she worked, badly and without shame. The recording shifted to a crowded hall where hundreds of voices rose together, not in obedience but in response. A crystal network awakened beneath a city. Heat flowed through pipes. Lamps bloomed in apartments. Trains lifted on singing rails.

The testimony continued beneath the images.

“Baseline provision independent of affective contribution.”

“No district should have to earn its heat by being happy.”

The words appeared in every language the Stabilate had retained.

Outside the tower, the skyline began to change.

At first, nothing happened. The central clock completed its synchronization cycle. The command channels attempted to reassert the morning directive.

Then the directives began contradicting one another.

A regional administrator ordered all receivers disconnected. The eastern command countermanded the order, declaring the transmission an authorized historical emergency. A third voice called for immediate arrest of every person displaying affective instability. Behind them, a fourth voice was crying too hard to finish its sentence.

The command structure had always presented itself as a single mind.

Now its pieces spoke over one another.

In a residential block three districts away, a man removed his implant and held it in his palm. His wife, who had been dead for nineteen years, was laughing from the wall speaker. He pressed the device against his chest as if it might let her in.

At a school terminal, a teacher fell to her knees. Children gathered around her, uncertain at first, then frightened by the wetness on her face. One boy touched her cheek.

“Are you ill?” he asked.

She shook her head.

“No,” she said. “I remember.”

In the lower transit tunnels, passengers stopped between stations. Some pried open the doors. Some remained seated, staring at one another as if seeing strangers become human.

The Archive played grief next.

A low, wordless lament moved through the tower. It was not beautiful in the way the Stabilate defined beauty. It was uneven. It broke. A voice gasped for air and continued anyway.

Elian felt the sound enter their body through the wound.

Cael’s face appeared in memory: dust on his mouth, the burned insignia on his uniform, his fingers closing around the black chip.

If you found the song, let someone else hear it.

“I’m sorry,” Elian whispered.

Serev looked down.

For a moment Elian thought he had not heard. Then the Inquisitor’s mouth tightened.

“You don’t know what you’re apologizing for,” he said.

“No.”

It was the most honest answer Elian had ever given him.

The recording changed. Laughter returned, then a woman saying, I love you, and another voice answering, I know. The words were simple enough to be dangerous.

Serev’s hand closed around the blade.

Elian watched him fight himself.

They knew that posture. The rigid shoulders. The locked jaw. The inward turn, as though feeling were a room with no door and he could survive only by sealing it from within.

“You had nobody to tell,” Elian had said.

The sentence had not freed him. It had reached him.

On the screen, a crystalline reservoir flared gold. Across the city, old conduits shuddered awake. In dark apartments, neglected heat panels glimmered. A tower at the northern edge flickered, then held.

Serev lifted the blade.

The music beneath the transmission rose—not triumphant, not yet. It sounded like hundreds of people trying to find the same note.

A tear slipped from the corner of Serev’s eye.

It traveled slowly down his cheek, catching the amber light.

Elian saw their own first tear in it: the same shock, the same revulsion, the impossible proof that the body could betray the doctrine and remain alive.

Serev seemed to feel the tear only when it reached his mouth. His face twisted. He raised one hand as if to wipe it away, but stopped.

The blade lowered.

Not dropped. Not surrendered.

Lowered.

Rhen looked over, breathless. “Elian?”

“Keep it open,” Elian said.

The words barely emerged.

Rhen turned back to the transmitter. “I will.”

Beyond the glass, one window opened.

Then another.

A woman stepped onto a balcony and lifted both hands to her face. In the building opposite, a man reached across the empty space between himself and an old neighbor. Their hands met through the rails.

A light came on in a room that had been dark for years.

Somewhere below, someone began to sing.

The voice was untrained and shaking. Another joined it. Then another, from a different street, and another farther away. The city did not become harmonious. It became audible.

Elian lay bleeding on the white floor, watching the lights kindle one by one.

They thought of the woman behind the glass. The smile. The food wiped from their chin. The hand reaching through smoke.

They had carried her as a fragment because fragments were all the Stabilate had left them.

Now, with Serev standing silent beside them and the first songs rising through the tower, Elian let the memory become whole.

“Mother,” they whispered.

The word hurt.

Elian smiled anyway.
