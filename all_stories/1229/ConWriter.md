The archive’s climate system breathed cold air over my hands as I lowered the sealed case from its shelf. In the Republic, every object was catalogued, measured, and stripped of whatever sentimental contamination it might carry. I had spent eleven years preserving the official past: famines reduced to grain deficits, wars to logistical failures, revolts to instances of civic maladjustment. Emotion, according to the Ministry of Equilibrium, was an obsolete neurological disorder. Empathy was listed beside grief and affection in the Diagnostic Register, all three classified as destabilizing impulses.

The case had no accession number. That alone made my pulse misbehave.

I carried it to my worktable in the Sublevel Archive, beneath the Museum of Rational Progress, and placed it under the examination lamp. The object inside was a disk of black metal, no wider than my palm, webbed with copper filaments. At its center, a glassy surface held a faint amber glow. I had found it behind a false panel in a storage vault marked *Pre-Unification Power Systems*. The vault’s inventory claimed the room contained obsolete batteries. The disk had not appeared in any record.

My scanner recognized its age but not its purpose. Then, when I brushed dust from the center, the artifact warmed beneath my fingers.

A line of light unfolded across its surface.

**CIVIC HEART ARRAY: PRIMARY EMOTIVE CONVERSION NODE.**

I read it twice, refusing the first interpretation because it was impossible. The Republic taught that civilization had advanced only after humanity suppressed feeling. Before the Equilibrium, people had supposedly burned resources pursuing attachment, loyalty, and irrational hope. Yet the disk’s embedded text continued to assemble in the air above it, translating itself from an ancient code.

**Collective affect converted into usable current. Compassion stabilized distribution. Mourning maintained memory networks. Shared joy increased voluntary labor output.**

My mouth went dry. The old cities had not merely survived despite their feelings. Their feelings had powered them.

I leaned closer, and the disk answered with a projection: a map of the continent, threaded by rivers of amber light. Each pulse began in clusters labeled with words the Ministry had erased from public vocabulary—love, wonder, solidarity, sorrow—and traveled through civic towers into homes, hospitals, transit grids. The image showed a civilization glowing from millions of human responses, not from coal or fission. At the bottom, a final sentence appeared.

**The system fails when emotional variance is forcibly suppressed.**

Behind me, the archive door chimed.

I shut the projection with both hands. The amber map collapsed into the disk, but the lamp still caught its reflection in my eyes. “Identify,” said the corridor monitor. “State your purpose.”

“Routine artifact assessment,” I answered.

“Your vocal tremor indicates distress. Would you like to report a possible empathy episode?”

The question was automatic, but my body reacted as if a person had asked whether I was hurt. That was the danger of the old sickness: noticing another mind, imagining its fear, wanting to protect it. I hated the Ministry for naming that impulse illness. I hated myself more for understanding why they feared it.

I slid the disk into the inner pocket of my archive coat. “No report required.”

SEEK CORRECTION EARLY.** I forced my face into the blank expression required of licensed historians and walked toward the public lift.

My objective was no longer to authenticate the artifact. It was to get the evidence beyond the archive before the next audit erased it. The only person I knew to have a functioning transmitter was Mara Venn, an unauthorized broadcaster who worked in the old transit tunnels beneath the civic district. I had never met her in person; contacting an unregistered dissident was enough to earn a diagnosis. Still, the disk’s message had to reach someone who could make it impossible to bury.

I looked down. She was right.

“Temperature variance in the sublevel,” I said.

“Empathy can present as false environmental perception.”

For one reckless second, I imagined telling her everything: the light, the map, the proof that every forbidden feeling had once helped hold the world together. I imagined her expression changing, her carefully emptied face filling with astonishment. The image was so vivid it hurt.

**IF THE HEART IS SILENCED, CARRY THE SIGNAL.**

I memorized the route, closed my hand around the disk, and ran toward the tunnels before the regime could decide that wanting to save the truth was a symptom worth treating.

“That wasn’t an accusation.”

“I know.” I looked at the dark stain spreading through my sleeve. “That’s what makes it difficult.”

She did not ask me to explain. The Ministry called empathy a mental illness because it could not be scheduled, taxed, or contained. I had spent years helping turn that fear into policy. Now every instinct that pulled me toward another person felt like both a crime and a recovered sense.

Mara stopped at the nearest port. “Which one?”

I unfolded the intact portion of the strip beneath the beam of her lamp. The torn backing had left the edges ragged, but the authentication sequence remained legible. Beneath it, the printed fragments continued in broken lines: **ATTENTION COHERENCE—SHARED LOAD—CIVIC HEART ARRAY—ORIGIN ARCHIVE.** At the bottom was the municipal address we had followed through the inspection galleries.

“The port numbers correspond to the district trunks,” I said. “The emergency bridge opened the local distribution line, but it didn’t complete the handoff. We need to find the input terminal.”

“Can you tell which panel is active?”

“Not by looking.”

“Then we listen.”

She pressed her ear to the wall. I almost told her that was superstition, then noticed the faint vibration beneath the metal. It was not sound exactly. More like a pulse traveling through the copper, stopping and starting as if the network were trying to remember its own rhythm.

I moved along the gallery, dragging my fingertips over the painted panels. The first was cold. The second hummed weakly. The third gave a sharp static snap that made the hair rise along my wrist.

“There,” I said.

Mara joined me. A narrow maintenance hatch sat beneath the port, secured by two screws and a Ministry seal. The seal bore the familiar symbol of the Rational State: a single vertical line dividing a blank circle. No hands. No shared light. I took the receiver strip’s damaged backing from my pocket and slid its metal edge beneath the seal.

“Don’t ruin the rest of it,” Mara said.

“I’m not planning to.”

“Planning has had a difficult night.”

I pried carefully. The seal cracked, and the hatch opened onto a nest of insulated wires. One cable pulsed amber beneath its sheath. Another carried a faint blue current toward the clinic above. The third had been cut and capped with a suppression module.

“They’re filtering the line from inside the clinic,” I said. “Correction records, public receivers, everything passes through the same monitor.”

“Can we bypass it?”

“Not from here. The input terminal is farther down the gallery.”

The door behind us shuddered again, harder this time. Dust fell from the ceiling. A voice spoke through the metal, flattened by the service lock.

“Remain where you are. Emotional contamination is treatable. Resistance indicates impaired judgment.”

The words entered me with the force of a remembered signature. I had written versions of that sentence into arrest authorizations. I had believed that naming a person’s fear made it manageable. I had believed that calm belonged to the state because the state had the instruments to measure it.

Mara touched my shoulder. “We have to move.”

I nodded, but my body would not obey immediately. The gallery seemed to divide around me: one direction led toward the terminal and the chance to restore the original broadcast; the other led back to the door, to surrender, assessment, and the seductive certainty of official reassurance. If I surrendered, someone else would decide what the artifact meant. Someone else would decide whether my grief was evidence or pathology.

I wrapped the authentication strip around my uninjured wrist and tightened it like a bandage.

“Forward,” I said.

We ran.

The gallery narrowed beyond the opened hatch, forcing us between the wall and a row of rusted relay cabinets. Mara went first, turning sideways where the passage constricted. I followed, using my shoulder against the metal whenever my injured leg buckled. Behind us, the service door began to split at the lock. The sound chased us through the passage, each impact marking the distance the Ministry had left us.

At the far end, a circular chamber opened beneath the clinic’s foundation. Its floor was crowded with dormant terminals, and a single green lamp blinked above an unmarked console. I crossed the threshold, and the panel woke beneath my hand.

A line of text appeared.

**AUTHENTICATION REQUIRED. EMOTIONAL SOURCE IDENTIFICATION PENDING.**

Mara reached the chamber just as the gallery door burst inward behind us.

I placed the intact receiver strip against the console reader. The machine rejected it once, then again. On the third attempt, the green lamp turned amber and a new route illuminated across the panel—outward, beyond the clinic, toward the relay chamber and the public receivers.

I pressed the sequence into the reader with my bleeding hand.

The city answered through the walls.


The city answered through the walls with a vibration that traveled up through my boots and into the bones of my injured hand. Every relay cabinet in the circular chamber woke at once. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Green indicators kindled along the floor in a widening ring, and the route displayed on the console extended beyond the clinic into a branching map of the civic district.

Then the prompt changed.

**EMOTIONAL SOURCE IDENTIFICATION PENDING.**

I stared at the words. The machine was not asking who had entered the sequence. It was asking what had generated it.

Mara reached the console and caught my elbow before my knees gave way. “What does it want?”

“An origin.”

“Can you give it one?”

The gallery behind us rang with boots and shouted commands. A beam of white light cut through the broken service door, sweeping across the chamber’s curved wall. I felt the old reflex to flatten myself, to wait for authorized instructions, to let trained personnel determine whether my fear was proportionate. The Ministry had built obedience into the body and called the result health.

I looked at the receiver strip. Its torn edge rested against the reader, the surviving fragments trembling in the machine’s magnetic field.

“It wants an emotional source,” I said. “Not a name. A state of mind.”

Mara’s expression tightened. “You mean it won’t transmit facts unless we confess to feeling?”

“I mean the original system may have been designed to recognize intent. The Civic Heart Array was powered by shared attention. It needs proof that the signal is being sent for people, not merely at them.”

A voice boomed from the gallery. “Step away from the console. Emotional excitation has impaired your judgment. Compliance will begin restorative treatment.”

The phrase struck deeper than the threat. I had approved that language for clinic notices, softened its edges, and argued that patients were safer when their distress was translated into neutral terms. Now the state was using my own vocabulary to erase the reason I stood there.

Mara’s hand remained around my arm. “Tell it.”

I almost laughed. “Tell the console what?”

“The truth.”

The console pulsed. A small aperture opened beneath the prompt, revealing a shallow metal plate. I placed my palm against it. Pain burst through the cut, and the machine drew a thin line of light from my skin.

**SOURCE REQUIRED.**

I tried to summon a statement without feeling it. *The artifact contains historical evidence of pre-Republic infrastructure.* The words appeared in my mind as neatly as a catalog entry. The console remained dark.

Behind us, the service door groaned under another impact.

I thought of the voices carried through the shaft: workers describing turbines, children singing, hospitals drawing strength from attention freely given. I thought of the citizens whose records I had sealed because they had grieved too loudly, loved the wrong person, or recognized suffering in someone the Ministry had marked defective. My throat closed.

“I am afraid,” I said.

The console gave a low tone.

Mara did not move.

“I am afraid that if this stops with me, the Republic will turn the evidence into another diagnosis. I am afraid of being corrected until I can look at what we found and feel nothing. And I—” My voice broke. The old training urged me to pause, regulate, regain professional composure. Instead, I pressed harder against the plate. “I want them to know they were never sick for caring.”

The chamber filled with sound.

Not music. Not speech. A layered resonance rose from the walls, as though thousands of distant receivers had been connected to the same breath. The map on the console brightened. One route led toward the relay chamber. A second forked upward into the clinic’s public announcement grid. A third extended toward the street-level receivers.

**SOURCE ACCEPTED. SHARED LOAD RECOGNIZED.**

Mara leaned over the display. “There. The relay chamber.”

A red mark appeared at the end of the route.

**MANUAL HANDOFF REQUIRED.**

The service door burst inward. A figure in a gray Ministry shield stood beyond it, visor reflecting the console’s light. I could not see the person’s face, only the raised restraint device and the pale emblem stamped across the armor: a circle divided by a single line.

“Remove your hand,” the officer ordered. “You are experiencing empathic delusion.”

My palm was still locked to the plate. The console had begun transferring the archive, but a narrow progress bar crawled forward with agonizing slowness.

Mara pulled open a floor panel beside the terminal. Beneath it, a ladder descended into a shaft lit by the same amber pulse traveling through the city.

“The route is physical,” she said. “We take the handoff to the relay.”

“If I break contact—”

“You don’t. I’ll carry the strip.”

The officer stepped into the chamber. “Mara, your association with an emotionally contaminated historian confirms your susceptibility. Kneel for assessment.”

Hearing her name spoken by the Ministry made the danger suddenly solid. They had identified her, not merely tracked us. Yet Mara only reached for my coat and tucked the receiver strip securely inside the inner seam.

“Keep your hand on the plate,” she said. “Move when I move.”

The progress bar reached its first mark.

I tore my palm from the console. The chamber shrieked, but the map remained lit.

Mara dropped through the floor hatch. I followed, lowering myself onto the ladder as the restraint device discharged behind me. Blue light struck the console, splintering its casing. The city’s answering vibration faltered, then resumed below our feet.

I descended after Mara into the relay shaft, carrying the pain, the fear, and the accepted source of both. Above us, the officer shouted that empathy was an illness. Ahead, the amber route led toward the relay chamber and the public receivers, and the archive continued moving through the walls.


The ladder shook beneath another pair of boots before I had descended far enough to lose sight of the hatch. I kept my injured hand hooked around the rail and lowered myself one rung at a time. Blood made the metal slippery. Below me, Mara stopped on a narrow landing where the amber conduit bent into the wall.

“Three more,” she whispered. “Then step left.”

I counted because counting required nothing of me except obedience. On the third rung, my leg failed. My boot scraped empty air, and Mara caught the back of my coat, dragging me sideways onto the landing. I struck the wall shoulder first. The receiver strip crackled inside my coat.

Above us, a lamp beam moved down the shaft.

“Citizens,” the officer called. “Your distress has been recorded. Do not compound it.”

For one shameful instant I wanted to answer. Recorded meant witnessed; witnessed had once meant that someone would come to help. I had helped preserve that confusion in official histories, describing compulsory treatment as care until the words seemed interchangeable.

Mara pressed two fingers against my wrist.

“Can you stand?”

“Yes.”

“Then help me with this.”

A maintenance grille blocked the passage behind her. She had loosened one latch, but the other was painted shut. I wedged my good hand beneath its edge while she pulled. The grille gave with a squeal that seemed impossibly loud. We ducked through, and Mara eased it back into place rather than letting it fall.

The passage beyond was barely high enough to crouch in. Copper conduits crowded the ceiling, warm against my neck. Each amber pulse moved ahead of us, vanished around a bend, then returned as a dim reflection along the floor. The archive was still traveling. That fact steadied me more than any promise could have done.

We reached a junction marked by two arrows. Fresh Ministry lettering indicated **CLINICAL DISPOSAL ACCESS** to the right. Beneath scraped paint on the left, I found the lower half of an older word.

“Relay,” I said.

Mara lifted her lamp. “Certain?”

I traced the surviving letters without touching them. “The old municipal typeface. They used that narrow leg on the R only for infrastructure signs.”

She turned left. My useless, meticulous knowledge had become a direction someone could follow. I almost smiled, then caught myself suppressing it with the same reflex that made me hide my bleeding hand.

The passage ended at a wheel-operated door. Mara braced one foot against the frame and pulled. Nothing moved. I joined her, gripping with my uninjured hand, and together we forced the wheel through half a turn. The seal broke with a damp sucking sound.

We stepped down into the relay chamber.

Its cabinets rose in close rows, leaving only a central aisle. Amber light ran through glass channels beneath the floor. At the far end stood a waist-high desk with a receiving slot and a mechanical lever secured beneath a transparent cover. No guards. No welcoming voice. Just machinery waiting for an action we had not yet completed.

Mara shut the door and threaded a loose maintenance bar through the wheel.

“That won’t hold long.”

“It only has to hold while we find the handoff.”

At the desk, I took the strip from my coat. Blood had soaked its backing, but the printed sequence remained visible. Mara held its curled edges flat while I guided it into the slot.

The desk clicked.

**ORIGIN ARCHIVE AVAILABLE. RECEIVING OPERATOR REQUIRED.**

A second line appeared.

**LOCAL CLINICAL NETWORK CONNECTED. PUBLIC DISTRIBUTION UNCONFIRMED.**

Above the lettering, a small speaker hissed. An announcement filtered through: “Involuntary concern for unrelated persons is a reportable symptom. Remain available for assessment.”

My mouth went dry. We had reached the clinic network, but the connection to the street receivers remained unconfirmed. People confined upstairs might hear the archive first. They could speak, cry out, refuse their medication—and attendants would know exactly where to find them.

“Wait,” I said when Mara reached toward the lever.

She stopped.

“If we send this into the wards, they may punish anyone who responds.”

“Yes,” she said quietly.

“They won’t have chosen the risk.”

Mara rested her hands on the desk. “Then we tell them the risk. We don’t tell them how they have to feel.”

I looked at the announcement grid. All my working life I had removed testimony from public records because readers might find it destabilizing. Now I had a real danger to point to, and the familiar authority to decide for strangers was waiting in my hands.

“Can you put a warning before the evidence?”

“Once the handoff clears.”

I moved aside so she could reach the operator plate.

Mara placed her palm against it. The desk requested a spoken identifier, and she gave her full name.

“Mara Venn. Receiving for relay distribution.”

The strip advanced into the reader. Behind us, the maintenance bar struck the door with a hard metallic knock. Someone had reached the other side.

**HANDOFF ACCEPTED**, the desk displayed.

I lifted the cover over the lever for Mara. Beneath the floor, the amber channels brightened toward the public distribution cabinets; their indicators remained dark.

“Start with the warning,” I said.

She drew the microphone toward her as the door wheel began to turn against the bar.


Mara leaned toward the microphone. The wheel behind us turned another fraction, and the maintenance bar bowed in its brackets.

“To anyone receiving this transmission,” she said, her voice trembling only at the edges, “the material that follows is historical evidence. It may produce fear, grief, anger, or recognition. None of those responses is a crime, and none of you is required to act on them. If you are under clinical supervision, protect yourself first. Lower the volume if attendants are nearby. Do not identify yourself. Do not gather unless you have a safe route.”

The relay listened. A green line appeared beneath the warning, converting her words into a waveform.

I watched the public indicators. One remained black. Then another flickered amber.

“Include the source,” I said. “Tell them what powered the Array.”

Mara glanced at me. “You’re sure?”

“No.” The admission felt cleaner than certainty. “But they deserve the whole truth.”

The door shuddered. A bolt struck the outer face, and a strip of gray metal split along the seam. I reached for the lever. My injured hand protested, but the cover was already raised. Underneath, the handle waited like an old-fashioned signal arm, polished by generations of operators who had believed their work belonged to everyone.

Mara continued. “Before the Republic, the Civic Heart Array converted coordinated human attention into usable power. Not obedience. Not emotional suppression. Shared attention. People gathered to mourn, celebrate, teach, comfort, and witness one another. Those acts were measured, distributed, and transformed into energy for transit, hospitals, water systems, and public light.”

The waveform thickened. Across the console, dark receiver symbols began to illuminate.

One. Three. Seven.

A burst of static interrupted her. Beneath it came a clipped Ministry voice: “Unauthorized affective content detected. Listeners are advised that empathy-based cognition indicates treatable impairment.”

Mara’s mouth tightened, but she did not answer the voice. She looked at me instead.

“Keep going,” I said.

The door wheel spun. The bar screamed against the brackets.

I pulled the lever down.

For a breath, nothing changed. Then the relay chamber filled with a deep, rising hum. Amber light surged through the floor channels and climbed the cabinets in branching veins. The receiver strip vanished into the desk, drawn toward machinery older than the Republic’s first decree. Somewhere beyond the chamber, relays engaged in sequence, each one answering the last.

The public indicators turned green.

Mara’s warning went out again, stronger this time, carried beneath the archive’s opening tones. I heard fragments through the chamber speaker: her description of the Array, my recorded testimony from the archive, the names of forgotten systems that had once depended on care rather than punished it.

The door burst inward.

The Ministry officer shoved through the gap, one shoulder wedged past the bent frame. The restraint device in their hand glowed blue. “Step away from the relay.”

I froze. The officer’s visor reflected my face in broken pieces: pale, bloodied, frightened, still capable of recognizing another person as a person. The Ministry had taught me that recognition was contamination when it crossed the approved boundary. It had called empathy a mental illness because illness could be isolated, treated, and made to apologize.

Mara seized the microphone cable and yanked it free. The broadcast stuttered but did not stop; the desk had taken control of the signal.

“Now,” she said.

She kicked open a narrow panel beneath the console. Behind it, a service chute dropped toward the lower distribution galleries. I grabbed the edge and lowered myself through. My injured leg struck the metal lip, sending pain through my hip, but I kept moving. Mara followed as the officer fired. Blue light scorched the console above us.

The panel slammed shut. Darkness swallowed the chamber, leaving only the amber pulse beneath the chute grate. I landed on a sloped maintenance passage and nearly fell. Mara caught my coat, then pulled me toward a low opening marked with a hand-painted arrow.

“Where does that go?” I whispered.

“Street receiver junction.”

The passage tilted downward. Behind us, metal buckled and boots entered the chute. Ahead, a faint wash of ordinary city light appeared through the grate. I crawled toward it, dragging my injured hand over dust and cable insulation.

At the junction, Mara forced the grate upward. Cold air rushed in carrying rain, exhaust, and the distant murmur of people stopping in the street. Above us, public receiver horns had begun to speak. Her warning traveled through the district, followed by the archive’s first recovered voices.

I climbed out into the service court. Across the buildings, windows opened one by one. No alarm sounded yet. No Ministry announcement drowned the transmission. The city was listening.

Mara emerged beside me, breathing hard. “They heard it.”

I looked back at the dark grate as blue light flashed below. Then I faced the street, where strangers stood beneath the receivers, uncertain and unshielded.

“They can decide what it means,” I said.

And with the archive moving through every lit receiver, we ran.
