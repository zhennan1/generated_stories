## Chapter 1: The Corridor of Static

Adam Khalil knew the city by what it did to his feet.

The cracked pavement outside his apartment shivered whenever a bus passed on the avenue. Water pumps trembled behind plaster walls. A generator coughed in the alley below, its rhythm traveling up through the soles of his boots. He had learned to read the neighborhood through vibration: the bakery door slammed in the morning; the old woman on the third floor dragged her chair across the balcony; children played football against the wall beside the mosque.

None of it made a sound.

Ten years of deafness had not made silence peaceful. Peace suggested a choice. Adam's silence was a sealed room into which the world still hurled objects. He saw mouths open and close behind glass. He felt traffic through the ground. He watched pigeons scatter from rooftops and imagined the wingbeats, though imagination had begun to lose its edges.

He shaved by touch, buttoned his shirt by habit, and checked the hospital appointment card beneath his wallet. A routine examination, the card said. Blood pressure. Balance. The doctor wanted to look at the scar behind his left ear, though every doctor who looked at it eventually shrugged.

Nothing physically wrong, they had told him for years.

Nothing that could be repaired.

Outside, the city leaned into another hot morning. Dust filmed the parked cars. A fruit seller arranged bruised oranges beneath a striped awning while two soldiers at a checkpoint examined papers. Adam passed without being stopped. He kept his face empty, his hands visible, his eyes moving.

At the hospital, taxis clogged the entrance. The building had once been white. Now soot and rain streaks made it the color of old bone. Several windows were covered with cardboard. A plastic sheet hung over the emergency doors, lifting and settling in the wind like a tired lung.

The lobby was crowded enough to become dangerous. People moved without regard for one another, and Adam watched every shoulder, every swinging elbow. A woman spoke rapidly to a clerk behind a cloudy window. The clerk pointed toward a staircase. A boy with a bandaged hand leaned against his father. An orderly pushed a bed through the crowd, the patient beneath the sheet perfectly still.

Adam felt the bed's wheels rattle through the floor before he saw it. He stepped aside.

At registration, he produced his card and tapped deaf and appointment on his phone. The clerk glanced at him, then began speaking. Adam pointed to his ear. The clerk exaggerated his mouth, forming words like a child performing for a foreigner.

“Third floor,” Adam read.

He nodded and moved away before gratitude could be demanded of him.

The stairwell smelled of bleach, sweat, and something medicinally sweet. On the second landing, a nurse brushed past, her lips moving around an apology Adam did not catch. He touched the railing. Beneath his palm, the hospital pulsed with hidden machinery.

On the third floor, the corridor was packed. Patients sat shoulder to shoulder on metal chairs. Relatives stood against the walls, clutching folders, bottles, sleeping children. Fluorescent tubes flickered overhead, dividing the corridor into photographs.

He found a place near a cracked pillar and waited.

Waiting was the skill war had left him. Waiting, watching, translating what men meant when they said one thing and prepared another. Before the blast, he had translated between soldiers and villagers, between orders and their consequences. He had believed language could create a narrow bridge across any distance.

Then the bridge had collapsed.

A doctor called his name. Adam saw it on the doctor's lips before the soundless announcement reached no one he could hear. He stood, collected his file, and followed the white coat into a room with peeling paint.

The examination took nine minutes. The doctor shone a light in his eyes, tested his balance, pressed behind his ear, and asked questions Adam answered by writing.

Any change?

No.

Headaches?

Sometimes.

Ringing?

Adam almost smiled. He wrote: I am deaf.

The doctor frowned at the joke, not understanding it, and wrote a referral for another scan. Adam folded the paper and left.

The corridor had grown more crowded. A child in a red sweater was crying beside the elevators, mouth wide, face bright with tears. Adam watched the mother bounce him against her hip. He could not hear the child's distress, but felt it in his chest as faint pressure. He looked away.

Someone touched his shoulder.

Adam turned sharply.

A man stood close behind him. Younger than Adam by perhaps five years, though hardship had pulled his face tight. He wore a dark jacket despite the heat. His hair was cut short. A white scar began beneath his right ear and ran forward along his jaw, disappearing into rough stubble.

The man did not apologize. He looked directly at Adam.

For one suspended moment, Adam saw recognition in his eyes. Not familiarity. Recognition—the terrible certainty of someone who had found what he was hunting.

Then the man leaned in.

“Barqa,” he whispered.

The word entered Adam like a bullet.

Sound exploded.

The fluorescent lights shrieked above him, a swarm of electric insects. The elevator doors groaned open. Shoes struck the floor from every direction: rubber soles, leather heels, hurried sandals. A monitor somewhere beyond the corridor gave a high, regular beep. The child screamed, and the scream cut through Adam's teeth.

He staggered backward.

The stranger was already moving. Adam caught a glimpse of the scar, the dark jacket, one hand pushing through the crowd. He reached for him, but a woman stepped between them. Bodies closed like water.

“What—” Adam said.

His own voice frightened him. It was hoarse, enormous, a stranger speaking from inside his skull.

The corridor surged with noise. A telephone rang. Someone shouted for a nurse. A cart rattled. Adam heard the scrape of his breath and the frantic hammering of his heart. Every sound arrived without distance or mercy, stacked upon every other sound until the world became a wall collapsing inward.

He dropped the file.

The floor rushed up. His knees struck tile. Hands seized his shoulders. Faces bent over him, mouths opening and closing. The child kept crying. The lights buzzed. Somewhere, a woman said, “Sir, can you hear me?”

Yes, Adam thought.

Yes. Stop.

He tried to cover his ears, but the noise was inside them.

Then darkness took him.

When he opened his eyes, he was in a curtained examination bay. A doctor stood at the foot of the bed, speaking slowly while a nurse wrote on a clipboard. Adam could hear the pen scratching. The sound was delicate and unbearable.

“Do you know where you are?” the doctor asked.

Adam nodded.

“Can you hear me?”

He nodded again.

They tested him for stroke, seizure, infection, intoxication. They shone lights, drew blood, pressed electrodes against his scalp. No bleeding. No fever. No obvious neurological cause. The doctor kept asking when his hearing had returned.

Adam wrote: A man spoke to me.

What did he say?

Adam stared at the question.

Barqa.

He wrote the word and stopped. He could not explain that it was not merely a village. It was a locked door in his mind, a place whose dust he had spent ten years refusing to remember.

By evening, the sounds thinned. The hospital's clatter receded, as though a tide were drawing away. The last thing Adam heard was the nurse closing the curtain.

Then silence returned.

He sat alone on the edge of the bed, breathing into it.

Outside, the city moved beneath the dark. He could not hear its engines or prayers or arguments. Yet the word remained, bright and burning.

Barqa.

A name only the dead men of his squad were supposed to know.

## Chapter 2: The Dead Man's Room

By morning, Adam had almost persuaded himself that the voice had been a seizure, some private electrical failure. Then he remembered the stranger's hand on his shoulder. That touch had come before the sound.

He returned to the hospital with yesterday's referral folded in his pocket. In the stairwell, a cleaner dragged a wet mop over the steps. Adam waited for her to look up before passing; he needed to know she had seen him. It was one of the small negotiations by which he moved through the world.

The third-floor corridor was nearly empty. Without the crowd, he could see patches where chairs had rubbed the paint down to plaster. A paper cup stood beneath a leaking pipe. Here was the pillar. Here was the place his knees had struck.

Above the elevators, a security camera pointed toward the wall.

At the nurses' station, he typed a question on his phone and held it out. The nurse reading it wore a cardigan over her uniform. She looked at him, then at the corridor, and recognition tightened her face.

She began speaking. He touched his ear and offered the phone again.

She typed with one finger: Cameras haven't worked since winter. You need to ask downstairs.

He found a blank corner of his referral and drew a face, adding the scar along the right jaw. Under it he wrote: Dark jacket. Yesterday. Before I fell.

The nurse studied the drawing longer than its quality deserved. She glanced toward the far end of the corridor.

312, she wrote. Visiting Mansour. I thought he was family.

Adam pointed down the hall.

Her hand closed briefly over the paper. She added: Rashid Mansour died last night. They haven't cleared the bed yet.

For a moment he could think only of timing: a man passing through, a word spoken, another man dying behind a door.

He wrote, Was the visitor here when he died?

She shook her head. Someone behind Adam raised a bag of fluid toward her. She returned his phone and went to work.

Room 312 was open. Adam stopped outside as though consulting his referral, then stepped in.

Two beds stood against opposite walls. One held a sleeping man with a yellow blanket tucked beneath his chin. The other was bare except for a striped mattress and an abandoned pair of slippers beneath it. A rectangle of cleaner paint marked where a picture had hung.

Adam checked the empty bedside cabinet. A cracked comb, tissues, a plastic spoon. Nothing that explained why a stranger had carried Barqa out of this room.

He felt ashamed. He had become the sort of man who searched a dead patient's furniture while another patient slept a few feet away.

Still, he lifted the mattress.

A black notebook lay between it and the bed frame, its cloth cover worn pale at the corners. He drew it out and opened it against his palm.

Dates filled narrow columns. Names, or fragments of names, sat beside numbers and crossed-out initials. A folded sheet made a lump near the back. Halfway through, he found Barqa written three times, the last repetition pressed so hard it had torn the paper.

Inside the cover was a small printed emblem: three slanting blue lines above the words MERIDIAN SOLUTIONS.

A movement cut across the doorway.

The woman standing there held a clear bag containing a man's shirt. A hospital identification card hung from her neck. She looked from the lifted mattress to the notebook.

Adam let the mattress fall. He held the book out, suddenly unable to offer anything but the evidence against himself.

Her mouth moved too quickly.

He shook his head and pointed to his ear. Anger sharpened her face; then she looked at the referral in his other hand. She put the bag down and took his pen.

What are you doing with my father's things?

He read it twice.

I'm sorry, he wrote. A man visited him yesterday. Scar on his jaw. He spoke to me. I heard him.

She stared at the last sentence.

I've been deaf ten years.

She took the paper back. What did he say?

Adam hesitated, then turned the notebook to the torn page.

The woman sat on the empty bed.

For several seconds she kept her eyes on the word. When she reached for the pen, her hand shook.

My father said that before he died.

She printed her name beneath it: Nadia Mansour. Records office, ground floor.

Adam pulled a chair around so he could see her face. She spoke slowly now, letting him follow what he could, writing when he lost her.

Her father had been a field medic. He rarely spoke about those years. He slept with a lamp on. Sometimes Nadia found him washing hands that were already clean.

“I thought it was a person's name,” she said, and wrote the sentence afterward to be certain.

“A village,” Adam told her.

She waited. He had nothing else he could give.

He expected her to call someone. Instead she turned through the notebook, touching the margins rather than the writing.

Take it, she wrote at last. Find out why he was frightened.

He asked about the visitor. She had not seen him, but she could try the visitor log. She gave Adam her number. Before he left, she made him send a message so she would have his.

Outside, afternoon light lay white on the shuttered shops. Adam took the backstreets home, the notebook held beneath his shirt.

At the first turning, someone said his name.

“Adam.”

He spun around. A delivery rider passed with his mouth closed. Two boys leaned over a broken phone.

Then a siren sounded, impossibly distant. Adam felt no approaching vehicle through his soles. He pressed a hand to the wall and listened until it vanished.

Farther down the alley came a woman's weeping: one broken breath, then another.

He searched the balconies, the dark windows, the recessed doors.

He could not find her. He stood there long after he could hear nothing again.

## Chapter 3: Barqa

Adam locked his door and rested his forehead against it. In the courtyard below, his landlord's wife was shaking a rug over the rail. Dust floated past the stairwell window. He watched it settle without hearing the blows that had sent it into the air.

Inside, he put Rashid's notebook on the kitchen table, beside a bowl with a crack through its glaze. He washed his hands, then caught himself looking at them.

He dried them carefully.

The notebook resisted reading. Rashid had written in Arabic and English, sometimes changing languages halfway through a line. Adam found abbreviations for dosages, grid references, dates without years. He took a pencil from a drawer and began copying the entries onto the backs of unpaid bills.

The work steadied him. Before the war, before silence, a page had been something he could enter and put in order.

A repeated set of initials resolved into ranks. The numbers beside them were service numbers, not quantities. He recognized the format of his own before he found his name: A. Khalil, translator. Beside it Rashid had drawn a small open circle.

Several names had crosses.

He laid the pencil down.

The folded sheet near the back opened into a crude map. A road entered a village from the south and forked at a square. Small rectangles clustered around a well. Beyond them stood a long building shaded with diagonal strokes. Barqa was written along the upper edge.

His finger stopped at the square.

Dust blew across it. Not the courtyard dust, pale and domestic, but a brown cloud thick enough to taste. A line of people stood with their hands empty at their sides. Someone was speaking behind Adam, impatient with him.

He jerked his hand away.

The table returned. The bowl. The dead refrigerator with its door held shut by a strip of tape.

Beneath the map lay a photograph, its glossy face stuck lightly to the paper. He eased it free. Six soldiers and a man carrying a medical bag stood in front of a truck. Adam recognized himself at the edge, younger and narrower, squinting into sunlight. He could not remember the photograph being taken.

He turned it over. There were surnames in Rashid's handwriting, arranged to match the men. Khalil. Haddad. Two others he had known once, or should have known.

Haddad stood beside him, one hand hooked into a shoulder strap. His face was unscarred. Adam studied the straight nose, the heavy eyebrows, the slight downward pull at the left corner of the mouth.

He reached for his pencil.

Its tip scratched across the bill.

He dropped it so abruptly that it rolled under the table.

The sound had been small, dry, unmistakable. He retrieved the pencil and tried again. Nothing. He pressed harder until the point broke.

Pain began behind his right eye.

He worked until the light failed. Meridian Solutions appeared on a page headed Transport, again beside a sequence of transfers, again beneath a date ten years old. He circled the company name each time. Whatever Rashid had been recording, the emblem inside the cover was not an advertisement accidentally preserved.

At the bottom of the soldiers' list, he found the full name: Jawad Haddad. A cross beside it. The same operation date as the others.

He knew that name. He had seen it on the casualty list sent with his discharge papers. In the hospital afterward, he had read the dead men's names over and over, waiting for the names to bring back their faces.

Now he held one.

He slept badly and left early for the military cemetery.

Beyond its gate, the city continued climbing around the dead: unfinished apartment blocks, satellite dishes, a crane that had not moved in months. Adam showed the caretaker the name on his phone. The old man spoke, saw Adam's incomprehension, and pointed him toward the western rows.

Adam walked between stones whitened by sun. Some carried photographs; some had only names and dates. He found Jawad beneath a dusty cypress.

JAWAD HADDAD.

The date matched Rashid's list.

Below it, a short inscription praised sacrifice without saying what had been sacrificed, or by whom.

Adam crouched. He took the photograph from the notebook and held it beside the stone. With his thumb he covered the strip of face beneath the younger man's right ear, tracing where a scar might run toward the chin.

The hospital corridor returned with a clarity that made him nauseous. The stranger turning toward him. That same mouth shaping the word.

Barqa.

“Jawad,” Adam said.

He felt the name leave his throat but did not hear it.

This was not a resemblance he could dismiss. The man who had touched him in the corridor had stood beside him in a war. Here was the official end of that man's life, cut neatly into stone.

A child's face rose between the photograph and the grave: hair flattened with sweat, eyes fixed on something beyond Adam's shoulder. A sharp burst of gunfire followed, too near, and he ducked, one hand striking the earth.

Nothing moved among the graves.

He could not tell whether he had heard the shots or remembered hearing them.

Then, from beyond the cemetery wall, a call to prayer began. A thin voice climbed through the morning. Adam held still. He had known those intervals all his childhood, yet now he waited for each note as if it might injure him.

The voice cut off halfway through a phrase. A bird continued opening its beak in the cypress, soundlessly.

Pain tightened behind both eyes. He bent over his knees until the nausea eased. Beneath his palm, pressed against his chest, his heart struck once, twice, suddenly loud. He took his hand away. The beating remained for three more blows, then vanished too.

He wanted someone to sit beside him without asking anything. For a moment he nearly messaged Nadia that he was frightened.

Instead he photographed the headstone and sent it to her with a picture of the soldier.

I think this is your father's visitor. Jawad Haddad. Please keep looking for the name he used.

He put the notebook inside his jacket and stood slowly. The migraine made the white stones hard to look at.

At the gate he turned back. He had spent ten years believing there was nobody left to ask.

Somewhere beyond this cemetery, a dead man knew where to find him.

## Chapter 4: Shadowed Backstreets

Nadia answered before Adam reached the cemetery gate.

Her message came with a photograph of the hospital visitor log. Adam enlarged it until the letters blurred.

RASHID MANSOUR—ROOM 312.

Below it, in another hand:

SAMIR NASSER. VISIT—

The page had been cut off. Nadia had circled the name. He signed for a package two nights before my father died. The guard remembers the scar.

Adam opened the notebook and sent her a photograph of a faded purple stamp beneath a fold of the inside cover. Can you read this?

Her reply identified it: AL-NAHDA PHARMACY, OLD QUARTER. The street number had faded.

Adam typed, I am going there.

Don’t go alone, Nadia replied.

He put the phone away.

By noon a taxi left him beneath an awning where laundry hung over the old quarter like flags. Stone balconies leaned toward one another, shutting out the sun. Broken tiles paved the alleys; water leaked from pipes into black gutters. Signs painted on walls led him deeper: a green cross, an arrow, half a word in Arabic.

Al-Nahda Pharmacy occupied a wedge between a locksmith and a shop selling chargers. Its sign had lost two letters. Inside, shelves crowded with faded boxes and dusty bottles.

The old pharmacist looked at Adam’s phone, then at the name Samir Nasser. His expression changed. He pointed toward the back.

Adam wrote, Where is he?

The pharmacist shook his head, tapped two fingers against his lips, and wrote an address on cardboard: behind the bathhouse, second alley, blue door.

On the counter, a delivery book lay beneath a jar of cotton. The same purple stamp marked several pages. Beside one shipment, in tiny print, were the words: Meridian Solutions—sedatives, dressings, syringes.

Adam’s stomach tightened.

He found the bathhouse by its cracked dome and the smell of boiled water. The second alley narrowed until his shoulders nearly brushed both walls. A cat slipped over a drain. At its end stood the blue door.

Adam reached for it.

An arm closed around his throat.

His back struck the wall. A second man stepped from a recess and blocked the alley. They wore cheap shirts and dusty trousers. The first smelled of cigarettes.

He spoke close to Adam’s face. Adam read the demand rather than heard it.

The notebook.

Adam shook his head.

The fist to his stomach emptied his lungs. While he folded, the second man searched his pockets: wallet, phone, referral. The notebook remained under his shirt.

“Where is it?” the first man said.

Adam saw his teeth move and felt only stone against his skull. He drove his heel into the man’s knee. The grip loosened. The second man caught his wrist and slammed it against the wall.

Then the blue door opened behind them.

The stranger moved without haste. He caught the second man’s elbow, turned it until the man struck the doorframe, and drove two fingers beneath his jaw. The man collapsed, gagging.

The first drew a knife.

The stranger stepped inside its arc, seized the wrist, struck the forearm, and folded him onto the tiles. The knife clattered down.

Adam heard it—a bright metallic note, impossibly near.

The stranger looked at him. Not Samir Nasser. Not the dead man in the cemetery.

Jawad.

“Move,” he said.

This time Adam heard the word as a ragged thread. He stumbled after him through the door into a courtyard filled with broken furniture. Jawad kicked open a rear gate and pulled him into another alley.

They ran beneath balconies, past a woman clutching a child, past a man who lowered his eyes. Every footfall struck inside Adam’s skull.

“Wait!”

Jawad did not slow until they emerged beside a shuttered bakery. He turned, breathing hard. Up close, the scar began beneath his ear and traveled jaggedly toward his chin.

Adam touched it.

Jawad let him.

“You don’t remember me,” Jawad said. His voice shook. “That’s what they wanted.”

“Who?”

“But I need you to remember.”

“Barqa. What happened there? Why are you alive?”

For an instant something softened in Jawad’s face. Then it closed.

A motorcycle appeared at the far end of the street. Jawad shoved Adam behind crates and disappeared through a doorway.

Adam followed, but the room beyond was empty. A curtain stirred. Behind it was only a passage leading to a courtyard wall too high to climb.

He returned to the bakery alley. The two men were still in the passage. One groaned. The other began to move.

Sound broke over Adam like a flood.

The groans became wet and furious. Footsteps slapped the stones. A door banged. The motorcycle screamed away. His own breath came in ragged bursts, and beneath it his heart hammered so loudly he thought it might tear through his ribs.

He pressed his hands to his ears. It made no difference.

For ten years silence had been a wall. Now the wall had opened, and behind it waited everything he had lost.

Adam bent over the gutter and vomited. When he looked up, Jawad was gone.

The stranger was not his enemy. He knew that now. He knew nothing else—not where Jawad had been, who had sent the men, or what memory would demand.

Somewhere in the shadowed city, a dead man was running. Adam had finally begun to hear him.

## Chapter 5: The Second Voice

Adam woke beneath a ceiling crossed with cracks. For a moment he mistook them for the streets of Barqa, the crooked lines in Rashid’s notebook. Then a hand tightened around his wrist.

He jerked upright.

Jawad sat beside the mattress, holding a strip of gauze. A basin stood between his boots. The water in it was pink.

“Stay still.”

Adam saw the words without hearing them. Silence had returned, though a thin pressure remained behind his eyes. His left sleeve was rolled above an abrasion where the men had dragged his arm against the wall.

He pulled away. “Where?”

Jawad pointed to the floor, then spoke again, keeping his face toward the window. “Above a tailor’s. The shop is closed. We’re safe for now.”

There were paper patterns on a shelf, a treadle sewing machine beneath a sheet, a kettle on an unlit burner. Through the shutters Adam could see a strip of evening sky and electrical cables sagging between buildings.

“You left.”

“I drew the motorcycle away. When I came back, you were on the ground by the bakery.”

“How did you get me here?”

“Slowly.” Jawad held up the gauze. After a moment Adam offered his arm.

His shirt lay open. The notebook rested beside the mattress, with his phone, wallet, referral, and the photograph. He counted them before he could stop himself.

“I didn’t take anything,” Jawad said.

Adam picked up the phone. A message from Nadia filled the screen: Are you home?

He typed with one thumb. Alive. With Jawad. Will explain when I can.

He did not know whether that would comfort her.

Jawad tied the bandage. His hands were steady, but the flesh beneath his right eye twitched. Without the urgency of the alleys, he looked older than Adam had expected a survivor to look.

“Tell me,” Adam said. “No more disappearing.”

Jawad sat back so Adam could read his lips.

“We served together. You translated. I drove, carried a rifle, did what I was told. Until Barqa.”

“I know we served together.” Adam lifted the photograph. “I found your grave.”

Something moved in Jawad’s face.

“Is my father’s name on it?”

Adam nodded.

Jawad looked down. Adam waited until he looked up again.

“Meridian Solutions took command in the village,” Jawad said. “Not our officers. Contractors. They ordered civilians killed.”

Adam watched the mouth form the next words.

“You translated the order.”

“No.”

“You put it into words the soldiers understood.”

“No.” Adam stood too quickly. The room tilted. He caught the shelf and sent a paper pattern sliding to the floor. “I translated checkpoints. Interviews. Supply lists.”

“You translated that too.”

Outside, someone crossed the narrow strip of visible sky on a balcony. Adam watched a shirt billow from the person’s hands. Such an ordinary gesture, performed while this accusation occupied the room.

“Why would I forget?”

“Rashid Mansour. He was the medic with us. There were injections during the withdrawal. We were told they were for shock.”

Adam looked at the black notebook.

“What were they?”

“Something to suppress memory. Rashid told me afterward, when I found him. He said he’d been given orders.”

“His daughter doesn’t know.”

“I don’t know what she knows.”

“You’re saying a drug erased a village?”

“Not erased. You’re here, asking about it.”

Adam pressed his knuckles against his brow. He remembered the hospital doctor turning his head toward a light, testing a body that had offered no explanation. Nothing about Jawad’s account explained why a fallen knife could suddenly ring inside him.

“And my ears?”

“A roadside bomb hit our truck as we tried to get out. That was the last time I saw you.”

“Until the hospital.”

Jawad nodded.

“Where were you for ten years?”

“Some of them in rooms I couldn’t leave.” He touched the edge of the basin with his boot. “Meridian took me after the blast. They wanted to know who else had seen. Who had refused. They worked on me until they thought there was nothing useful left.”

He paused, and Adam had to wait for his mouth to move again.

“They left me outside a compound with bodies. At night I crawled away.”

Adam looked at the white seam along his jaw. In the alley he had touched it to establish something simple: flesh, not a photograph, not a dead man’s apparition. Now Jawad was asking him to accept everything that flesh had endured.

“You could have learned my name from Rashid,” Adam said. “Found the photograph. Followed me.”

“Yes.”

The answer unsettled him more than an argument.

“I can tell you,” Jawad continued. “I can’t do the remembering for you.”

Adam knelt beside the chair. He reached out, then stopped. Jawad gave a small nod.

This time his fingers rested where the scar pulled at the corner of Jawad’s mouth. Beneath the rough ridge the jaw was warm. Jawad turned slightly toward his hand.

A truck burned.

Not a complete scene. A door hanging open. Black smoke flattening beneath a pale sky. Jawad’s face behind the broken window, blood bright along his jaw, one hand reaching through.

Adam’s own boots were moving backward.

Then turning.

He tried to hold the image still, to find the part in which he went back. Instead he felt the terrible ease of running once he had begun, the ground coming under his feet, the truck falling behind him.

He took his hand away.

“I left you.”

He heard himself say it.

The second voice was his own, cracked and unfamiliar. Then came the scrape of Jawad’s chair, water shifting in the basin, a horn in the street below. Scissors fell somewhere downstairs. Their impact split the room open.

Adam clapped his hands over his ears. Sound drove through his palms: pipes knocking, a vendor calling, fabric snapping on the balcony. Each noise arrived whole, without distance or mercy.

Jawad caught him before his head struck the shelf.

His mouth moved close to Adam’s face, and now Adam could hear him too.

“I have you.”

Adam shut his eyes. The burning truck remained. He could no longer tell which world he was trying to escape.

## Chapter 6: Meridian

By morning, sound had become a tenant in Adam’s skull.

It did not speak continuously. It clicked, dripped, scraped, and breathed. The tailor shop below exhaled pipes and settling wood. A pigeon struck the shutter once and sent a bright nail through his head. When Jawad poured water into a cup, Adam flinched before the stream began.

Jawad stopped with the kettle tilted.

“Don’t,” Adam said.

Jawad set it down. He had learned to keep his mouth visible even when he was silent. That courtesy made Adam want to strike him.

Nadia arrived after sunrise wearing the hospital’s gray cardigan over street clothes. She came up the back stairs, glanced at Jawad’s pistol, and closed the door behind her.

“You said you would explain,” she told Adam.

“I did.”

“Your explanation was that you were alive and with a man listed as dead.”

Jawad moved away from the window. Nadia looked at him for a long moment, then at the scar.

“Samir Nasser,” she said.

“Jawad Haddad,” he answered.

“I know who you are.” Her voice reached Adam clearly. It was softer than the hospital’s voices, but every syllable had an edge. “My father wrote your name.”

She placed a thin folder on the table. The papers smelled of dust and disinfectant.

“Rashid wasn’t only employed by the hospital,” she said. “I searched the archived contracts after you sent me the photograph. Ten years ago he worked as a field medic for Meridian Solutions. Their insurance records call him a consultant. Their payroll calls him a contractor.”

Adam opened the folder. A stamped copy showed Rashid’s signature beneath a Meridian emblem. His stomach tightened.

“Why didn’t he tell you?”

“He told me nothing useful.” Nadia took out a ledger page, its columns cramped with dates and initials. “But he kept this in a locked records box. I found it behind the old radiology files.”

Jawad leaned over the table.

“Names?”

“Soldiers,” Nadia said. “Some marked dead. Some marked transferred. Beside them are words like compliant, unstable, no recall.”

Adam touched the page. The paper trembled—not from his hand, he thought, but from a low electrical hum in the walls.

“Silenced soldiers,” Jawad said.

“Or patients. Or witnesses.” Nadia folded the page back into the folder. “There is an address repeated beside the Meridian stamp. A front office beyond the eastern freight yards. It was closed years ago, but the city registry still lists the company there.”

Adam heard a truck change gears three streets away. He pressed two fingers beneath his ear until the pain dulled.

Jawad noticed. “We go today.”

“To a locked office?” Adam asked. “With what? Your dead man’s authority?”

“With evidence.”

“You mean revenge.”

Jawad’s face hardened, but he did not deny it.

Adam had stored his old equipment in a municipal locker beneath the railway station, a place he had not visited since leaving the army. He remembered a recorder among the cables and dead batteries: a cheap digital device issued to translators who needed a record of disputed orders. He had kept it without knowing why.

Nadia drove them in a borrowed hospital van. Adam sat in back with the folder, while Jawad watched the streets through the rear window. The city passed in layers of blown dust, blue plastic awnings, and walls carrying three generations of political posters. Every horn struck Adam as if it had been sounded inside his chest.

At the station, the locker key resisted. When the door opened, a smell of rust and old paper rose out.

The recorder lay beneath a wool scarf.

Adam held it in both hands. The casing was scratched, its tiny screen blank. He pressed the power button.

Nothing happened.

Jawad found a battery in the van’s emergency kit. Nadia watched while Adam fitted it. The device clicked on.

A file appeared without a date.

BARQA—TRANSLATION.

Adam could not breathe.

“Play it,” Jawad said.

“No.”

“Adam.”

“I said no.”

He slid the recorder into his pocket. The sound in his skull had become a swarm. Nadia’s breathing. Jawad’s boot shifting on concrete. A train grinding somewhere beyond the station wall.

Then the hearing flared.

His own voice filled the locker room, thin beneath static: “All civilians are to gather at the square. Repeat, all civilians are to gather at the square.”

A man answered in English, close to the microphone. “No witnesses outside the perimeter.”

Adam knew that voice only as a shape in memory: calm, impatient, accustomed to obedience.

Then his translation: “No one is to leave.”

Gunfire erupted.

Nadia grabbed his shoulder. The recorder fell against his palm, still speaking. A child screamed once. Someone shouted Jawad’s name. Adam heard his younger self say, “We have families here,” and the contractor’s voice reply, “Translate the order.”

The file ended in a burst of static.

Adam bent over, vomiting onto the concrete.

Jawad crouched beside him but did not touch him.

“You heard it,” he said.

“I made them understand.” Adam wiped his mouth. “I carried the order.”

“You can make them hear it now.”

The eastern office occupied a low concrete building behind a chain-link fence. Its sign had been removed, but four pale screw marks remained above the door. Through a broken window Adam saw empty desks, a dead computer, and a Meridian logo half-hidden beneath peeling paint.

Nadia photographed the entrance. Jawad checked the alley.

A white sedan turned slowly onto the street.

“Inside,” Jawad said.

They moved toward the side door. The sedan stopped. Two men stepped out. One wore a hospital maintenance jacket. The other held a phone and looked directly at Nadia.

Adam heard the phone vibrate before the man raised it. He heard Jawad draw his weapon. He heard Nadia whisper, “They followed me.”

The men crossed the road.

Jawad shoved Nadia through the door. One attacker seized her cardigan and pulled her backward. Adam caught her wrist. The sound of fabric tearing ripped through him.

He struck the man with the recorder.

The man staggered. Jawad slammed him against the wall, while the second fled toward the sedan. Tires screamed. Adam heard every footfall, every ragged breath, every small animal panic in Nadia’s throat.

Inside the office, the silence was worse than noise. It held the recording between them.

Jawad picked up the recorder and offered it back.

“Trust me once,” he said. “Not forever. Just long enough to expose them.”

Adam looked at the scar along his jaw, then at Nadia’s torn cardigan.

He took the recorder.

A distant siren began to rise, and pain opened behind his eyes like a door.

## Chapter 7: The Day We Left

They left the office by the yard behind it, passing between stripped delivery vans. Jawad would not let them use the hospital vehicle again. Nadia removed its keys from her pocket and stared at them before putting them away.

Two taxis and a long walk brought them back to the shuttered tailor shop. Adam remembered almost nothing of the journey except a driver's loose bracelet clicking against the steering wheel. Each click had seemed to enter through his teeth.

Upstairs, Nadia drew the curtains. Jawad set a chair against the door, then checked the back window.

Adam laid the recorder beside the mattress.

“Don't play it again,” Nadia said.

He had not meant to. His thumb was already on the button.

He moved his hand away and lay down in his clothes. Somewhere behind the wall, water struck metal at uneven intervals. He waited for the next drop, unable to stop waiting.

Then the interval filled with another sound: a spoon knocking against a glass.

Ten years earlier, Jawad had stirred too much sugar into his tea.

“Keep that thing away from me,” he said, nodding at Adam's recorder. “My voice sounds like my uncle's on it.”

They were in the truck outside Barqa. Dawn lay low across the fields. Their lieutenant stood beside a second vehicle, listening to a contractor in clean boots. The tip had come overnight: a bomb maker, a workshop, explosives stored beneath a family house.

Adam checked the recorder and slipped it into his breast pocket. Orders changed between languages. Later, officers sometimes denied having given them.

Jawad handed back the empty glass.

“If they have bread in there,” he said, “ask politely.”

The village smelled of baking and diesel. A woman was sweeping dust from her doorstep into the unpaved lane. She stopped when the trucks appeared. Behind her, a boy held a red plastic sandal by its strap.

They searched the named house first. Adam translated questions for a gray-bearded farmer whose hands shook so badly he could not keep his identity card still. Soldiers opened grain sacks and overturned bedding. Beneath the floor they found a leaking water pipe.

No workshop. No explosives. No bomb maker.

The contractor came through the gate without asking what they had found. A radio hung against his vest. His sleeves were rolled with identical folds.

Their lieutenant called him Sulaiman.

“Gather them,” Sulaiman said in English.

Adam glanced at the lieutenant. The man looked away.

“All civilians are to gather at the square,” Adam translated. “Repeat, all civilians are to gather at the square.”

They came carrying documents, keys, a baby wrapped despite the heat. The farmer kept trying to speak. Adam told him to wait, and afterward remembered that instruction more clearly than anything the man had said.

Sulaiman listened to his radio. Then he looked toward the lane leading out through the orchards.

“No witnesses outside the perimeter.”

“No one is to leave,” Adam said.

Sulaiman turned to him.

“Not leave. Survive.”

Adam heard a soldier behind him shift his rifle.

“These are civilians.”

“Translate the order.”

There was time to refuse. Adam would spend ten years inside that small space of time, though he would not remember its shape.

“Kill them,” he told the soldiers in Arabic. His voice was low. They heard him. “Everyone in the square.”

Jawad stepped out of the line.

“No.”

The word seemed impossibly loud. Sulaiman reached for his radio again. Their lieutenant said Jawad's name as though correcting a minor breach of discipline.

Jawad backed toward the woman with the baby. “This way.”

The first shots struck the wall above the farmer. Then bodies obscured him. Adam could not find a place to look that did not require him to do something.

“We have families here,” he said.

“Translate the order.”

But he had already translated it.

Jawad pushed the woman into a side passage. Her husband followed, dragging the boy with the sandal. Adam moved after them, his hand pressed against the recorder through his shirt. He did not remember deciding to stop it. He remembered the small button beneath his finger.

Beyond the houses, their truck stood by an irrigation ditch. Jawad pulled open the rear canvas and lifted the boy inside. The mother climbed after him. Her husband hesitated until another burst of firing sent him scrambling up.

At the road junction Rashid intercepted them with a medical bag. Other soldiers were gathering there, pale and shaking. Someone had vomited against a wheel.

“For shock,” Rashid said.

Adam offered his arm because the lieutenant was watching. Rashid would not meet his eyes. A needle entered below the rolled sleeve; then Rashid turned toward Jawad.

The memory did not soften. Adam remembered wanting it to.

Jawad took the wheel. Adam climbed beside him. Behind the canvas the baby made a thin, indignant noise. For a few seconds, moving away from the village, Adam thought they had accomplished something that could still be called good.

The roadside lifted.

He never saw the device. There was white dust, a blow beneath the truck, the windshield breaking inward. When he found himself on the road, one boot was missing.

Jawad was trapped behind the buckled wheel.

“Adam!”

He crawled toward the open door. Blood ran from Jawad's jaw onto his collar. In the back someone was coughing. Adam reached for the crushed metal and burned his palm.

“Pull me.”

He pulled. Jawad screamed.

From the junction came an order to fall back. The fuel was burning. Adam looked toward the voice, grateful for an instruction, grateful so suddenly that the shame of it survived everything that followed.

“Adam. Look at me.”

He stepped backward.

Jawad reached through the broken window.

Adam ran.

A second blast threw him forward. The road struck his face, and the world closed without a sound.

He woke clawing at the mattress.

Nadia knelt near him, saying his name. His shirt clung wetly to his back. Jawad stood beside the shutter, half turned from the street, his hand still resting on the pistol at his waist.

“Sulaiman,” Adam said.

Jawad went still.

“I remember his name. I remember what I said.”

Nadia lowered the glass she had been holding out to him.

Adam tried to tell Jawad about the door, the heat, the order that had given him permission to run. What emerged was a broken breath, then another. He bent over his knees and sobbed without covering his face.

Jawad sat on the floor within reach. He did not say that it was all right.

Behind the wall, water dripped into metal. A beam creaked overhead. Nadia put the glass down with extraordinary care.

Adam heard it all, and beneath it the raw, unfamiliar sound of his own grief.

## Chapter 8: The Price of Sound

The sound did not lessen with morning.

Adam woke to the city arguing through the walls: a bus grinding its gears, a woman calling from a balcony, water hammering in a pipe above the tailor shop. Each noise arrived sharpened. The room had no distance. He pressed his palms to his ears. It made no difference.

“There’s someone who can help,” Jawad said.

“I don’t want help.”

“You want the pain to stop.”

The man who came upstairs wore a doctor’s coat beneath a brown street jacket. Jawad called him Yusef. He set a metal case on the table beside the recorder.

“Rashid taught me in the field,” Yusef said. “Before Meridian bought his loyalty.”

Adam laughed once. The sound startled him. “And now you sell silence?”

“I offer it.”

Yusef opened the case. A syringe lay in blue wrapping, its liquid clear as rainwater.

“It interrupts the pathways opened by the suppressant and the trauma. Your hearing will recede. The memories attached to it will recede too.”

“Bury them.”

“Make them unreachable.”

Jawad turned toward the window. In the glass, Adam saw the scar along his jaw and the pistol in his hand.

“How long?”

“Perhaps years. Perhaps forever.”

“And the truth?”

“Truth is not a medicine.”

Outside, a motorcycle passed. Adam heard its engine fade, heard every cough in the street and his own heart trying to escape. He imagined silence returning—the clean wall between himself and the world, the old peace that had let him sleep. Then he imagined Barqa and Jawad reaching through the broken window.

“No,” Adam said. “If I forget again, they win again.”

“Pain is not proof of courage.”

“No. But forgetting was proof of fear.”

A shot cracked below.

The sound tore through him. Nadia dropped behind the table. Jawad kicked the chair from the door and fired through the wood. Plaster burst from the wall. Yusef crawled for his case.

“They found us,” Jawad said.

Adam heard the men before he saw them: two sets of feet on the stairs, one dragging slightly, a magazine slapped into a weapon. He pointed.

“Left side. They’re coming up.”

Jawad opened the door a fraction, fired twice, and pulled Nadia into the corridor. Yusef followed. They ran down the rear stairwell and out behind the tailor shop.

The old quarter folded around them in alleys of crumbling balconies and hanging laundry. Men shouted behind them. Adam heard a safety click before a gunman emerged around the corner.

“Down!”

He dragged Nadia beneath a stone arch. Jawad fired over them. Another man appeared on a roofline, boots scraping tile.

“Roof,” Adam gasped.

Jawad looked up and shot. The man vanished.

The city had become a map made of sound: a rifle bolt, footsteps on a metal stair, a van turning into the lane. Adam named each danger and Jawad answered it with movement. They crossed a courtyard, ducked through a bakery, and burst into a lane smelling of yeast, sewage, and hot stone.

Then the noise broke him.

A child screamed nearby. A truck horn blared. Gunfire struck the walls. Adam dropped to his knees, hands clamped over his ears, unable to breathe.

Jawad crouched before him. “Adam. Look at me.”

“I can’t.”

“You can.” Jawad gripped his shoulder. “You left me once. Don’t leave yourself now.”

Adam found the separate rhythm of Jawad’s voice and followed it upward. He stood.

They reached an abandoned print shop. Nadia locked the rear door while Jawad dragged a press against it. Yusef, pale and shaking, put the syringe back in its case.

“We need a public outlet,” Nadia said. “Someone who can’t be quietly erased.”

“The recording can go online,” Adam said. “Not from one phone. Everywhere at once.”

“I know a reporter,” Nadia said. “Kareem Haddad. He has a secure upload.”

Jawad laid the recorder on the press. “We go to him.”

A van braked outside the rear door.

Nadia was still beside it. Before Jawad could reach her, the door slammed inward against the press, forcing it across the floor. An arm caught Nadia around the neck. Jawad raised his pistol but she was between him and the gunman. A second man fired into the ceiling. Adam fell against the desk, shielding the recorder.

By the time Jawad reached the lane, the van was moving. He aimed after it, then lowered the pistol. Nadia's face showed through the rear glass. Her mouth was open.

Her phone lay on the threshold. It began to ring.

Jawad answered and put it on speaker.

“Bring me the recorder,” a man said. “And the notebook. Your translator too.”

Adam knew the voice before the man gave his name.

“Sulaiman.”

“You remember. Good. My men should have collected Mansour's papers before you found them. Now you will deliver them. The Meridian office at the eastern freight yard. One hour. No police, no journalists, or she dies.”

The line went dead.

Yusef leaned against the press, bleeding from a cut above his eye. “I'll find another way out,” he said. “Go.”

Jawad picked up his pistol. His hand shook as he checked the magazine.

Adam put the recorder inside his coat, beside Rashid's notebook and Nadia's folder. He could still hear her gasp when the man had seized her.

“We go together.”

“This is bait.”

“I know.”

“And after we get Nadia?”

Beyond the walls a radio played in someone's kitchen. Adam waited until he could distinguish the singer's voice from the pounding in his head.

“Then we make them hear.”

## Chapter 9: The Fugitive's Mirror

The eastern freight yard had been abandoned by the railways and adopted by Meridian.

Adam knew this before they reached the office because the city changed around it. The backstreets thinned into corrugated fences and heaps of ballast. Engines groaned beyond the walls. Wind dragged grit across the asphalt. Every sound arrived inside his skull without mercy: Jawad’s breathing, the click of his pistol, a loose chain striking a gate.

They left the taxi beneath a dead billboard and crossed the yard on foot. Jawad wore a stolen workman’s jacket over his bloodstained shirt. Adam carried the recorder and notebook in a canvas satchel, Nadia’s folder tucked beneath his belt. The evidence felt heavier than paper.

“West entrance,” Jawad whispered.

Adam pointed toward the dark warehouse. “Two men behind the office. One smoking. The other has a radio.”

Jawad nodded. They circled the fence between rusted containers. A guard turned at the wrong moment. Jawad struck him beneath the ear and caught him before he fell. Adam took the radio. A voice crackled through it.

“Check the rear room.”

Nadia.

Adam’s chest tightened. “They’re moving her.”

Jawad pulled the guard’s keys free. “Then we move faster.”

The office had once been a customs building. Its windows were painted black, and Meridian’s silver compass marked the door. Inside, the air smelled of dust, oil, and old disinfectant. Adam heard five men: two in the corridor, one above, two near the loading bay.

Jawad opened a side door. They slipped into a records room lined with cabinets. He killed the lights. Darkness gathered, but Adam no longer needed sight alone. A guard passed. Adam caught his wrist and drove his hand beneath the man’s jaw. The guard folded silently.

“You’re learning,” Jawad said.

“I learned in the army.”

“No. You learned there how not to feel it.”

Beyond the wall, Nadia kicked a chair.

They found her in a glass-walled conference room. Her hands were bound, one cheek swollen, a pistol pressed to her temple by a young guard.

At the far end stood Sulaiman.

Age had thickened him but had not softened him. He wore a clean shirt and polished shoes, like a man receiving guests in his own house. Adam recognized the voice before Sulaiman spoke. It was the same voice preserved on the recorder beneath gunfire—the voice that had ordered the villagers gathered.

“Adam Khalil,” Sulaiman said. “You came carrying your conscience.”

Jawad raised his pistol. “Let her go.”

Sulaiman smiled. “Samir Nasser. Or should I say Jawad Haddad? The dead are inconvenient when they return.”

Nadia stared at him. “You were there.”

“I commanded the operation,” Sulaiman said. “Barqa was a necessary correction. Your father understood necessity.”

The word entered Adam like a blade.

“You ordered the killing.”

“I ordered containment. You translated it beautifully.” Sulaiman nodded toward the satchel. “Give me the recorder and notebook. Then take the injection.”

A man stepped from the shadows carrying Yusef’s metal case. He opened it and displayed the syringe.

“Silence is a merciful country,” Sulaiman said. “You lived there for ten years. Return to it. Forget Barqa. Forget Jawad. Forget the faces you invented to punish yourself. You can live.”

Adam heard the fluorescent tube buzz, Nadia’s breath catch, and Jawad’s finger tighten against the trigger.

“I am living now,” Adam said.

“No. You are remembering.”

Adam looked at Jawad. The scarred side of his face was rigid, his eyes fixed on Sulaiman’s throat. Beneath the rage was something colder: years spent sharpening pain until it could be used. Jawad had not come only to rescue Nadia. He had come to finish the war inside himself.

“Jawad,” Adam said.

“Move.”

“Don’t.”

Sulaiman laughed softly. “He deserves the shot.”

Jawad’s pistol rose another inch.

Adam stepped between them. “If you kill him, Meridian gets a dead commander and a story about a terrorist. The recording disappears. Nadia becomes evidence of nothing.”

Jawad’s eyes did not leave Sulaiman. “He killed them.”

“And you will let him choose what we become?”

For an instant Adam saw the burning truck, Jawad’s hand through smoke, his own back turning away. He had abandoned a man who survived by becoming the weapon Adam feared to be.

“Give me the gun,” Adam said.

Jawad’s jaw trembled. “You gave me nothing in Barqa.”

“I know.”

Sulaiman moved his hand.

The room exploded.

Glass burst inward. The young guard fired; Nadia dropped. Jawad shoved Adam aside and returned fire as Sulaiman’s men poured through the corridor. Adam heard each weapon before it discharged—the scrape of a shoulder, the lift of a rifle, the breath before a trigger.

“Left!” he shouted.

Jawad fired. A man fell. Adam seized Nadia’s bound wrists and dragged her toward the loading door while bullets punched through the glass.

“Jawad!”

“I’m behind you!”

They reached the passage. Jawad slammed the door and staggered.

A dark stain spread beneath his ribs.

“Keep moving,” he said.

Adam caught him. “You’re hit.”

“Not dead.”

Nadia, freed now, took his arm. Together they ran into the loading bay. A truck stood with its engine idling, rear doors open. Adam heard boots closing from three directions.

“Under the truck.”

They crawled through oil and gravel as bullets struck the metal above. Jawad’s blood warmed Adam’s hand. At the far side they reached a drainage ditch and slipped beneath the freight-yard fence.

Only in the alleys beyond the tracks did Jawad collapse against a wall.

Adam pressed his hands to the wound. The city roared around them—horns, dogs, a distant call to prayer beginning and breaking in the wind.

Jawad gripped his coat and shoved the recorder into his hands.

“Make them hear.”

“We will. Stay with us.”

Jawad looked at him through the pain. “You came back.”

Adam could not answer. The man he had left in the fire had saved him again. No apology could cross that distance. No life was long enough to repay it.

Nadia pulled them toward the darkness. Adam carried the recorder against his heart, listening to Jawad breathe, and understood that truth was not a door one passed through. It was a debt that followed.

## Chapter 10: What the Silence Hides

The bakery owner let them in because Nadia showed him her hospital badge, and because Jawad was bleeding onto his doorstep.

He lowered the shutter behind them. His son brought towels. Adam knelt on flour-dusted tiles and pressed where Nadia told him to press, while she used the baker’s telephone to call the emergency entrance.

For once, the city’s noise became useful without becoming a weapon. An ambulance. A stretcher unfolded. Someone asking what had happened.

“Gunshot,” Nadia said. “He needs a surgeon.”

Adam stayed beside Jawad until the hospital doors divided them. Beneath the broken light outside the operating theatre, he found himself looking at his hands as though they belonged to the person who had done the shooting.

Nadia sat next to him. One of her wrists was swollen. Someone had offered her ice wrapped in a dishcloth.

“You stopped him killing Sulaiman,” she said.

“And then he was shot.”

“Yes.”

He looked at her, grateful and wounded by the absence of consolation.

She pressed the cloth to her wrist. “We still have to send it.”

They used a computer in a records room whose door no longer shut properly. Nadia called Kareem Haddad from the desk telephone and made him repeat the address of his upload page. Adam connected the recorder with a cable found in a drawer. Beside the audio file they uploaded photographs of the notebook, the ledger, the employment records.

The progress bar stopped twice. Each time, Adam held his breath.

Then the reporter’s message arrived: Received. Originals must be preserved. Can you speak on record?

Adam watched the cursor blink.

He could still put down a false name. He could describe himself as someone who had merely overheard an order. There were so many small ways to run.

He typed: My name is Adam Khalil. The translator on the recording is me.

At four in the morning, the surgeon came looking for them.

They had stopped the bleeding, he said, but Jawad’s heart had stopped afterward. They had tried to bring him back.

Adam heard every word. There was no merciful failure of hearing, no blank place in which a different sentence might have been spoken.

“Can I see him?”

Jawad lay beneath a thin sheet. Someone had cleaned his face, leaving the scar pale against his jaw. Adam sat beside him and held the hand that had pushed the recorder into his own.

“I sent it,” he said.

There was nothing else he could report that Jawad needed.

He stayed until a nurse came to ask his name, and then Jawad’s. The second question took him longer.

“Jawad Haddad,” he said. “The other death was a lie.”

*

The story did not break like glass. It spread through the city in messages, copied files, a television playing above a mechanic’s bench.

Kareem withheld the villagers’ screams from the public excerpt. Investigators received the complete recording. He checked dates against military records, found a former driver who remembered Meridian’s vehicles, and asked Adam the same questions on three different days.

“What did you understand when you translated it?”

Adam wanted a better answer by the third day.

“I understood enough.”

Nadia gave her father’s papers under her own name. When Kareem asked whether Rashid had tried to help the men he treated, she laid a finger on the ledger.

“Print what he wrote. Not what I wish he meant.”

There were denials. Meridian called the recording fabricated, then incomplete. Its local office closed. Men who had never heard of Barqa began explaining why it had been necessary.

But other witnesses came forward, and copies of the evidence had gone beyond the city before anyone could seize the originals.

Three weeks after Jawad died, Nadia found Adam in the hospital courtyard and handed him her phone.

Sulaiman was being led down courthouse steps between two officers. His shirt collar had folded beneath his jacket. The report said he had been arrested under a prosecutor’s warrant in the Barqa investigation.

Adam played the clip once.

“He’ll have lawyers,” Nadia said.

“Yes.”

“And friends.”

“Yes.”

The sight did not make him lighter. But Sulaiman was alive, and would have to hear other people speak.

*

Before dawn, Adam climbed to the hospital roof.

Below him, the city remained itself: water tanks patched with tar, satellite dishes facing distant countries, a bus coughing smoke at an empty stop. Somewhere beneath his feet a trolley wheel struck the same cracked tile again and again.

His hearing had stayed. The doctors could document it, though none could explain why it had returned. They spoke cautiously about further examinations and ways to manage the pain. He had begun carrying earplugs. Resting from sound, one doctor told him, was not the same as refusing it.

Some nights he still imagined the injection. He did not know what had been in the syringe or whether its promise could have been kept. He remembered wanting it. That was enough to trouble him.

The roof door scraped open. Nadia brought two paper cups of tea.

“There’s a woman downstairs,” she said. “The legal aid people sent her. She has documents in English. Her son disappeared at a checkpoint.”

Adam curled his hands around the cup.

“I haven’t translated for anyone in years.”

“I know.”

“What does she need?”

“To understand what she’s signing.”

A muezzin began the call to prayer. Another voice joined from farther away, slightly behind the first. Traffic stirred beneath them. A child laughed on a neighboring balcony, delighted by something Adam could not see.

He listened, and Barqa was there too. Not in the street: in him. The shots, the order in his own voice, Jawad asking to be heard. No morning would arrange those sounds into innocence.

He finished his tea.

“Tell her I’m coming.”

Downstairs he would ask the woman to sit where he could see her face. He would read every line, including the small print. If she needed him to repeat something, he would repeat it. He would not tell her where to sign until she understood.

Adam held the roof door for Nadia, then followed her toward the waiting room.
