## Chapter 1: The Corridor of Static

Adam Khalil knew the city through his feet.

Buses made the pavement tremble before they appeared. Water pipes shuddered in the walls when the generator started. Even the old apartment building announced its movements through vibration: a stair creaking, a door slamming, a truck grinding along the avenue below.

Sound belonged to other people.

For seven years Adam had lived behind glass. He could read lips in three languages and understand a conversation from hands, posture, and expression. He could feel a shout through a tabletop or recognize an approaching motorcycle by the tremor it sent through the pavement. But the world reached him incomplete, stripped of its voice.

He had learned to live with that absence.

He had not learned to stop watching for danger.

That morning, he shaved beside the bathroom mirror and touched the pale scar behind his left ear. The doctors had called it blast trauma, permanent nerve damage. They had been very certain. Adam had been twenty-eight then. He was thirty-five now.

The public hospital had summoned him for a routine examination. Routine, he had learned, was a word institutions used when they had forgotten why they were touching you.

He put the appointment slip in his pocket, checked his phone, and walked outside.

The old quarter was already hot. Vendors arranged oranges beneath patched umbrellas. A traffic officer argued with a man in a gray robe. Their mouths opened and closed, their hands cutting the air. Adam kept moving.

The hospital leaned over the street like a tired animal. Its balconies were cracked, its walls stained by exhaust and old rain. Inside, the air smelled of disinfectant, damp plaster, and boiled vegetables. A ceiling fan turned above the reception desk, its chipped blades casting a broken shadow.

Adam showed the clerk his papers. She spoke without looking up.

He pointed to his ear.

With an irritated sigh, she wrote the department number on a scrap of paper.

Second floor.

The stairwell was crowded. Adam climbed with one hand on the railing, feeling footsteps travel through the metal. On the landing, orderlies carried a stretcher past him. A woman followed, gripping the hand of a boy whose mouth was wide with crying.

Adam looked away.

The second-floor corridor was packed with patients. Men sat beneath peeling health posters. Women held folders in their laps. A child slept across his mother’s knees. Fluorescent tubes flickered overhead, making the crowd move in broken frames.

Adam found a place beside a dusty window.

Once, he had carried voices across borders. As a military translator, he had made orders understandable and pleas impossible to ignore. His mouth had been a bridge between strangers.

Then the bridge had collapsed.

A man in a brown jacket emerged from the crowd near the nurses’ station. Adam noticed him because he moved against the flow. He was tall but stooped, with close-cropped hair and a pale scar running from the corner of his mouth into his cheek.

The man’s gaze passed over Adam, then returned.

Recognition stirred in Adam, though he could not place the face.

The stranger came closer. His left hand was curled tightly at his side.

He seemed to be passing. His shoulder brushed Adam’s.

His lips moved.

Adam saw one word.

Barqa.

Sound broke open.

It struck him as if the hospital had exploded inside his skull. The fluorescent lights buzzed. Shoes scraped across the tile. A metal tray clattered somewhere nearby. A monitor beeped. Voices rose over one another, indistinct and furious. Someone was praying. A nurse shouted for a doctor.

Then the child began to scream.

Adam clapped his hands over his ears, but the sound only grew louder. He heard his own breath rasping, the pounding of his heart, the harsh machinery beneath the building. Every noise arrived at once, sharp enough to cut.

The scarred man was walking away.

Adam pushed into the crowd. He saw the brown jacket near the stairwell, then behind three men, then gone. He seized a stranger’s sleeve.

“What?” the man demanded.

The word struck Adam like a blow.

His knees buckled.

The corridor tilted beneath him. Lights burst white across his vision. The child was still screaming. Someone called his name.

Then the floor rose and swallowed him.

He woke in a narrow examination room with a doctor leaning over him. The doctor’s mouth moved rapidly. A nurse loosened Adam’s collar and pressed fingers to his wrist.

Adam pointed to his ears.

The doctor spoke again, slower.

“Can you hear me?”

The shape of the question reached Adam, but not its sound.

He shook his head.

They tested his pupils, blood pressure, and reflexes. They drew blood. A machine was rolled into the room and cold instruments pressed against his chest. Adam tried to explain about the stranger, about the word, about the torrent of noise, but every explanation failed in his hands.

He wrote on the back of his appointment slip:

I heard.

The doctor read it and frowned. Beneath the sentence, he wrote:

You are deaf.

Adam stared at the words.

By late afternoon, they released him. No stroke. No seizure. No visible injury. A physician recommended rest and an appointment with an ear specialist, as though a specialist could locate seven years of silence and explain why it had vanished for less than a minute.

Outside, the city looked unchanged.

Traffic pushed through the avenue. Vendors waved customers toward their stalls. A motorcycle cut across the pavement. Adam saw everything and heard nothing.

Silence had returned, settling over him with its old, familiar weight.

At home, he sat on the edge of his bed without removing his shoes. A truck passed below, sending a faint tremor through the building. Dust drifted from the ceiling.

His phone lit with a message from the hospital reminding him about his next appointment.

Adam deleted it.

In the bathroom, he stood before the mirror and touched the scar behind his ear. His face looked hollow and older than it had that morning.

The stranger’s lips moved again in memory.

Barqa.

The word had not been spoken to him in years. Not by anyone who knew what it meant.

A village. A road. A line of names he had buried so deeply that even memory had stopped searching.

Adam lowered his hand.

The silence remained, but it no longer felt sealed. Somewhere inside it, a door had opened.

The stranger had disappeared.

The word had not.

It burned in the dark behind Adam’s ears.

## Chapter 2: The Dead Man's Room

Adam returned to the hospital with the word **Barqa** written three times on the inside of his wrist.

He had slept badly. Whenever he closed his eyes, the stranger’s mouth appeared again: the pale scar running from it into his cheek, the careful shape of the whispered word. Adam had searched Barqa before dawn. It was a village beyond the eastern hills, a road on an old military map, and nothing else. No explanation for why the word had broken through seven years of silence.

The hospital was already crowded. Relatives filled the corridors; orderlies pushed carts between them; a child clung to his mother’s dress. Adam knew the movement of the place by sight and vibration. He ignored the stares and went directly to the corridor where the stranger had passed him.

There was no one there.

Adam stopped beneath the fluorescent lights. He remembered the sudden flood of sound—the buzzing tubes, shoes scraping tile, a clattering tray, the scream of a child. Now the corridor was mute again.

He looked up.

The security camera above the nurses’ station had been smashed. Another camera near the stairwell hung from its wires, turning slightly in the draft.

A nurse in a green coat passed with a stack of files. Adam stepped into her path and sketched a face in the air: close-cropped hair, a scar beside the mouth. Then he wrote in his notebook:

*The man who was here yesterday. Brown jacket.*

The nurse read it. Recognition crossed her face.

“Room three-twelve,” she said.

Adam caught the words from her lips.

She pointed toward the elevators. “He visited the old man.”

“Who?”

“Rashid Mansour.” She tapped the file. “He died last night.”

Adam pointed toward the stairwell and wrote, *Why did the scarred man visit him?*

The nurse’s expression tightened. “Ask Nadia. Records office. His daughter.”

She started away, but Adam caught her sleeve. He wrote, *What happened?*

The nurse glanced down the corridor before answering.

“He was sick for a long time. Kept asking for people who weren’t there.”

Then she freed herself and disappeared behind the swinging doors.

Adam took the stairs.

Room 312 was at the far end of a dim ward. A paper sign on the door read:

**MANSOUR, R. — RELEASED TO MORGUE**

The door stood open.

Inside, the bed had been stripped. A gray blanket lay folded at its foot, and a plastic cup rested on the bedside table beside a comb tangled with white hair. Adam searched the drawer, then the cabinet. Nothing.

He crouched and slid his fingers beneath the mattress.

They touched cloth.

The notebook was black, its cover softened by years of use. Adam carried it to the window and opened it beneath the weak daylight.

The first pages contained columns of dates and initials.

**K-17 — transferred.**  
**Haddad, J. — no confirmation.**  
**S-4 — paid in full.**  
**Barqa — 06/11.**

Names had been crossed out or circled until the paper tore. Between the lists, sentences appeared in Arabic and English:

*They said the order came from above.*

*Not soldiers. Witnesses.*

Then, underlined twice:

**MERIDIAN SOLUTIONS**

Adam knew the name. Meridian supplied security teams and vehicles to governments that preferred their violence at a distance. He knew the logo too: a silver compass inside a black square.

He turned the page.

A crude map filled the center spread. A road curved around a cluster of buildings. Beside it, in block letters, someone had written **BARQA**.

Beneath the map was a list of names.

One stopped Adam’s hand.

**Jawad Haddad.**

He had seen that name carved into a military cemetery three years earlier. His squadmate. Listed dead in the same operation that had taken Adam’s hearing.

A pressure gathered behind his eyes. Dust. Heat. A man shouting with his mouth open.

The door moved.

A woman stood in the opening, holding a file against her ribs. She was around thirty, with dark hair pulled into a loose knot and the exhausted face of someone who belonged to the hospital more than the city outside.

Her eyes went to the notebook.

Adam rose.

She closed the door behind her. She did not call security.

“You are Adam Khalil,” she said.

He nodded.

“Nurse Salma told me you would come.”

Adam pointed to the notebook, then to the empty bed. He drew the stranger’s scar with one finger and wrote:

*He visited your father. He said a word to me yesterday.*

“What word?”

Adam turned the notebook around.

**BARQA.**

The woman’s grip tightened on the file.

“My father said it too,” she said. “Before he died.”

Adam stared at her.

She sat on the edge of the bed. “He had failing lungs, but that wasn’t what frightened me. He stopped sleeping. Kept asking who had come through the door. There was no one.”

She touched the black cover.

“My father was a field medic. Army first, then contractors. He never told me where he worked. When I was young, he woke screaming. Later, he stopped screaming and began writing.”

Adam opened the notebook to the Meridian logo.

Nadia looked at it and went pale. “I know that mark.”

He tapped the name Haddad, then wrote:

*Who is he?*

“I don’t know.”

Adam wrote, *He is dead.*

“That is not an answer.”

He closed the notebook. His fingers had begun to shake.

Nadia looked toward the corridor. “If you take that, people will know.”

“Who?”

Her mouth twisted. “That is what I’ve been trying to find out.”

For a moment, they faced each other across the stripped bed. Outside, bodies moved soundlessly past the glass: doctors, cleaners, families waiting for news.

Nadia reached into her pocket and took out a small brass key.

“I can access the old hospital records,” she said. “My father’s employment files. Transfers. Anything they failed to remove.”

Adam wrote, *Why help me?*

She looked at the bed.

“Because he spent his life refusing to tell me what happened. At the end, he held my hand and apologized to someone who wasn’t there.”

She stood.

“Come tomorrow. After sunset. Records basement.”

Adam slipped the notebook beneath his coat.

At the door, Nadia spoke again. He turned.

Her lips formed the question slowly.

“Do you hear him?”

Adam shook his head.

Outside, the city struck him in fragments.

His own name, whispered close to his ear.

A distant siren.

Then a woman weeping, so softly he could not tell whether she was in the street or inside his skull.

The sounds vanished. Silence settled over him again.

Adam stood beneath the hospital’s cracked façade, one hand pressed against the notebook, and understood that the silence had begun to crack with him.

## Chapter 3: Barqa

Adam’s apartment had one room, one window, and no reason for anyone to visit.

He locked the door and stood with his back against it, watching the city move beyond the glass. Traffic crawled along the avenue in red and white threads. He could not hear the horns, engines, or arguments passing between balconies. He saw them instead: mouths opening, hands rising, bodies leaning toward one another with the exhausted urgency of people who believed volume could change the world.

For seven years, silence had been a wall.

Tonight, it felt like a door that would not close.

He placed the black notebook on the table beside Nadia’s brass key and the scrap of paper on which he had written the word three times.

BARQA.

Under the yellow light, he opened the notebook.

The first pages were filled with names, dates, initials, and amounts of money. Some names had been crossed out so heavily the paper was torn. Adam recognized the habits of military records: compression, omission, the false neatness of men trying to make terrible things look administrative.

He copied the names into a clean column.

S. Qasim.  
H. Nasser.  
R. Mansour.  
J. Haddad.

He stopped.

The name seemed to change the air around it.

Jawad Haddad.

A memory stirred behind his eyes: a raised hand against a white sky, a crooked grin, boots sinking into dust. He reached for it, but it dissolved.

He turned the page.

A crude map filled the center spread. A road ran east from the hills, then divided around a cluster of small squares. A well was marked with an X. On the northern edge, someone had drawn a rectangle and written:

BARQA  
06/11

Beneath it, in English:

NOT SOLDIERS. WITNESSES.

Adam traced the words with his fingertip.

A pressure gathered behind his right eye. In the margin, Mansour had written a sentence in Arabic, then translated it below:

They said the order came from above.

Adam knew both languages. He did not know why his hands had begun to shake.

He leaned closer.

For less than a second, he heard a pen scratching paper.

Scratch. Scratch. Scratch.

The sound seemed to come from the table, though the pen beside his phone lay untouched. Adam stared at it. Silence returned.

He pressed his palms to his temples, then forced himself to turn another page.

Near the back were several photographs, most of them photocopies faded to gray. One showed Rashid Mansour in a field uniform, younger and broad-faced, standing beside a military truck. Another showed three men in desert gear beside a concrete barrier.

Adam recognized himself first.

He was twenty-five, thinner, his hair too long beneath his helmet. On his left stood a younger Jawad Haddad.

Adam touched the photograph.

Jawad had the same crooked mouth and deep-set eyes as the man in the hospital. His left hand rested against his thigh, the fingers already curled inward. There was no scar along his jaw then. His face was open, almost boyish.

Behind them, printed on the truck door, was a silver compass inside a black square.

Meridian Solutions.

Adam sat down.

The stranger’s eyes had been the same. So had the hand, the mouth, the way he had looked at Adam—not like a stranger, but like someone waiting for a locked door to open.

Jawad.

The name moved through him without sound.

Jawad was dead.

Adam knew this as surely as he knew his own name. The military record listed him as killed in the same operation that had taken Adam’s hearing. There had been a folded flag, perhaps a ceremony. Adam remembered a major speaking to Jawad’s mother and people touching his shoulder while he stood apart, unable to bear their pity.

But he did not remember a grave.

He searched the notebook again. On a page near the back, Jawad’s name appeared beside a date and one word:

CONFIRMED?

The question mark had been pressed so deeply it nearly pierced the paper.

Adam rose.

The cemetery lay beyond the western ring road, behind a wall stained black by exhaust and rain. He arrived after sunset and showed his old service identification to the guard, who barely glanced at it.

Inside, rows of white stones stretched beneath weak lamps. The city glimmered beyond the wall. Adam walked between the graves, reading names without seeing them, until he reached the military section for the Barqa operation.

JAWAD HADDAD  
TRANSLATOR’S AUXILIARY  
1988–2014

The stone was clean. Too clean. No flowers, photograph, or offering marked the earth around it.

Adam crouched and placed his palm against the marble.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then the world cracked open.

A child’s face appeared in the dust, round and terrified, one cheek streaked with blood. Behind him stood villagers in a line. Someone shouted in English. Someone answered in Arabic. A rifle slammed against metal.

Adam smelled burning rubber.

A man called his name.

“Adam!”

The voice was young and furious.

Jawad.

Adam tore his hand from the grave.

Sound surged into him. Traffic beyond the wall became a river of grinding engines. Wind hissed through branches. Somewhere in the city, a woman laughed. From a distant minaret came the opening notes of the call to prayer, thin and mournful beneath the darkness.

His heart thudded once, twice, impossibly loud.

Then everything vanished.

Adam bent forward, gripping the stone as silence dropped over him again. Pain drove behind his eye, bright and pulsing. The memory remained: villagers, dust, the child’s face, Jawad calling him.

He fumbled for his phone and opened the notes application.

JAWAD IS ALIVE.

He stared at the sentence. It looked absurd, the desperate conclusion of a man whose mind had been damaged by war.

After a moment, he added:

OR SOMEONE WANTS ME TO THINK HE IS.

A scrape of gravel sounded at the edge of hearing.

Adam turned.

The path between the graves was empty.

Another scrape, closer.

He held his breath. Nothing followed. Only the pressure in his skull, the dark between the lamps, and the clean white stone beneath his hand.

Jawad Haddad had been buried ten years ago.

Yet Jawad was the man who had whispered Barqa.

Adam looked down at the name carved into the grave and understood that the dead man had spoken to him for a reason.

Now he had begun to remember why.

## Chapter 4: Shadowed Backstreets

The visitor log had been written in a clerk’s cramped hand, each name squeezed into the margin beside the date.

Adam read it three times before the false name separated itself from the others.

**Kamal Darwish.**

The visitor had signed in just after midnight, the same night Rashid Mansour died. Beneath the name, impressed faintly into the paper, was the outline of a stamp: a circle divided by a diagonal line, with three Arabic letters inside.

Adam had seen the mark before.

It appeared on the last page of Mansour’s notebook, beside an address in the old quarter.

He copied the address, folded the paper, and slipped the notebook beneath his shirt.

The pharmacy had once been painted white. Its walls were now the color of old bone, and the green sign above the door was eaten with rust. Through the glass, shelves leaned beneath dusty boxes. A boy in a blue coat sat behind the counter reading a newspaper.

Adam stood across the street and watched.

No one entered. No one left.

The old quarter folded around the pharmacy in crooked layers: balconies sagging over alleys, shutters sealed against the sun, electrical wires knotted from wall to wall. There were streets in the city where the police came only when paid, streets where a person could vanish between one doorway and the next.

Adam crossed.

The boy looked up. Adam showed him the copied address.

The boy glanced at it, then at Adam, and shook his head.

Adam pointed toward the rear of the shop.

The boy shook his head again and returned to his newspaper.

A narrow passage ran along the building. Adam entered it, one hand brushing the wall. He read the place through vibration and pressure: a generator trembling behind brick, footsteps passing overhead, a door opening somewhere nearby.

At the passage’s end stood a wooden gate marked with the circular stamp.

The latch was open.

Beyond it lay a courtyard crowded with plastic chairs, rusted bicycles, and a blue water tank. A door stood ajar beneath a string of dead bulbs.

Adam stepped inside.

Something struck the back of his head.

White light burst across his vision. He dropped to one knee, clutching the notebook as it slipped from beneath his shirt.

A boot drove into his ribs.

The man above him wore a gray jacket and had a narrow face, a shaved scalp, and a fresh cut across one eyebrow. Behind him stood another man holding a length of pipe.

The first man pointed at Adam’s chest, then made a grabbing motion.

The notebook.

Adam shook his head.

The pipe came down. He rolled aside. Metal smashed the stones where his skull had been. He kicked backward, caught the gray-jacketed man behind the knee, and surged upright.

The second attacker seized his collar and slammed him into the wall.

Adam drove his forehead into the man’s nose. The grip loosened. He twisted free and ran through the open door.

Inside was a narrow room packed with sacks, broken furniture, and hanging laundry. He vaulted a crate, struck a table, and fell. The notebook skidded across the floor.

The gray-jacketed man appeared in the doorway.

Adam reached for it.

The man reached it first.

He bent, smiling.

A brown sleeve flashed across the room.

The stranger seized him by the throat and flung him into the doorframe. His head struck wood. He folded.

The second attacker lunged from behind. The stranger caught the pipe between both hands, wrenched it free, and drove an elbow into the man’s jaw. The pipe clattered away. One more short blow dropped him beside the sacks.

The stranger picked up the notebook and thrust it against Adam’s chest.

Adam saw the scar first: a pale seam running from the corner of the man’s mouth toward his cheek.

Then the eyes.

He had seen them in a photograph beside a military truck, young and laughing beneath a sky bleached white by dust.

Jawad.

Adam’s body recognized him before his mind could accept the name.

Jawad caught his wrist and pulled him through the rear of the room. They burst into an alley scarcely wide enough for their shoulders. Adam stumbled after him over damp stones. Jawad moved with a limp but no hesitation, turning beneath hanging sheets and through courtyards linked by broken walls.

A woman shouted from a window. Adam saw the anger in her face but heard nothing.

Jawad dragged him through a gap between two buildings and into a courtyard of shattered tiles. He released him.

Adam struck the wall with both palms.

**Jawad.**

The name formed silently on his lips. His mouth worked around all the questions that had waited seven years.

Jawad stood across the courtyard, breathing hard. His left hand was curled tight against his chest, the fingers drawn inward exactly as they had been in the photograph.

Adam pointed at him, then at himself.

Jawad shook his head.

“You don’t remember me.”

The words arrived without sound, read from the shape of his mouth. Yet beneath the movement came a faint impression, like a voice traveling through deep water.

Adam stepped forward.

“That’s what they wanted.”

A pulse began behind his eye.

He reached for Jawad’s sleeve. “Barqa.”

The word left him as a dry breath. Jawad flinched.

“I need you to remember.”

From the alley came movement. Adam turned toward it, then back.

Jawad was retreating through a doorway beneath a sagging arch.

Adam followed.

The passage was empty.

He searched the adjoining alley and the roofs above it. Nothing moved except a torn plastic bag snagged on wire. Jawad had vanished as completely as if the city had swallowed him.

Then the world broke open.

A groan rose behind Adam.

He spun. The gray-jacketed man crawled across the stones, clutching his throat. The second man rolled onto his back, making a wet, choking sound.

Adam heard it all.

The scrape of cloth. Loose gravel shifting beneath a palm. Water dripping from a pipe. His own breath tearing in and out of his chest.

Sound slammed into him.

Footsteps thundered beyond the walls. A motorbike shrieked past. Somewhere above, a metal shutter crashed down. His heart hammered with unbearable clarity.

Adam pressed both hands over his ears.

It made no difference.

The men groaned. He heard himself gasp. He heard the old quarter breathing through its cracks.

Jawad was alive.

And he was not the one hunting Adam.

That certainty should have brought relief.

Instead, Adam stood trembling in the filthy courtyard, the notebook clutched against his ribs, while the restored world screamed around him.

## Chapter 5: The Second Voice

Adam woke to the smell of dust, iodine, and hot iron.

For several seconds he could not move. Cracks veined the ceiling above him, and morning light lay across a thin mattress on the wooden floor. His shoulder throbbed. His forearm burned where the pipe had struck.

Then he heard a needle pass through cloth.

The tiny tearing sound came again as someone drew thread through gauze.

Adam sat up.

The room lurched. The floorboards groaned beneath his hand. From below came a machine’s rapid clatter, a man shouting, then laughter rising through the ceiling in a distorted burst.

Jawad sat beside the mattress, his back against the wall. He had removed his brown jacket. His shirt was dark with sweat, and the scar along his jaw pulled pale when he bent his head. His left hand rested curled against his thigh, the fingers trembling.

“You’re awake,” he said.

The words reached Adam in fragments, rough and delayed, as though carried through a damaged radio.

Adam touched his ear. “Where?”

“Above a tailor shop. In the old quarter. They won’t look here.”

“Who are you?”

Jawad’s eyes hardened. “You know.”

“I know what your face resembles.”

“You know my face.”

Adam tried to stand. His knees gave way. Jawad caught him and lowered him back down.

The contact brought another burst of sound: cloth rubbing cloth, a breath close to his cheek, the wet click of a swallow.

Adam shoved him away.

Jawad let him.

Between them lay the black notebook, its pages swollen from the alley. A pistol rested beside it.

“Why did you save me?” Adam asked.

“Because they were going to kill you.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It’s the only one you’re ready for.”

Adam laughed, startled by the thin, ugly noise of his own voice. He had heard it only in broken flashes since the blast.

Jawad glanced toward the shuttered window. “The men at the pharmacy work for Meridian.”

The name tightened Adam’s chest.

“Meridian Solutions stopped being a security company years ago,” Jawad said. “They became whatever governments needed. Escorts. Interrogators. Cleaners.”

“Barqa,” Adam said.

Jawad nodded. “Barqa was a Meridian operation. They were attached to our unit as advisers, but when we entered the village, they took command.”

“I remember the village.”

“You remember pieces.”

“Villagers. A child. Weapons.”

“And an order.”

Adam looked at him.

“Sulaiman gave it,” Jawad said. “Every adult in the square. Then the houses. He claimed the village sheltered bomb makers. There was no bomb maker. No weapons cache.”

“Why kill them?”

“Because there were witnesses.”

The room seemed to contract. Beneath them, the tailor’s machine stopped.

Adam remembered the words in Mansour’s notebook.

“Not soldiers,” he said.

“Witnesses.”

Jawad leaned forward. “Mansour was our medic. He worked for Meridian before he understood what they were doing. After Barqa, he injected us with a suppressant. He said it would make the memories inaccessible.”

“Us?”

“You. Me. Two others who didn’t live long enough to tell anyone.”

Adam shook his head. “Memory suppressant.”

“Mansour called it that. He thought forgetting was better than being hunted.”

“Did it work?”

Jawad looked at him. “You lost seven years.”

Adam stared at his hands. They were shaking.

“During the escape,” Jawad continued, “an IED hit the convoy. You were thrown clear. The blast damaged your ears. I was trapped in the second vehicle.”

A picture stirred: orange light, a wheel turning slowly in the air, dust thick as smoke.

“Your body was never recovered,” Adam said.

“Meridian recovered me.”

Jawad’s curled hand trembled harder.

“They kept me alive to find out what I remembered. When I wouldn’t talk, they tried other methods. Later they left me in a drainage canal. Mansour found me and brought me to the city. He spent the rest of his life recording names.”

“And you let him die?”

“I arrived too late.”

“You were at the hospital.”

“Outside.”

“Why didn’t you come to me?”

Jawad’s face tightened. “You had built a life around not knowing.”

“What life?”

Neither answered.

The tailor’s machine began again below, its needle striking in quick, exact blows.

Adam watched the scar on Jawad’s face. “Show me.”

Jawad turned away.

“The scar.”

“Adam—”

“Show me.”

Light caught the ridges running from the corner of Jawad’s mouth toward his cheek. Adam reached out before fear could stop him.

His fingertip touched the scar.

The world broke open.

A truck burned in the road, its engine screaming. Smoke rolled across the village. A child crouched behind an overturned water jar, hands clamped over her mouth. Voices shouted in English and Arabic.

Jawad was inside the truck.

His face was younger, blood running from his hairline. His left hand clawed at the bent door.

“Adam!”

Adam stood outside with his rifle against his chest.

“Adam, don’t leave me!”

Behind him, someone shouted the order to fall back.

He turned.

He ran.

The memory widened. Jawad’s hand reached through the flames, fingers stretching toward him. The truck collapsed with a roar. Heat struck Adam’s back.

He pulled away from the scar and stumbled across the room.

“I left you,” he whispered.

Jawad said nothing.

“I heard you.”

“You couldn’t save me.”

“I heard you.”

The ringing inside Adam’s skull vanished.

Everything rushed in.

The tailor’s machine below. Jawad’s breathing. Water ticking through a pipe. A motorbike whining through the street. Fabric snapping beneath a blade. His own heart hammering, each beat vast and wet.

Then the city rose around him—horns, footsteps, vendors calling, a child crying somewhere beyond the walls. The sounds layered into a physical pressure that crushed his chest.

Adam clapped both hands over his ears.

It did nothing.

Jawad seized his shoulders. His mouth moved, but Adam could not separate the words from the flood.

The room filled with the burning truck.

With Jawad calling his name.

With the last thing Adam had chosen not to hear.

## Chapter 6: Meridian

Meridian

By morning, Adam could hear the tailor’s machine through three floors of plaster.

Its needle clicked and paused, clicked and paused. Water moved in the pipes. A woman coughed below. Somewhere in the street, a truck changed gears.

He sat on the mattress with his palms over his ears. It made no difference.

“You should sleep,” Jawad said from the window.

Adam heard the words, the breath behind them, even the scrape of Jawad’s stubble as he turned.

“I slept for seven years.”

Jawad looked at him but did not answer.

Nadia arrived after sunrise carrying three paper cups of tea and a canvas bag. Her hands shook. Adam heard the tea trembling against the lids.

“You look worse,” she said.

“So do you.”

“I spent the night in the hospital basement.”

She set the bag down and removed a ledger wrapped in brown paper. Its cover was cracked and stained. Inside, columns of names, dates, dosages, and transfers filled the pages. Some names were crossed out. Others were marked in black:

SILENCED.

Adam recognized S. Qasim and H. Nasser from Rashid’s notebook. Beside each was a date and an injection amount. Jawad’s name appeared near the back, followed by a question mark and a torn strip of paper.

“My father kept this hidden in the records annex,” Nadia said. “It was filed under flood damage. There was no flood.”

Jawad stared at the ledger. “He worked for Meridian.”

“For eleven months,” Nadia said. “Not as a hospital employee. As a contractor medic.”

She turned a page. Rashid’s handwriting filled the margin.

*Memory suppression successful. Subject retains language and motor function. Emotional triggers unpredictable.*

Below it:

*Sound may return with memory.*

Adam pulled his hand away.

“He wasn’t only treating soldiers,” Nadia said. “He was recording what happened to them. Payments, transfers, people who disappeared afterward.”

“Why?” Jawad asked.

“Because he was afraid he’d forget.”

The tailor’s machine continued below them.

Nadia unfolded a photocopy. At the top was an address on the city’s western edge, beyond the old quarter and the scrapyards. Beneath it:

MERIDIAN SOLUTIONS—LOGISTICS AND SECURITY SERVICES.

“The notebook’s stamp belongs to Meridian,” she said. “This was a front office. Officially closed eight years ago. The utilities are still active.”

Jawad’s expression hardened. “Then they’re still working.”

Adam thought of the silver compass inside the black square. “I have something else.”

His storage locker was beneath the bus station, in a row of rusting metal boxes. He had not opened it in years. The key turned reluctantly.

Inside lay old uniforms, a water-damaged dictionary, photographs, and a black digital recorder no larger than a matchbox.

Adam lifted it.

The bus station roared around him. Engines coughed. Vendors shouted. A child cried near the ticket windows. Every sound entered his skull like a separate blow.

His thumb brushed the recorder’s cracked screen.

Heat.

Dust.

Diesel.

Then his own voice, younger and steady, speaking Arabic.

“Gather the civilians in the square. Separate the men from the women and children.”

A man answered in English, calm and close.

“Tell them it’s for their protection.”

Adam staggered.

His own voice continued.

“Tell them resistance will be treated as hostile action.”

A woman screamed.

Gunfire erupted—several shots, then a burst of static. Someone shouted Jawad’s name. A child began to cry.

The recorder fell from Adam’s hand.

When he returned to himself, he was on one knee. Nadia gripped his shoulder. Jawad stood before them with his pistol drawn, scanning the station.

“What did you hear?” Jawad asked.

“The order,” Adam said. His fingers closed around the recorder. “I translated it.”

“Then we have proof.”

“We have a damaged device.”

“We have your voice.”

Adam looked at him. “It could be altered.”

“By whom?”

“I don’t know.”

Jawad crouched in front of him. “You think I made this?”

“I think I don’t know what you made.”

The station seemed to narrow around them.

“You think I survived ten years to invent a story about you?” Jawad asked.

“I think you survived ten years by becoming someone I don’t know.”

Jawad looked away.

They took the recorder to Nadia’s apartment. She connected it to an old laptop. The file was damaged but readable. A jagged waveform appeared, breaking apart beneath static. The voices came in fragments, but the words remained clear enough.

Jawad leaned toward the screen. “Send it to someone.”

“Who?” Nadia asked. “Meridian threatens journalists, buys them, or buries them.”

“Send it to all of them.”

Adam stood by the window. His hearing had not faded. Traffic hissed below. A radio played in the neighboring flat. A dog barked several streets away. Pain pulsed behind his eye.

He wanted silence more than justice.

Nadia closed the laptop. “I need to return to the hospital. Rashid’s personnel file may identify his Meridian supervisor.”

“You’re not going back,” Jawad said.

“I work there.”

“They know you helped us.”

“They know nothing.”

A car door slammed in the street.

Adam looked down. Two men stood beside a gray sedan across from the hospital entrance. One wore a maintenance jacket. The other held a phone and watched the upper windows.

Adam recognized the shaved head and the cut through the eyebrow.

His hearing sharpened. He heard the phone vibrate in the man’s hand, the scrape of a shoe against pavement.

“They found the hospital,” Adam said.

Nadia reached for her coat.

Jawad caught her wrist. “No.”

“If they take the records—”

“They’re watching the entrance.”

“There’s a service door.”

A vehicle turned into the alley below. Then footsteps sounded in the stairwell.

One set.

Then two.

Jawad drew his pistol.

“Nadia,” he said, “the back window.”

She froze.

A fist struck the apartment door. The wood flexed. Adam heard a man outside say something too low for Nadia to catch.

He heard every syllable.

“Open up.”

The sound cut through him. He seized the recorder and closed his hand around it.

Jawad looked at him. “Can you move?”

Adam nodded, though the room tilted.

They climbed onto the narrow rear balcony as the door began to splinter. Below, the alley lay in shadow, cluttered with crates and rusted pipes. Nadia swung one leg over the railing.

Behind them, the lock gave way.

Adam heard the men enter.

He heard a weapon’s safety click off.

And beneath everything, faint but unmistakable, he heard his younger voice translating the order again.

## Chapter 7: The Day We Left

The safety clicked.

Adam heard it as clearly as a bell beside his skull.

Nadia gasped. Jawad’s breath tightened. Behind them, the apartment door splintered under another blow.

And beneath those sounds, impossibly near, another voice continued speaking.

“Gather them in the square.”

His own voice.

Not the rough voice he used now, but the younger one preserved on the recorder: precise, obedient, carrying an order from one language into another.

Jawad gripped his shoulder. “Adam. Look at me.”

Adam did.

“Go,” Jawad said.

The door broke inward. A shot struck the wall. Nadia clutched the recorder to her chest as they moved toward the narrow balcony.

Then the city vanished.

Ten years earlier, Barqa lay beneath a white, pitiless sky.

The convoy entered the village shortly after noon: three armored trucks and a utility vehicle, all coated in dust. Adam sat between Jawad and Corporal Nasser, his translation kit knocking against his hip.

They had been told there was a bomb maker in the village, a man named Faris. The operation was supposed to be simple: search the houses, find the suspect, and leave.

Jawad leaned close. “You think there’s a bomb maker?”

“I think someone wanted us to come here.”

“That is not the same answer.”

The convoy stopped in the village square. Mud-brick houses pressed around it. A mosque with a green dome stood beyond the rooftops. Children watched from beside a wall, and women emerged from doorways, gathering their children behind them.

Then the Meridian vehicles arrived.

They had no national markings, only black squares bearing a silver compass. Four contractors climbed out wearing new body armor and carrying weapons cleaner than the soldiers’ rifles.

Their leader removed his sunglasses.

Adam remembered the narrow mouth and the silver watch. He did not know the man’s name yet.

The army captain approached him, received a folder, read it, and stepped away.

“Change of command,” the contractor said in English.

He turned to Adam. “You’re the translator?”

“Yes.”

“Then make yourself useful.”

The search lasted twenty minutes. They found no explosives, no bomb maker, and no weapons except two rusted hunting rifles. The house named in the report belonged to an old man who could barely stand.

The contractor surveyed the empty rooms and shrugged.

“Tell them to assemble in the square,” he said. “All adults. Men separate from women and children.”

Adam stared at him.

“For their protection,” the man added. “Resistance will be treated as hostile action.”

Adam translated.

The villagers obeyed because he spoke their language. He told the men to stand on one side and the women and children on the other. A boy in a red shirt held a tin wheel that clicked as he turned it. His mother kept one hand around his wrist.

Jawad moved beside Adam.

“What are they doing?”

“I don’t know.”

“You know.”

A contractor struck an old man with his rifle. Jawad stepped between them.

The contractor raised the weapon again.

The Meridian leader gave an order.

Adam translated automatically. “Lower your weapon. Step away.”

Jawad looked at him, disbelieving.

The leader pointed toward a narrow lane. “Take the women and children to the eastern road.”

Adam understood then.

Jawad did too. He seized the contractor’s rifle and shoved him back, then began moving the family toward the lane.

“Stop!” the leader shouted.

Adam translated the command.

Jawad kept going.

The square erupted.

Gunfire hammered against the walls. Men dropped. Women screamed. The boy in red disappeared beneath the bodies, and his tin wheel rolled through the dust.

Adam kept speaking because the words had become a machine inside him.

“Move! Get down!”

Jawad reached the lane. Three children ran ahead of him. The woman carrying a baby stumbled.

Then the utility truck exploded.

The blast threw Adam against the ground. Heat crossed his back. Dust filled his mouth. For a moment the world became light without sound.

He pushed himself up.

The truck had folded against a wall. One wheel spun above the wreckage. Fire crawled along the canvas roof.

Jawad was inside.

His legs were trapped beneath the twisted cab. Blood ran from his forehead. His mouth moved, but Adam heard nothing.

Then Jawad struck the broken windshield.

“Adam!”

The voice came from far away.

Adam ran toward him.

A contractor seized his vest. “Fall back!”

Adam tore free. Jawad’s hand reached through the shattered window, slick with blood. Adam caught it.

Behind him, Sulaiman shouted another order. Adam knew the voice now. Sulaiman.

“Fall back, translator!”

The flames climbed beneath the dashboard. Someone fired toward the houses. More explosions shook the road.

Jawad tightened his grip.

“Don’t leave me!”

Adam looked at him. He could pull. He could stay.

Then Sulaiman shoved him backward.

Adam let go.

He ran.

He ran past the square, past the bodies, past the boy’s red shirt and the tin wheel turning in the dust. He ran because the order was clear. He ran because the road was open. He ran because survival felt like another command he could obey.

Behind him, the truck burned.

The blast swallowed the last sound in the world.

Adam woke choking.

He was on the floor above the tailor shop, tangled in a blanket, his shirt soaked through. Gray light seeped around the shutters.

For several seconds he did not know where he was.

Then sound rushed into him.

Water dripped inside the wall. A pipe knocked twice. The building creaked. Somewhere below, a sewing machine began its rapid chatter. A scooter coughed in the street. A dog barked in a distant alley.

Nadia was crying softly.

Jawad stood near the door, breathing hard.

Adam heard his own heart battering his ribs.

And beneath everything, clear after ten years, Jawad’s voice said, “Don’t leave me.”

Adam pressed both hands over his ears. The sound did not lessen.

He sobbed for the village, for the people he had gathered beneath the rifles, for the child in red, and for the hand he had released.

Jawad came closer but did not touch him.

“Adam,” he said.

Adam heard the name.

He heard all of it now: the dripping water, the breathing, the waking city, and his own grief.

“I left you,” Adam whispered.

Jawad said nothing.

In the dark, Adam listened to the world he had spent seven years believing was gone.

## Chapter 8: The Price of Sound

By noon, Adam could hear the building breathing.

Water moved through the pipes. The tailor’s machine rattled below. Voices rose in the alley, each word cutting cleanly through the next. Covering his ears did nothing. Sound traveled through bone and teeth, filling him until he could scarcely think.

Nadia had spread the recorder, Mansour’s notebook, and the photocopies across the table. Jawad stood at the window, watching the street.

“We can’t stay here,” Nadia said.

A gray-haired doctor entered without knocking. Jawad drew his pistol.

“He’s with me,” Jawad said.

Dr. Youssef Darzi opened a leather case. Inside lay a glass syringe wrapped in cloth.

“Mansour told me about you,” he said to Adam. “The treatment worked too well.”

Adam’s headache tightened. A pipe groaned in the wall. Somewhere below, a spoon struck a cup.

“What treatment?”

“The memory suppressant. It damaged more than memory. Your hearing was part of the injury.”

Darzi lifted the syringe. “This can quiet the auditory pathways. Perhaps permanently. It may also reduce the memories—the images, the emotional force.”

“Bury them,” Nadia said.

Darzi did not answer.

Adam looked at the needle. Silence had been a wall between him and Barqa, but also a cell. He had lived inside it because the alternative was hearing Jawad’s voice beneath the burning truck.

A faint click came from outside.

Adam turned. “Down.”

The window exploded.

Jawad fired as glass scattered across the room. Darzi dropped behind the table. Two men rushed from the stairwell, one in a maintenance jacket, the other shaved-headed with a cut through his eyebrow.

Adam heard the second man’s shoe scrape before he saw him. He swung a chair into the attacker’s knees. The man fell. Jawad shot toward the stairs, forcing the other gunman back.

“Rear balcony!” Jawad shouted.

They climbed over the railing. Bullets struck the wall behind them. Nadia dropped onto an awning, followed by Darzi. Adam landed on stacked crates, pain jolting through his ankle.

A van turned into the alley.

“Left!” Adam said.

Jawad trusted him. They ran through a passage between shuttered shops as bullets tore through a fruit stand. The backstreets swallowed them: laundry overhead, shuttered windows, soot-dark walls. Adam heard boots behind them, a radio issuing clipped orders, and the van grinding against a corner.

“Three behind us,” he said. “One on the roof.”

Jawad dragged Nadia into a doorway and fired upward. A body struck corrugated metal.

Adam stumbled and vomited into the gutter. Every sound had become unbearable—Nadia’s breathing, Darzi’s case striking the pavement, the distant call to prayer twisted beneath gunfire.

Jawad crouched beside him. “Take the injection.”

For one moment Adam imagined silence returning. No gunshots. No remembered cries. No child’s tin wheel rolling through dust.

Then he saw his own hand releasing Jawad’s.

“No,” he said.

Jawad’s expression hardened. “You’ll break.”

“I left them once.” Adam forced himself upright. “I’m not leaving them again.”

They reached the basement beneath an abandoned print shop after dusk. Darzi examined Adam’s pupils while Nadia connected the recorder to a laptop.

The file played in fragments.

Adam’s younger voice translated the order to gather the villagers. Sulaiman answered in a calm, measured tone. Then came gunfire, screaming, static, and someone shouting Jawad’s name.

“We send it everywhere,” Nadia said. “Journalists, human-rights groups, foreign stations.”

“First we authenticate it,” Darzi replied.

“First we survive,” Jawad said.

Nadia began copying the file. The laptop chimed.

Adam heard a door open above them.

Jawad raised his pistol.

Nadia’s phone chimed next. She looked at the screen and went pale.

The photograph showed the hospital records room. The cameras were dead. Mansour’s ledger lay open on the desk.

A message appeared beneath it:

COME TO THE WESTERN OFFICE, OR SHE DISAPPEARS.

Another photograph loaded. Nadia, seen from behind, walked beside a black vehicle. Her hands were bound.

Adam looked at the Nadia standing beside the table.

She stared at the image. “That was taken earlier.”

A third message arrived.

The photograph showed a woman in Nadia’s coat being forced through a hospital corridor. Her face was blurred.

“They took her while we were escaping,” Jawad said. “Or they prepared this before.”

“They want us at the office,” Nadia whispered.

Jawad shut the laptop.

Beyond the basement walls, engines moved through the city. Footsteps passed overhead. Adam heard the trap closing around them.

He picked up the recorder.

“Then we make them hear us first,” he said.

## Chapter 9: The Fugitive's Mirror

The western office stood behind a corrugated wall, its windows blind with dust. The silver-compass emblem had been scraped from the gate, but electricity still hummed inside.

Adam felt the vibration through his boots before he heard the generator. Sound entered him everywhere now—through bone, teeth, the thin bones behind his eyes. Jawad crouched beside the service door, studying a ring of stolen keys.

“Nadia is in there,” he whispered.

“You’re sure?”

“No.” Jawad glanced at the dark building. “But they want us to believe she is.”

“And if she isn’t?”

“Then we find out.”

They slipped inside.

The office smelled of hot wiring, mold, and old paper. Emergency lights glowed along the corridor. Adam heard the electric pulse in the walls, Jawad’s sleeve brushing the plaster, his own heart beating too hard.

A man in a maintenance jacket leaned over a security console at the corridor’s end. Jawad struck him behind the ear. Adam caught the falling body and lowered it quietly.

The man’s radio hissed.

“Two inside,” a voice said. “Check the west stairwell.”

Jawad looked at Adam.

“Not alone,” Adam said.

They moved through rooms filled with monitors, sealed crates, and files stamped with the black square and silver compass. One screen showed the hospital corridor. Another showed Nadia’s apartment. A third showed Adam entering the hospital days before.

He stopped.

“They watched us before the message.”

“Keep moving.”

They found Nadia in a conference room beneath a flickering fluorescent tube. Her wrists were tied to a chair, her cheek swollen, but she was conscious.

“Adam.”

Her voice struck him with painful clarity.

He knelt beside her. “Are you hurt?”

“Only enough to be angry.” She nodded toward the wall. “They want you alive.”

The wall opened.

A man stepped through in a dark suit, his silver watch catching the light. Adam knew the narrow mouth, the controlled eyes. Memory supplied dust, gunfire, and a command spoken without urgency.

“Sulaiman,” Jawad said.

Sulaiman studied him. “You were always good with faces.”

“You left me in a canal.”

“I left many things in that village.”

Adam’s hearing surged. The pump beneath the building groaned. Nadia’s breathing quickened. The fluorescent tube buzzed inside his skull.

Sulaiman drew a glass syringe from his coat.

“You have been suffering,” he said. “Mansour warned me what the treatment might do. He was sentimental about consequences.”

“Where is the woman in Nadia’s coat?” Adam asked.

“Alive.”

“And the people of Barqa?”

“Dead.”

Jawad raised his pistol.

“Don’t,” Adam said.

Sulaiman’s gaze remained on him. “He has spent ten years rehearsing this moment.”

Jawad’s finger tightened.

“You know what happens if you shoot me,” Sulaiman said. “You become exactly what you claim I am.”

“You killed them.”

“I gave an order. You carried weapons. Khalil translated it.”

The square returned in fragments: the dust, the old man falling, his younger voice speaking English.

For their protection. Resistance will be treated as hostile action.

Sulaiman extended the syringe toward Adam.

“Take this. Let the memories sink where they belong. The hearing will quiet. The guilt will become a dream without edges. You can leave, change your name, live.”

Nadia strained against the bindings. “He’s lying.”

“Of course,” Sulaiman said. “But not about the silence.”

Adam stared at the syringe. He imagined waking inside his old quiet, free of gunfire and Jawad’s voice, free of the child crying in the hospital corridor. He imagined being released from the red shirt vanishing among the bodies.

Then he looked at Jawad.

There was no justice in his eyes. Only revenge, sharpened by ten years of captivity and survival. Jawad had become a weapon because it was the only way to remain alive.

“Lower it,” Adam said.

Jawad did not move.

“He deserves to die,” Jawad said.

“Yes.” Adam swallowed against the noise. “But he doesn’t get to choose what we become.”

For a moment, Jawad looked at him as if hearing an old language.

The conference-room windows exploded.

Gunfire tore through the room. Adam dropped, covering his ears, though the shots traveled through his hands and teeth. Sulaiman disappeared behind the wall. Jawad overturned Nadia’s chair and shielded her.

“Move!”

The recorder lay on a table beside the Meridian files. Adam seized it. A bullet struck the tabletop.

Jawad cut Nadia’s bindings and dragged her toward the service door. Adam followed, hearing boots rush along the corridor and radios bark orders.

At the stairwell, Jawad shoved Nadia ahead.

A shot cracked.

Jawad staggered. Blood spread beneath his ribs.

“Jawad!”

“I’m fine.” His voice was thin. “Go.”

Adam caught him beneath the arm, but Jawad pushed him away and fired down the corridor. The attackers fell back.

“Do you have the recorder?” Jawad asked.

Adam held it up.

“Make them hear.”

Another burst tore through the wall. Jawad stepped into the open, shooting again while Adam and Nadia ran down the stairs.

Adam heard every shot behind him. He heard Nadia sob once, the slap of their feet on concrete, his own breath breaking apart.

Then Jawad’s breathing stopped.

Outside, dawn had not yet reached the city. They fled between shuttered shops and overflowing drains, beneath balconies crowded with laundry and satellite dishes. Adam looked back once.

Jawad was gone.

The recorder dug into his palm. The man he had abandoned had saved him again. No life Adam lived could repay that debt. He could only carry it, testify with it, let the truth cut him open whenever it demanded blood.

Behind them, the Meridian office burned in the dark.

Adam could still hear the city.

He could still hear everything.

## Chapter 10: What the Silence Hides

By the time Adam and Nadia reached the safe apartment, dawn had begun to pale the sky.

They climbed six flights through a stairwell smelling of dust, boiled coffee, and leaking drains. Nadia held one arm against her ribs. Adam carried the recorder beneath his shirt and the Meridian files in a plastic bag at his waist. Every footstep struck inside his skull.

He could hear pipes ticking in the walls, a rat scratching behind plaster, Nadia’s uneven breathing. Beneath those sounds came the others: rifle fire, a woman screaming, Jawad calling from the stairwell.

Go.

The apartment belonged to Nadia’s cousin, who had fled the city and left no furniture except a mattress, a broken fan, and a table covered in newspapers. Nadia locked the door and leaned against it.

“Is he coming?” she asked.

Adam set the recorder on the table.

“I heard him stop,” he said.

Nadia lowered her eyes. Neither of them spoke. The city pressed against the windows. Adam heard a truck changing gears three streets away, Nadia swallowing, his own pulse stumbling in his ears.

Then he took out the phone they had stolen from Meridian.

“There’s a journalist,” Nadia said. “Leila Haddad. She published the hospital files.”

“Will she believe us?”

“She doesn’t have to. She has to hear it.”

Nadia connected the recorder with a frayed cable. The first attempt failed. The second released a burst of static so fierce Adam clapped his hands over his ears.

For an instant he was back in Barqa: dust in his mouth, bodies in the road, Sulaiman’s calm voice ordering him to speak.

Nadia gripped his wrist. Her lips moved, but he could not understand her.

“Again,” Adam said.

The third attempt opened the file.

His younger voice filled the room, formal and careful in Arabic. He heard the pauses where he had searched for words that might make the order less terrible.

Gather the adults. Separate the men. Tell them resistance will be treated as hostile action.

Then Sulaiman’s voice:

For their protection.

Gunfire followed.

Nadia began to cry silently. Adam heard every breath.

Leila answered on the fourth call. Nadia described the recorder, the files, Rashid Mansour’s ledger, and the Meridian office. Leila demanded proof the audio had not been manufactured.

Adam placed his hand over the phone.

“I translated it,” he said.

Nadia repeated him.

There was silence on the line. Then Leila asked, “Is it authentic?”

“Yes,” Adam said. “I was there.”

They sent copies through three encrypted channels. Nadia attached photographs of the ledger, Meridian’s payments, hospital transfers, and the surveillance screens from the office. Adam added statements in Arabic and English, naming himself as the translator who had carried out Sulaiman’s order.

When he pressed send, his hand shook.

There was no relief. Only a thin ringing behind his left eye.

By noon, Meridian’s offices were being raided. By evening, the story had crossed the border. News channels played fragments of the recording beneath images of the burned building. Meridian first denied involvement, then blamed rogue contractors, then claimed Sulaiman had left the company years earlier.

The lie lasted four hours.

A photograph of Sulaiman from the recovered files led to his arrest at a northern checkpoint. He was carrying a passport under another name. On television, he walked between two officers with his head lowered, his mouth drawn into the same narrow line Adam remembered from Barqa.

Adam felt no satisfaction.

The following morning, fire crews searching the Meridian office found a body beneath the collapsed stairwell. The face was damaged, but the scar along the jaw remained. So did the crescent mark on the right thumb.

Nadia identified him.

Adam did not go to the morgue. He had already seen Jawad’s last moments in the dark: one hand braced against the wall, the other firing toward the stairs, his breath growing ragged.

Go.

The body only made the command final.

For two days, Dr. Darzi kept the syringe in a locked drawer. On the third, he placed it between them in the examination room.

“You can still choose,” he said.

Adam listened to the fluorescent lights buzz. He heard footsteps in the corridor, a nurse laughing softly, a patient coughing behind a closed door. There was nowhere inside the world to hide now.

“If I take it, will I forget him?”

“Perhaps. Not completely.”

“Barqa?”

“Some of it.”

“The sounds?”

Darzi looked at the syringe. “The worst may fade.”

Adam remembered the silence that had protected him: not peace, but a locked room. Inside it he had worked, eaten, slept, and told himself that surviving meant he was innocent. Now Jawad reached him every second.

“Then I won’t take it.”

Darzi nodded. “You understand what that means?”

Adam looked toward the window.

“It means I’m responsible for what I hear.”

Before dawn, he climbed to the hospital roof.

The city lay beneath him in concrete layers and shadow. A bus coughed awake. Shop shutters rattled open. Somewhere a kettle whistled; somewhere else a woman called a child back from the street.

Then the muezzin began.

The call rose over the rooftops, clear and human, passing through broken windows and unfinished buildings. Adam listened until the final note thinned into morning.

A car horn answered. A broom scraped pavement. Two men argued over bread. From a neighboring roof came a child’s laughter.

And beneath everything, forever, Barqa.

The gunfire. The crying. His own voice giving the order. Jawad calling his name.

Adam closed his eyes.

He could not unhear any of it. That was the price. Truth was not a door one opened and left behind. It was a room carried inside the body.

Nadia found him there with a folder under her arm.

“The rights group wants a statement,” she said. “They need translators for the survivors.”

“For the army?”

“For the people who survived the army.”

Below them, the city continued waking.

Adam took the folder. His hands trembled, but they did not let go.

For seven years, he had mistaken silence for forgiveness. Now every voice demanded an answer.

He would learn to give one.
