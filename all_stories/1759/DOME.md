The emergency lamp flickered twice, leaving the screw between Sami Darwish’s fingers briefly invisible. He held still until the red light returned. Beneath his other palm, the generator’s vibration weakened, shuddered through the concrete wall, and stopped.

He waited for it to come back.

A thin crack crossed the ceiling above his workbench. Water had spread around it in overlapping brown rings, each marking a winter when nobody had repaired the roof. In the red light, the stains looked almost black.

Sami set the screw in a shallow tin and covered the open infusion pump with a cloth. Without power, he could clean its corroded contacts, but he could not test the repair. He wiped his fingers against his trousers. Dust remained in the creases around his nails.

His phone showed 00:00 on the stopwatch he used to measure outages. He started it.

Across the courtyard, windows went dark. A nurse appeared behind the glass of the connecting corridor, raised both hands toward the ceiling, then disappeared. Sami watched for the green indicator above the operating rooms. It stayed lit. The separate battery supply was holding.

He put his screwdriver into his breast pocket and took the torch.

At the damaged wing’s entrance, someone had fastened a demolition notice over an older notice forbidding entry. The new paper was already curling in the damp. Forty-eight hours until clearance began. Rooms, fittings, remaining records: everything had to be removed or formally abandoned.

He photographed it. Nobody had mentioned the records to him.

Beyond the broken courtyard wall, the backstreets were waking into their evening trade. A baker carried flat trays through a doorway, using his hip to push aside a hanging blanket. Children stepped around a drain running between houses built from concrete block and salvaged doors. Displaced families rented single rooms above the repair shops; former combatants sat outside them on plastic chairs, some still wearing army trousers gone pale at the knees. A man with an artificial leg steadied a bicycle while its owner worked the pump.

Sami could see their mouths moving. The street reached him through gestures, dust, and the warmth escaping the bakery.

He turned toward the former military communications room.

The hospital had reclaimed most of the wing after the war, but this room remained a store for things nobody wanted to sign for. Shelves held cracked batteries, patient registers, coils of cable, and archive boxes swollen by damp. Sami had been asked to salvage anything useful before the clearance crew arrived.

He braced the door with a brick. His torch picked out a field telephone beneath a bundle of straps.

The handset lay beside it in a transparent evidence bag. Its label read: RECOVERED—LOWER STAIRWELL. The date beneath had blurred, but he could still make out the month.

His last month on duty.

He lowered himself onto a chair and opened the bag.

One side of the handset was crushed. The receiver grille had lost three slats, and the cable ended in a frayed stump. He turned it over, testing the split casing with his thumb. Something loose shifted inside.

Then a man said, “The civilians must remain inside the lower-stairwell evacuation point.”

Sami dropped it.

The handset struck his knee and landed among the straps.

He gripped the edge of the desk. His torch beam trembled across the wall, climbed, and settled on an empty nail.

There had been a voice.

He waited, his jaw clenched so hard that pain spread toward his ear. Nothing followed. No scrape of his shoe as he moved it. No sound from the chair beneath him. He pressed two fingers against his throat and spoke.

The familiar vibration touched his fingertips. His voice remained beyond his reach.

Slowly, he picked up the handset.

“Repeat,” he said, shaping the word carefully.

Nothing.

He checked the cable again. Four short wires protruded from the cut sheath. There was no connector, no battery compartment, no concealed attachment. Through the break in the casing he could see a bent contact and a fragment of green board.

He held it against his ear.

“Do not send them into the street.”

His stomach tightened.

He knew those words. During his last wartime shift, they had come through a radio while he stood beside an evacuation list, waiting for permission to move the families assembled downstairs. The order on the list had been to clear the building. The warning had made him stop with his pencil above the first name.

He remembered asking who was speaking.

He remembered reaching for the transmit switch.

What came afterward returned in pieces: a door thrown sideways, grit between his teeth, a hand pressing cloth against his head.

The voice now was unfamiliar. He could not place its owner among the faces he remembered. Yet the phrasing was exact, down to the awkward designation nobody in the hospital used anymore: lower-stairwell evacuation point.

Sami put the handset back in its bag, then took it out again. If he sealed it away, he feared he would persuade himself nothing had happened.

He found Leila in the treatment room beside the courtyard.

She sat with a ledger open across her knees. A mug stood untouched on the floor. The skin beneath her eyes looked bruised, and she had pinned one sleeve above a dressing on her forearm. When he entered, she lifted a finger for a moment to finish counting.

He waited within her sight.

She closed the ledger.

“Generator?” she signed.

“Stopped. Operating rooms still have power.”

Her shoulders lowered slightly. Then she noticed what he carried.

He placed the handset on the desk and signed, “I heard someone.”

Her eyes moved to his face.

“Through this.”

She glanced at the severed cable, then back at him. “You felt it?”

“Heard.” He touched his ear, irritated by the inadequacy of the gesture. “Words.”

Her hands became very still.

Sami took out his phone. Typing let him preserve the sentence without watching doubt enter her expression.

The civilians must remain inside the lower-stairwell evacuation point. Do not send them into the street.

He passed it to her.

Leila read it twice. Her thumb pressed against the edge of the phone.

“When?” she signed.

“Just now. Twice. I heard it during the evacuation too. Before I was injured.”

She pulled the chair opposite hers closer with her foot.

“My brother was there,” she signed. “Younis. That was where he disappeared.”

Sami nodded. He knew she had lost him during the evacuation. He had never known which part of the building.

“Can you help me identify the speaker?”

“Yes.”

He showed her the photograph of the demolition notice.

Leila enlarged it, read the deadline, and rose. She reached for the handset, then paused until he put it into her hand.

“Show me where you found it,” she signed.

Sami led Leila back through the damaged wing, keeping the handset tucked against his ribs. The emergency light still burned at the corridor’s far end, thin and red. Its glow caught the damp marks on the walls and the pale dust on his hands. Without the generator’s vibration, the building felt less quiet than emptied: the pipes and doorframes seemed to hold their breath.

Leila walked close enough to see his hands. She was tired; he could tell by the careful economy of her movements, the way she favored her dressed arm and kept her eyes on the floor where loose tiles might shift. But when they reached the communications room, she set down her phone and began examining the place with the brisk attention of someone already arranging a list in her mind.

Sami placed the handset on the scarred table. He had checked its cable before bringing it to her. The outer jacket was split near the base, exposing copper strands, and the line ended without a plug. A bent contact showed through the open casing. Beside it, caught beneath the metal frame, lay a fragment of green circuit board.

Leila leaned closer. “This was recovered from here?”

He nodded and pointed to the shelf where he had found it. The old labels had peeled from their drawers, leaving rectangles of cleaner paint. A register sat under a layer of grit, its spine swollen from water. He opened it carefully. The first pages stuck together, and one tore when he eased them apart.

“Can you remember exactly what you heard?” she signed.

He took out his phone. The sentence was there, typed before they left the treatment room.

The civilians must remain inside the lower-stairwell evacuation point. Do not send them into the street.

Leila read it without moving. Then she looked up at him. “That’s close to Younis’s wording.”

“Close?”

“He used to say ‘keep them inside’ when he was giving instructions. But this could be someone repeating the order.”

Sami watched her hands. She was being precise, not gentle. He trusted the difference.

The voice he had heard through the handset had not given him a name. The damaged connection had carried the words, not the room around them. There had been no answering voice, no clear repetition when he tried to listen again. He remembered a voice shaped by distance, unfamiliar enough that he could not claim it, familiar enough in its cadence to keep returning to him. The sentence was sharper than the speaker.

He wrote beneath the warning: Heard during last wartime shift. Speaker identity unknown.

Leila read the note. “You asked who was speaking?”

He nodded.

“And then?”

Sami’s fingers tightened against the phone. He remembered reaching toward the transmit switch. He remembered the door thrown sideways, a hard change in the room’s light, and faces turned toward him. He did not remember what any of them had said. The recollection had edges, but no sound. He had sealed it away because he feared he would persuade himself nothing had happened.

“Then the door was hit,” he typed. “I was injured before I could answer.”

Leila’s expression changed, but only briefly. She did not reach for him. “We need the records.”

They searched the drawers nearest the table. Most held old wire, corroded screws, and paper labels too stained to read. In a shallow cabinet, Sami found a bundle of recovery tags tied with cotton string. The top one had a date and a room number, but the ink had run. Another tag bore the outline of a stamp and half a word: LOWER.

Leila examined the bundle while Sami returned to the register. A page near the back listed equipment removed from the wing after the evacuation. The columns were ruled in faded blue. He traced the entries with one finger until he found a line marked handset, unassigned, recovered. The date was legible. The room number was not.

It proved little. Still, it gave them a place to begin.

Leila turned to the demolition notice on her phone, enlarging the photograph they had seen earlier. Sami leaned in to read the bold date. Forty-eight hours. After that, the damaged wing and its neglected archive would be cleared.

“They’ll empty the archive first,” she signed. “If anything is still there, it may be boxed or destroyed.”

Sami looked from the notice to the register. The handset might have been left here by accident, or stored after recovery, or carried into the room by someone who needed it. The entries could narrow those possibilities. The evacuation orders might show who used the lower stairwell, who had authority to hold civilians there, and whether the warning he remembered belonged to the official record or to something else.

Leila glanced at his notes. “You think the order kept them there.”

“I remember being told to hold them.” He typed, then added: “I believed it was the evacuation order.”

The words looked too clean on the screen. He remembered the mouths he had seen afterward in the backstreets, moving around questions he could not hear. For years, he had crossed those streets for wire, switches, and salvaged parts: past a bakery that opened before dawn, a repair shop with its shutters patched in different metals, families displaced from one block and former combatants from another. People lived beside the aftermath as they lived beside cracked pavement, stepping around it because there was no other route.

Younis had disappeared during that evacuation. The same lower stairwell might have held him, or the warning might have been spoken by someone who never saw him. Sami could not make the words settle into an answer merely because Leila wanted her brother back. He knew that want. He had carried his own version of it without giving it a name.

Leila slid the register toward him. “We find the order first. Then we compare it with what you remember.”

Sami picked up the recovery tag and placed it beside the handwritten sentence. The broken handset, the partial record, and the memory offered no proof alone. Together, they made a trail, narrow but real.

He typed their immediate task into his phone: Trace the handset. Find the evacuation order. Start with the instruction that kept civilians inside the lower stairwell.

Leila read it and nodded. Outside the room, the red emergency light washed over the open doorway. The archive waited somewhere beyond the corridor, with less than two days left before its contents were cleared.

The blackout reduced Al-Rahma to red emergency lamps and the pale square of Leila’s phone. Sami followed her downstairs, the broken handset wrapped in his jacket. Beyond the corridor windows, candles appeared in buildings he knew by daylight but could scarcely distinguish now.

The maps lay in a cabinet beneath obsolete intake forms. Leila set her light on the table while Sami unrolled three evacuation plans, weighting their corners with empty stamp holders.

The lower stairwell appeared on all three. On the oldest, it was called the south access. On the newest, a black line ran from it toward the hospital boundary.

Sami placed his phone beside the maps. The warning he had transcribed remained on its screen.

Leila read it, then pointed to a blue rectangle near the southern wall.

“An entrance?”

“An awning,” he typed. “There used to be one.”

“Where exactly?”

He studied the map. He had passed beneath that awning, but memory supplied no reliable distance from the corner. He would not turn familiarity into certainty.

They found a maintenance sketch folded inside another plan. It showed the awning’s supports between a drainpipe and the south service door. A penciled arrow connected the doorway to the lower stairwell.

“That narrows it,” Leila said, making sure he could see her mouth. “We check outside.”

They left through the service gate. In the backstreets, people stood in doorways holding lamps. A baker carried a tray toward an oven whose glow reached the pavement. Sami saw a child laughing beside a stalled generator, but heard nothing.

These were streets he had crossed for years to buy wire and salvaged switches. Tonight he studied them as if someone had laid a second district beneath the familiar one.

At the south wall, Leila raised her light.

Two rusted brackets projected above a recessed doorway. Between them, a scrap of blue fabric had hardened around a bolt. The drainpipe matched the maintenance sketch. Beneath peeling limewash, an evacuation arrow pointed through the doorway.

Inside, steps descended toward a landing blocked with fallen plaster.

Leila compared the sketch with the opening, then wrote beneath their copied warning: South entrance; lower stairwell route. Location supported by maps and surviving fixtures.

She handed the notebook to Sami. He added: Does not identify the speaker or establish who was below.

When he unwrapped the handset, he felt a faint vibration against his palm.

He could set it down. The thought came with a sudden, almost childish relief. They had found the entrance without listening again.

Then the vibration returned.

He showed Leila the device.

“Wait,” she said.

He watched her mouth, understanding.

“I know,” he typed. “But we need whatever comes next.”

He raised the receiver.

Static opened into a voice.

“South access, beside the blue awning. Keep the passage open. Await further instruction.”

A pause followed, filled with distant interference. Sami held still, afraid that movement might break the connection.

“Who is speaking?” he asked.

The voice continued without hesitation.

“Message repeats. South access, beside the blue awning. Keep the passage open.”

It was not responding. The words followed their own fixed order, a transmission proceeding through minutes already spent.

Then he heard a shoe scrape.

Not through the receiver. Beside him.

Leila had shifted her foot on the grit. Beyond the doorway, someone dragged a shutter downward. Metal rattled against stone. A dog barked twice, and the sound struck him with such force that he turned toward it.

Leila caught his expression.

“You hear that?”

“Yes.”

His own voice sounded unfamiliar.

For several minutes the street surrounded him: a generator laboring, footsteps passing, Leila breathing close by. He listened greedily, trying to hold each sound separately.

Then the generator receded without slowing. Leila’s next words reached him only as movement. The handset hiss diminished to nothing.

He lowered it.

Leila wrote down the fragment while he could still repeat it precisely. Beneath it she added his question and the fact that the voice had continued unchanged.

Sami reached for a memory to steady himself: his mother at the kitchen table, guiding his hands through his first repair.

Nothing answered.

He knew the appliance had been a fan. He knew he had overtightened a screw and she had loosened it before letting him try again. Those facts remained as plainly as entries in a ledger.

But he could not enter the afternoon.

There had been warmth in her hand over his. He knew this because he had remembered it earlier that day. Now the statement meant no more than a description someone else had given him.

He sat on the step.

Leila crouched before him. “What happened?”

He typed slowly, testing each sentence against the absence.

“My mother taught me to repair a fan. I still know that. I cannot remember being there.”

Leila waited while he tried again. He closed his eyes, recalled the table’s chipped edge, the screw, the fan’s wire guard. None brought her back.

“Write the facts,” she said.

He did. Writing preserved what he knew, but restored nothing.

Under the transcription they recorded the temporary hearing, its fading, and the loss that followed. Sami added that he had chosen to listen, not chosen what it would take.

They could not use it to question the dead. They could not mistake what it said for documentary proof.

Leila put the notebook away.

“Independent records now,” she said.

Above the ruined steps, the blue scrap stirred in a wind Sami could no longer hear. They had located the warning. Next they needed records of who had occupied the stairwell and when the clearance had been issued.

By morning, dust had settled into the creases of their evacuation map. Leila held it against the hospital’s rear wall while Sami marked the passage they had just walked. The south entrance opened onto a narrow street with no shelter beyond the shallow recess of a shuttered bakery. Anyone leaving the stairwell would have had to cross it.

Yesterday, the blue scrap above the steps had seemed almost weightless. Now Sami understood what its position established: Younis had been warning about this exit, and the people beneath it.

Inside the archive, Leila found a ward diagram folded into a register. A pencil arrow led from the basement shelter to the south stairs. Beside it someone had written: HOLD HERE UNTIL STREET CONFIRMED.

She pushed it toward him.

The archive smelled of damp cardboard and plaster. Beyond the windows, workers were stacking empty crates beneath the loading canopy. A removal notice on the door gave them until the following morning. Some shelves had already been stripped; pale rectangles marked where labels had protected the wood.

They returned to the backstreets to check a second passage shown on the diagram. A later wall blocked it. Sami pressed his palm against the mortar, then shook his head. During the evacuation, this had offered no alternative either: the diagram itself showed the passage crossed out.

The handset stirred inside his coat.

He stopped.

Leila saw his hand close around it. She opened the notebook but did not offer it immediately. Her eyes went to the page where he had described the loss of his mother’s hand over his.

Before leaving the archive, they had written down several memories he could still enter, hoping afterward to distinguish forgetting from the handset’s cost. One was an afternoon with his father at the river landing, years before the explosion.

Sami looked at that sentence now.

He could put the handset down. The surviving documents might be enough. But the voice had brought them to the right entrance, and somewhere in its fixed sequence there might be another detail they could test.

He wrote, “I will listen.”

Then he lifted the receiver.

A shoe scraped behind him.

The small, rough sound startled him more than the hiss. He turned and saw Leila steadying herself on the broken pavement. Somewhere beyond the wall, metal struck metal twice.

Younis’s voice came through thinly.

“I counted them below. Seventeen that I can see. More behind the turn. South stairs occupied. Stop the evacuation. The site is not empty. Repeat, not empty—”

A crackle swallowed the rest.

Sami kept listening, although he understood that waiting would not persuade the transmission to explain itself. For several breaths he heard wind worrying the receiver cord. Then the wind vanished. Leila’s lips moved in silence.

He repeated the words aloud while she wrote.

Only afterward did he test the river landing.

He knew his father had taken him there. He knew they had missed a ferry and eaten oranges while waiting for the next. On the notebook page, in his own handwriting, he had described his father pretending to count the boats incorrectly so that Sami could correct him.

An hour earlier, that remembered afternoon had amused him.

Now he examined the sentence as if another man had written it.

He tried to summon his father’s face turning toward him, the intimacy of being allowed to win their little argument. Nothing came. The facts remained, detached and serviceable.

He wrote beneath them: “This one is gone.”

Leila read it, then closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, she copied the new fragment onto a separate sheet.

They went back to the archive.

The triage note lay between two admissions sheets stuck together along a rusted staple. Leila separated them with a flat blade. Its hurried pencil recorded seventeen people held at the south stairs, with others beyond sight. At 14:17, a porter had brought word from Younis: keep them below; street unsafe.

Underneath, in darker pencil: 14:21—movement ordered despite hold. Two stretchers returned.

Sami rested his fingertips beside the words, careful not to touch them.

The damaged radio index took longer. Water had dissolved the outer edges, but the central columns survived. At 14:17, Younis’s station had reported occupation and requested a stop. At 14:19 came a clearance entry.

Leila checked the index’s time convention against the hospital telephone register. Both recorded the same generator failure that afternoon at 13:52. She wrote the correspondence down, leaving room for any uncertainty they found later.

By late afternoon, almost twenty-four hours after the blackout, they had Nasser’s damaged official summary beside the two records.

The summary placed clearance at 14:14.

Its account made Younis’s warning arrive after the decision had already been transmitted. The surviving index placed that decision two minutes after his warning. The triage note independently showed the hold, then the order to move.

Leila drew no line through Nasser’s time. She copied it exactly.

Then she turned the index leaf.

A separate relay entry survived near the binding.

14:20. S-4. CLEARANCE REPEATED.

Below it, compressed into the remarks column: QUERY RECEIVED. ORDER CONFIRMED.

S-4 had been Sami’s call sign.

For a moment he watched Leila’s finger resting beneath it and waited for some qualification to appear. A damaged numeral. Another operator’s initials. There was none.

He remembered hearing enough of the warning to question the order. He remembered his own objection, the communications room door moving sideways across his vision, the sudden change in the light. He remembered reaching toward the transmit switch.

What followed still broke apart when he tried to hold it.

He took the notebook.

“I remember asking whether they understood there were people below.”

Leila read without looking up.

“I remember being told to keep them confined until clearance. I cannot recover every sentence or the interval afterward.”

His hand slowed.

“This entry records that I repeated the clearance. I had already heard the warning. I questioned the order, and then I sent it.”

Leila pulled the notebook toward herself.

“You knew enough to ask,” she wrote. “All these years, you let me believe you knew nothing.”

He could not dispute either sentence.

Near closing, she found an inventory reference to an unaltered carbon dispatch strip: old board, east return rack, channel 31. No shelf location followed. The board room was locked beyond the damaged corridor, and the removal list gave no indication whether the rack remained there.

Leila copied the reference.

Sami added his name beneath his account. Then he placed it beside the index, where she could take both.

The demolition notice had been moved to the outer door. By morning, every room marked in red had to be emptied or formally abandoned; by noon, the crews would begin on the east wing. A pencil line under the deadline had bitten through the paper. Beyond the boarded corridor, tools knocked and scraped as workers shifted equipment.

Leila stood beside the relay index, one hand braced against the table. She had copied Nasser’s time without alteration. Beneath it, in the narrow remarks column, the relay entry remained: QUERY RECEIVED. ORDER CONFIRMED. The call sign beside it was S-4.

“You knew enough to ask,” she said.

Sami watched her mouth. She spoke quietly, but there was no softness in the words. He wrote on the back of an inventory sheet: I knew enough to question the order. I cannot remember what happened after I transmitted it.

“That is not an answer to what I said.”

He looked down at the page. The blast had left him with memories that did not join up: a voice, the communications room door shifting sideways, light breaking across the wall. The gaps were real. So was the entry in the index. He wanted to tell her that he had spent years trying to recover what was missing, that he had not known whose warning it was. But he had known there were people below. He had questioned the order, then sent it.

He began to write, I thought if I explained what I remembered—

Leila took the sheet before he finished.

“Meaning is not what I asked about. Younis warned them. You heard enough to question it, then repeated the clearance. And you let me look for a stranger.”

Her fingers pressed into the paper. Sami had no reply that would not turn his uncertainty into a defense. He watched her gaze settle again on his call sign. His name beneath it, written in the notebook, seemed less like an explanation than a second record of the act.

Footsteps approached. Nasser entered carrying a folder under his arm. His suit was neat despite the dust in the corridor, and he moved with the assurance of someone used to being given keys. Since becoming a hospital trustee, he had overseen budgets, repairs, and now the demolition. The notices bore his office’s approval.

“I hope I’m not interrupting,” he said.

“You are,” Leila replied.

Nasser placed the folder on the table. “The deadline is real. Sami, there is repair work available with the contractor. I can arrange an advance, too. You understand old communications equipment better than anyone they have.”

He faced Sami directly, speaking with deliberate care. “The official account can say you relayed an authenticated clearance as instructed. It needn’t suggest you understood the consequences or intended harm. The hospital has no interest in turning a damaged record into a public accusation.”

Leila kept her hand on the index. “That account leaves out the warning.”

“It leaves out what no one can establish,” Nasser said. “The hospital has patients to move and a building to clear. The war is over. We cannot keep every room open because a damaged handset may produce a fragment.”

The handset lay between them, its dark casing dulled by dust. A coil of wire rested beside it. Sami could still feel the pull of its sounds in his mind, though he could not say what it might give next. Nasser’s offer had the polished shape of practical kindness: money, work, an acceptable record. Beneath it was the demand. Hand over the handset. Let the archive go.

Sami took the inventory sheet and wrote: No. I will not give you the handset. I will not sign your version.

Nasser read it. “Think carefully. I’m offering you a way back to work, and a record that does not condemn you for carrying out an order.”

“An order you cleared,” Leila said.

Nasser turned toward her. “You have an index, not the full exchange. You have Sami’s uncertain recollection. Neither proves what you believe.”

“No,” she said. “They show what happened. That is enough to keep looking.”

Nasser’s expression settled into a tired patience, the sort that could make resistance seem childish. “The hospital cannot halt demolition over old accusations. What do you expect to find in a box of damaged papers?”

“Younis,” Leila said.

The name changed nothing in Nasser’s face, but he shifted the folder against his side. “The casualty records are incomplete. You may not find anything.”

“Then we’ll find what they show.”

He looked back at Sami. “You may regret refusing this.”

Sami did not answer aloud. Instead, he opened the notebook to a clean page. His hand trembled slightly, so he pressed the heel of it against the table.

He wrote:

What I remember: I heard a warning that people remained below. I questioned whether they understood this. I remember being told to keep them confined until clearance.

What the records show: The relay index identifies me as S-4. It records that I repeated the clearance at 14:20, after a query had been received and the order confirmed.

What I cannot recall: The exact words after my question, the full interval before transmission, or whether I heard any further reply.

He stopped. The categories made the page look orderly; the events had not been. Still, he would not use that disorder to blur the one fact he could not deny. He added: I repeated the clearance. I had heard the warning and questioned the order before I sent it.

Then he signed his full name and wrote the date.

He slid the notebook toward Leila without asking her to take it.

“I’m not asking you to accept this as proof that I’m innocent,” he said, slowly enough for her to follow his lips. “I’m not asking you to forgive me.”

Leila read the statement. Her expression did not soften. “I’ll help secure the records because Younis matters. His fate matters.”

She held Sami’s gaze.

“Not because I trust you.”

He nodded. The distinction hurt more than anger would have, perhaps because it was exact. Nasser gathered the folder but did not leave. Leila placed the relay index beside Sami’s account, keeping both separate from the handset.

Outside, a tool struck metal, followed by the scrape of something heavy being dragged across stone. Leila turned toward the east corridor.

Sami followed.

Story:

The corridor to the communications room smelled of damp stone and hot dust. Beyond its boarded windows, demolition crews struck metal, paused, and struck again. Each blow trembled through the floor.

Leila walked ahead with the relay index and Nasser’s folder. Sami followed, keeping the signed account in his notebook. Her words stayed between them: she would help because Younis mattered, not because she trusted him. He knew better than to ask for anything else.

The communications-room door stood open. Leila set the papers on the table beside the handset. Dust outlined the places where equipment had been removed. She faced Sami and signed, Are you ready?

He looked at the handset. The earlier fragments had not made him innocent, nor had they settled what he remembered. He could refuse to listen. Instead, he nodded.

Leila’s fingers tightened on the folder. She moved to the other side of the table, giving him room.

Sami lifted the handset to his ear. A thin crackle stirred beneath the receiver’s pressure. Then a man’s voice came through, clipped and distant: “Under brass plate thirty-one, on the east return. Keep the strip with the register.”

The line fell silent.

A hammer rang out in the corridor. Sami heard it—not merely as a pulse in the floor, but as a hard metallic crack. Grit skittered across stone; Leila’s shoe scraped as she stepped closer. His own breath caught, loud in his ears. For one brief instant the room had depth, every sound distinct and placed.

Then it all receded. The hammer dulled to a vibration. Leila was speaking, but he could no longer hear her. She signed, What did it say?

He wrote the words in his notebook. As he did, he reached for a memory of his father tuning their old radio: the brown cabinet, the illuminated dial, his father’s patient fingers guiding the knob. He could picture the room and the light on the glass. But the voice that had filled that memory was gone. Not faint or distant. Gone.

Leila watched him, her face intent, but did not ask what the listening had cost.

They went to the east return, a narrow passage behind the communications room. Brass plates marked old conduits along the wall. At number thirty-one, Leila pressed an edge that shifted beneath her fingers. They worked it loose together. A recess behind it held a strip of carbon paper, folded inside brittle cloth.

Leila supported the paper while Sami opened it on the floor. The writing had faded, but the entries remained legible. Younis had warned that people were still below and were not responding to the evacuation call. A query followed. Then came the clearance, entered later, and the relay notation for Sami, S-4, with his retransmission at 14:20.

Leila laid the relay index beside the strip. In the summary Nasser had offered, the clearance appeared to come before the warning, leaving no interval for Sami to question it. The original times showed otherwise. The warning had come first; Nasser had cleared the order afterward. Sami had questioned it, then repeated the clearance.

The strip referred to the hospital casualty return. Leila found the register in the bundle beside it and searched by date. She turned the pages carefully, their brittle edges rasping under her thumb, until she found the interpreter’s staff number. The entry identified Younis and marked him dead after the evacuation.

Leila rested one hand on the register. Her shoulders had gone rigid. Sami stared at the two records, unable to make them fit into the clean sequence he had once used to defend himself. The papers did not tell him what he had intended. They showed what he had heard, what had been decided, and what he had transmitted.

A shadow crossed the doorway. Nasser entered, his gaze moving from the open recess to the papers on the floor.

“Those are hospital records,” he said. “The rooms are being cleared. Give them to me.”

He reached toward the carbon strip. Leila covered the casualty return with her palm and raised her phone. Sami moved his notebook onto the papers, opening it to his signed statement.

Nasser paused. “You have no complete record here. These can be read in more than one way.”

“Then let them be read,” Sami said. He formed the words slowly, facing Nasser. “I repeated the clearance. My account says what I remember, what the records show, and what I cannot recall.”

Nasser’s hand came down toward the strip. Leila photographed the warning and its times, then the casualty entry, the index, and the space where the strip had been hidden. Sami turned the pages for her. When Nasser tried again to take the carbon, Leila shifted the phone and captured his hand beside the original. She photographed the altered summary against the original timing, making the discrepancy plain.

They made two sets of copies. Leila sent one beyond the hospital while Sami checked each image for legibility. He added a signed copy of his account to both sets, then dated and signed it again. The originals went into a separate folder. Nasser could still challenge them, but he could no longer quietly keep every copy in the hospital’s hands.

Outside, the tools started again, closer than before. Leila passed Sami one folder. He took it without mistaking her help for forgiveness. They gathered the remaining papers and left the communications room together, carrying the evidence before the demolition crew reached the east return.

The corridor beyond the communications room smelled of wet plaster and hot machinery. Dust drifted through the light above the stairwell, each vibration shaking a little more from the cracked ceiling. At the far end, a worker was fastening red tape across a doorway. He looked at the folders under their arms.

“East return closes at six.”

Leila checked her phone. Forty-three minutes remained. Beside the time, the message she had sent beyond the hospital still showed a single gray tick.

They had made copies. They did not yet know whether anyone else could open them.

“The records office,” she said.

Sami followed her down the stairs, keeping one hand against the wall. On the landing he stopped to catch his breath. She waited two steps below him, impatient with his slowness, then ashamed of the impatience without finding it diminished. His account was in the folder. So was his acknowledgment that he had repeated the clearance. Helping him breathe would not change either fact.

The records office occupied two rooms beside the surviving outpatient entrance. A battery lamp stood on the counter. Behind it, the records clerk was tying bundles with cotton tape.

“We’re closed,” she said.

“These need to enter the records system before the east return comes down.”

The clerk glanced at the folder. “The server keeps dropping.”

“Can you register receipt?”

“That I can do.”

Leila laid the papers beneath the lamp. Here, away from the broken windows, she could see how badly some of her photographs had caught the light. A pale reflection obscured the end of one line on the carbon strip. In another image, her thumb covered part of the casualty return’s heading.

“We do them again,” she said.

The clerk brought a sheet of white card. Sami placed the strip on it and held the corners flat while Leila photographed it from directly above. She took a second exposure with the lamp farther away. They checked both at full size before moving on.

Warning. Altered summary. Casualty return. Signed account.

Laid in that order, the documents showed a sequence without requiring Sami’s memory to supply every connection. The warning bore a time earlier than the clearance recorded in the summary. The carbon preserved words the summary omitted. The casualty return recorded a death. Sami’s statement named his own part in transmitting the clearance and distinguished what he remembered from what he had learned that morning.

“Leave the edges in,” the clerk said. “And the reverse, even if it’s blank.”

Leila photographed every page again.

At the casualty return, she stopped.

In the communications room she had found the name and felt the world narrow around it. Now she read across the entry slowly, forcing herself past the name to the age, the neighborhood, the identifying number. She took the folded search slip from her wallet, softened along its creases by years of handling.

The numbers matched.

For a moment she heard the records clerk opening a drawer as though it were happening very far away.

“It’s him,” Leila said.

Sami lowered his eyes.

“Look,” she told him.

He looked at the two numbers. “Yes.”

She wanted the confirmation and hated its smallness. After years of asking at desks and gates, she had expected certainty to feel larger. Instead there was a narrow ruled space, ink thinning toward the end of a surname, a clerk’s compressed handwriting.

The entry did not tell her who had stayed beside her brother. It did not explain why no notice had reached her mother, or where his body had gone afterward. She placed the search slip beside the return and photographed them together, then returned it to her wallet.

Sami drew his account toward him.

“I should add where we found the strip.”

“On a separate page,” the clerk said. “Don’t change what you already signed.”

He wrote the location, the date, and the circumstances of its recovery. His pen hesitated over Nasser’s name.

“Write what happened,” Leila said.

He described Nasser’s demand for the papers and the photographs taken before they left. He added that the strip had been hidden in the recess, without claiming to know who had put it there. Then he signed beneath the addition.

The lights went out.

Nobody moved until the battery lamp steadied.

From outside came a shout, followed by the cough of an engine failing to start. The clerk turned her monitor off at its switch.

“When the power returns, it sometimes takes the machine with it.”

They used Leila’s phone to assemble the images into numbered sets. Each included an inventory identifying the originals and noting where the images overlapped. Sami read every line from the screen against the paper. Twice he caught a blurred figure. They replaced those photographs.

Service returned long enough for the first set to leave through the hospital’s records address. The clerk entered its receipt in her bound register and stamped the inventory. Leila sent the same set to the neighborhood missing-persons group and the local group coordinating families’ access to hospital records.

Her message was brief: where the papers had been found, what the discrepancy was, and a request to retain the complete set.

A reply arrived: Downloaded. All pages readable.

Then another: Saved outside the hospital.

Leila sat down.

At the doorway, Nasser stood watching the clerk wrap the originals in clean paper.

“You are circulating an accusation,” he said.

“The documents and my statement,” Sami replied.

“There will be an inquiry into how these were removed.”

The clerk looked up. “Their receipt is registered. So is their condition.”

Nasser’s mouth tightened. He made no admission. When he left, Leila could still hear his shoes in the passage after she could no longer see him.

The clerk locked the originals in a cabinet awaiting transfer and gave Leila the stamped receipt. On her phone, questions had begun to appear beneath the shared files. Some challenged Sami’s recollection. One asked who had authorized the altered summary. Another asked which records Nasser had selected for preservation.

Sami stood beside her chair.

“I’ll answer what I can,” he said.

“You should.”

After a moment, she added, “This is not forgiveness.”

“I know.”

Beyond the entrance, demolition machinery struck masonry. Leila flinched, then checked the messages again. The files were still there, opening on other people’s screens.

Another message arrived before Leila could put the phone away.

I have three files. Which one is missing?

She checked the attachments, then wrote: There should be four parts. Please keep them together.

The fluorescent tube flickered. Beneath the desk, the backup supply gave a continuous whine. The clerk printed a cover sheet while there was still power: the casualty return, the altered summary, the photographs of the carbon strip, Sami’s signed account. Each item had its own filename and page count. Beneath them Leila added where the originals had been found and the number of the stamped receipt.

“Put the date I wrote the statement beside it,” Sami said. “Not the date of the order.”

She did. His memory was not a contemporary transcript. Nor did the strip, by itself, establish everything said into the handset. She left those distinctions visible. His account still included his question about people below, and his admission that he had transmitted the clearance afterward.

The clerk enlarged the casualty entry on her screen.

“Before I finish the transfer record. You’re certain this identifies your brother?”

Leila had been certain, then frightened of certainty. She checked again: Younis’s full name, their father’s name, the old family address. The street had been renamed, but this was the address their mother had taught them to recite if they became lost.

“It’s him.”

Her voice sounded practical, almost indifferent. She hated that, although she could not have borne saying it differently. The entry recorded her brother’s death. It did not tell her who had last spoken to him, what he had understood, or where she could find his body. She had found an answer without reaching the end of his disappearance.

Nasser returned while the clerk was checking the receiving hospital’s address.

“The summary was prepared under emergency conditions,” he said. “You’re treating a fragment and a later recollection as if they settle the sequence.”

“They don’t settle all of it,” Leila said.

“Then your circulation is misleading.”

“The whole set is being circulated. You can say which part you dispute.”

Sami remained beside the desk. “My account says what I remember and what I did. I transmitted the clearance.”

“After an instruction,” Nasser said.

“Yes. That doesn’t remove my part.”

Nasser looked at Leila. “There are procedures for this.”

“Give your response in writing,” she said. “It can travel with the records.”

He asked instead for the inventory. The clerk handed him a copy, keeping the register beneath her palm.

A request arrived from the receiving records office: send the archive selection list and the authority under which material had been left for demolition. The clerk forwarded it to Nasser’s official address. It was not a charge or a judgment, but his decisions now required an explanation beyond this room. Leila read the request twice.

The lights failed before the second cover sheet finished printing.

For several seconds there was only the window’s grey light and the clicking of the printer. Then the clerk switched on a battery lamp. Leila held her phone near the window, waiting for one bar of service; Sami checked the saved packet against the inventory on his screen. Using her phone, the clerk forwarded the hospital packet to the named receiving officer. Leila resent the missing attachment to the local groups.

The clerk unlocked the cabinet. Together they checked the wrapped originals, protecting the carbon strip between clean sheets and stiff card. The handset and recovery tag went into a separate pouch, listed alongside the papers. Sami read out each item. Leila marked it off. The clerk sealed the transfer box and recorded who would carry it.

A porter appeared at the doorway. The demolition foreman wanted the passage cleared within the hour.

When Sami reached for one end of the box, Leila let him lift it. His hands were steadier now. She needed that steadiness; accepting it did not mean forgiving him. He would still have to answer her questions when there was no box to carry, no failing connection to occupy them.

“You’ll keep answering,” she said.

He looked at her. “Yes.”

They carried the box to the hospital vehicle waiting beyond the condemned wing. The driver signed for it on the bonnet, holding the paper flat against the wind. Leila watched the clerk compare his name with the transfer sheet before giving him the sealed box.

She waited at the entrance until the vehicle turned out of sight. On her phone, acknowledgments accumulated unevenly: the hospital server had retained the packet; two families had downloaded it onto other devices; another recipient had saved a copy outside the district. The woman with three files now had four.

Questions followed the acknowledgments. Someone challenged the timing in Sami’s statement. Someone else asked why the summary omitted what the carbon strip preserved. Leila sent the index again rather than attempting an answer she could not support.

Later came a photograph of the receiving officer’s signed inventory, the seal number written beneath it.

From the archive wing came a long metallic scrape.

Leila still had questions, and none of the messages told her where she could take flowers for Younis. She folded the receipt along its existing crease and put it inside her bag. When the machinery sounded again, she did not turn back toward the archive. What they had found no longer depended on those walls remaining upright.

At the forty-eight-hour mark, the hospital server returned its final receipt.

Leila read it twice in the courtyard office, then compared the file names against the inventory: the dispatch strip, the casualty record naming Younis, and the photographs of both originals. Each had been scanned, copied to the hospital archive, and sent to the independent recipients listed on the transfer sheet. The sealed box had reached its receiving officer before the demolition crew cleared the passage. His signed inventory and the seal number matched the receipt in her bag.

She checked the copies one more time. The dispatch strip’s faint characters were still legible beneath the crease. The casualty entry had not blurred at the fold where Younis’s name appeared. The documents had survived the journey without becoming cleaner or simpler than they were.

Outside, the demolition foreman called for the remaining staff to move back. The damaged wing stood behind temporary fencing, its windows empty, its exposed rooms divided by broken walls. Dust lay in the cracks of the courtyard paving. Leila could feel the machinery through her shoes before she heard its engine.

The deadline had not brought an answer. It had only made the evidence harder to destroy.

Nasser arrived carrying a folder. He had been overseeing the demolition and selecting which records to preserve; now, at the inquiry’s request, he handed over his official summary with the inventory. Its chronology placed the clearance at 14:14 and Younis’s warning after the decision had gone out. The dispatch strip and casualty record were attached beside it, not beneath it.

“Put the summary in the same packet,” Leila said. “With the originals’ locations and the recovery notes.”

Nasser paused, then nodded. His expression gave her no reliable way to tell whether he thought that fair, necessary, or merely unavoidable.

The packet identified which documents he had selected, which he had altered in summarizing, and where the original records had been found. It did not decide what the alteration meant. That was left to the reviewers, along with the disagreement in the times and the question of when the warning had reached the communications room.

Leila sent the packet to the inquiry and copied the families and recipients already holding the records. A new message appeared asking whether the documents proved that Nasser had knowingly changed the chronology. She did not reply yes. The documents established what they established; the rest still required examination.

Sami waited at the table with his notebook open. He had written his account in his own hand, then signed each page. Leila read it slowly, following the lines with one finger.

He accepted that he had retransmitted the clearance. He recorded the query that followed Younis’s warning and the warning he had transcribed. He wrote that he remembered reaching toward the transmit switch, and that he could not account for every moment between the warning and the transmission. He did not claim that the missing time excused him. He did not pretend that remembering the warmth of the room, or the handset voice, gave him a complete memory of what he had done.

At the bottom, he had added: I am responsible for retransmitting the clearance. I cannot offer a complete account of my memory.

Leila looked up. Sami watched her face, attentive to the pause.

“You’re leaving the uncertainty in,” she said.

He nodded and touched the page. “It’s true.”

It was not the whole truth of the people confined below the south stairs, nor a verdict on what he knew when he reached for the switch. It was the boundary of what he could honestly give them. Leila signed that she would attach it to the record. Sami signed back that he understood.

The temporary hearing he had regained had faded. When a metal tool struck the paving nearby, he did not turn toward the sound. The memories he had lost remained absent: the river landing, the ordinary details that had once belonged to a life rather than to a list of facts. His notes preserved dates and places, the repair to the fan, the warning, the decision to listen. They could not restore the experience that had made those things his, or the affection attached to them.

Leila had stopped treating that distinction as a problem she could solve for him. They communicated with signs and writing. His deafness was not another item for the inquiry to explain away; it was part of the life he was living now, beside the things neither of them could recover.

A tremor crossed the courtyard as the first section of the wing came down. Dust lifted beyond the fence and drifted across the high windows. Leila checked the server receipt again. The records remained available in more than one place, and the receiving officer’s inventory was preserved with them. The archive itself would not last through the day.

Sami closed his notebook. His hand lingered on its cover, as if he were weighing whether there was anything else he could add. Then he looked at Leila.

In the quiet space between the machinery’s movements, she signed: Meet again?

The invitation was small enough to refuse without ceremony. It made no promise about the inquiry, the missing memories, or the names in the casualty record.

Sami answered in sign. Leila watched carefully, then repeated his words: Tomorrow, if you want.

He corrected the last sign, gentle but precise.

She tried again. He nodded.

Beyond the fence, the damaged wing continued to come apart. Between them, the evidence remained, and so did the grief. Leila held his gaze and signed the time they would meet.

The forty-eight hours ended beneath a low, colorless sky. By then, the demolition crews had cleared the corridor beside the archive, and chalk marks on the damaged wing showed where the next section would be taken down. Dust had settled into the courtyard’s cracks. Whenever machinery started beyond the fence, a faint shiver passed through the paving and into the legs of the benches.

Before the last archive room was sealed, the records clerk checked the dispatch strip and casualty return against the inventory. The strip was thin at its folds, the ink broken where it had been handled too often. The casualty record held the names in close rows. Among them was Younis. Leila paused at his entry, as she had before, then moved her finger on without touching the paper.

The originals were wrapped separately. A verified copy of each document had been placed on the hospital server, and another set had gone with the receiving officer, whose inventory listed what he had taken. The server receipt was printed and added to the file. Nasser watched the clerk secure the parcel. He had chosen records for preservation, but he could no longer keep every copy inside the hospital, or decide alone who would see them.

“I am opening an inquiry into the removal,” he said.

The clerk wrote down his words. Nasser supplied the inventory identifying the originals and pointed out passages he disputed. His own alterations, made while summarizing the records, were preserved with the versions they had changed. No one covered them with a clean transcription. He said the warning in the dispatch could not be established; he could challenge the record, and others could examine his changes. Neither statement settled what had happened. The inquiry would have to proceed with the documents intact and the disagreement visible.

Sami read the account he had signed once more. It said he had retransmitted the clearance. It also said he could not claim complete memory of the moments around it, or use that uncertainty to declare himself innocent. He had written that the sound that struck him was missing from his recollection. He had not filled the blank with what others wanted him to hear.

The hearing that had briefly returned to him had faded again. When a metal tool dropped near the work fence, he did not turn. The absence was not dramatic; it was a fact his body no longer needed to announce. He heard nothing from the crews, and no new sound could guide him back to the memories already lost: the river landing, the familiar details of his old days, the affection that had once made those details more than entries in a notebook.

His notes kept the dates and places. They recorded the repair to the fan, the warning, and the decision to listen. They could not restore what those things had felt like when he lived them. Sami did not pretend otherwise. He wrote when a fact needed preserving and signed when words would take too long. The people around him learned to wait for his hands or turn a page toward him. His deafness belonged to his life now; it was not a riddle the inquiry could solve.

The next day, Leila found him in the courtyard beside the wall farthest from the demolition fence. She had brought no folder. For a while they sat with the notebook between them, open to the page where he had written what the evidence could prove and what remained uncertain. Leila looked at the list, then at him. Younis’s name was not on the page. She knew that leaving it out did not mean forgetting.

She raised her hands and asked whether he still wanted to meet again. The question was plain, with room for no, for later, for nothing more than this moment. Sami answered in sign. Leila watched his hands closely and repeated the answer, careful with its shape. She got one part wrong. He corrected it, gently and precisely, and she tried again. This time he nodded.

They settled on a time, not as a promise that grief would ease or the inquiry would reach a simple conclusion, but as something they could choose while both remained uncertain. Across the fence, the damaged wing waited for its next section to fall. The records had been carried beyond its reach. Leila held Sami’s gaze, and he returned it. Neither of them had recovered what was gone. Still, when she signed the time once more, he answered.
