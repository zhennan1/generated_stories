## Chapter 1: The Degree-Less

By midnight, the Undercroft had begun to sweat.

Condensation gathered along the iron ribs beneath Veritas’s Great Library and fell in warm drops onto the streets below. Steam from the university’s boilers traveled through the pipes to lecture halls, laboratories, and the private towers of tenured professors. Down here, among rust and soot, the degree-less lived beside the machines that served their betters.

Eli knew which pipes were safe to sleep beside.

The blue-banded lines carried clean heat. The green ones carried water. The copper pipes beneath the library carried something that could blister bone. He knew which vents exhaled paper dust and which exhaled poisonous ink. He knew where scholars threw out broken chairs, cracked tablets, and half-eaten pastries.

Degrees taught people to command the world.

Being degree-less taught Eli how not to be killed by it.

He crouched beneath a maintenance grate, lantern between his knees, prying the brass teeth from a discarded citation engine. The machine had once stamped references into parchment with a graduate assistant’s seal. Now its gears were clogged with wax and hair.

“Come on,” Eli muttered.

The engine clicked.

“Thank you.”

It clicked again, then spat black sparks. One burned through his sleeve. Eli slapped it out and pulled the final gear free. The scrap brokers would pay enough for it to buy two meals.

Something skittered across the ceiling.

Eli killed the lantern.

Eight tiny taps. A pause. Eight more.

Proctor-spider.

It descended on a silver filament, its body the size of a hound, its eight brass legs ending in needle points. A parchment seal fluttered beneath its abdomen.

ACADEMIC INTEGRITY DIVISION.

Its single glass eye brightened.

“Unauthorized salvage,” it whispered in a voice assembled from recorded lectures. “Unlicensed intellectual activity. Present credentials.”

Eli pressed himself into the shadow beneath the grate. The spider lowered one leg and scraped the floor.

Then a bottle shattered in the darkness behind it.

The spider spun toward the sound. Eli crawled through the grate, dragged it shut, and ran.

His shelter lay three tunnels away, inside an abandoned cataloging vault. He slipped through a curtain of old index cards and crouched beside his bedroll, listening.

Nothing followed.

From inside his coat, he took the object he had found that afternoon: a long, toothless brass key engraved with a circle crossed by three lines.

He had discovered it in a box labeled OBSOLETE—FOUNDATIONAL MATERIALS beneath a collapsed shelf in the sealed eastern stacks. The key had no teeth, but when Eli touched it to a locked door hidden behind the shelf, the door opened.

Vault 0 contained no books.

There was only a stone table, a dead brass lamp, and a codex lying beneath a sheet of glass.

Eli had broken the glass with a chair.

The codex was bound in a pale material that felt neither like leather nor paper. Its pages were blank—not white, exactly, but emptied of color. They drank the lantern light.

When Eli touched one, black ink appeared beneath his finger.

WHAT DO YOU PERMIT?

He had pulled his hand away. The words remained.

“What are the Foundational Texts?” he had whispered.

The ink gathered into a second line.

WHO WROTE THE FIRST LAW?

“I don’t know.”

WHO BENEFITS WHEN A QUESTION HAS ONLY ONE ANSWER?

Eli had fled when alarms began ringing through the library.

Now, in the cataloging vault, he opened the codex again. The same words waited on the page.

PERMISSION IS NOT TRUTH.

Every child in Veritas knew the Foundational Texts were eternal. The Principles of Matter, the Axioms of Rhetoric, the Canon of Healing: all had supposedly been discovered by the First Chancellor and preserved by the university ever since.

A degree did not merely prove mastery. It opened the door to a Discipline.

Without one, a person could not shape heat, summon evidence, mend flesh, or persuade a locked gate.

Without one, a person was only Eli.

A bell clanged overhead.

Not the hour bell. The harsh triple alarm used during inspections.

Voices entered the Undercroft.

“By order of Chancellor Voss, all unauthorized residents will submit for examination!”

Graduate assistants poured through the eastern archway in gray robes, silver-threaded collars, and shining degree badges. One raised a hand. Blue fire raced along the pipes.

Eli snapped the codex shut.

The flames reached the cataloging vault. Index cards burst into fire.

He grabbed the book and ran.

A spell struck the floor behind him, buckling stone. Another transformed a pile of scrap into shrieking paper birds. Eli plunged down a service stair, coughing as steam blinded him. He slipped, struck his shoulder, and nearly dropped the codex.

At the bottom stood a riveted iron door with no handle or keyhole.

Eli shoved it.

It opened.

An old woman stood beyond it, holding a wrench like a sword.

Her white hair was braided down her back. Goggles rested on her forehead, and her oil-black coat was patched with colored thread. Behind her, a furnace glowed and a network of pipes crossed the workshop walls in careful patterns.

She looked at Eli, then at the burning stairwell.

“You’re late,” she said.

“I’m sorry?”

“If you’re going to bring the university down on my roof, arrive before supper.”

The assistants shouted above.

Eli lifted the codex. “I found this.”

The woman’s expression changed—not to surprise, but recognition.

She stepped forward and caught his wrist.

“What does it say?”

“It asked what I permit.”

“And what did you answer?”

“Nothing.”

Fear entered her face.

“Good,” she said. “Come inside.”

She pulled him into the workshop and slammed the door. With three turns of the wrench, she sealed the bolts. The shouting faded to muffled thunder.

“My name is Wren,” she said. “Master of the lower maintenance guild, though the university stopped recognizing the guild forty years ago.”

“Why help me?”

Wren looked at the codex.

“Because dangerous questions have been asked before.”

“What happened to the people who asked them?”

She took the book from him but did not open it.

“Some were burned,” she said. “Some were promoted. The worst fate is difficult to say.”

The furnace breathed behind her. Pipes trembled in the walls like sleeping beasts.

“Come,” Wren said. “You’ll need shelter tonight.”

“And tomorrow?”

Wren carried the codex toward the back of the workshop.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “we find out whether it has begun answering you.”

## Chapter 2: The Names of Things

Wren did not sleep so much as change positions.

At midnight she lay beneath the workbench with one ear against the stone. At two, she sat beside the boiler, listening to its ticking. At dawn, Eli found her on the workshop roof, wrapped in oilskin and watching Veritas kindle below.

“Come up,” she said.

From the roof, the city rose in layers: the black chimneys of the Undercroft, the crowded tenements of the Commons, the bridges and lecture halls, and, at the summit, the Chancellor’s citadel shining like a coin.

“What are you listening to?” Eli asked.

“Everything.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It is if you know the names.”

She took a brass-headed hammer from her belt and tapped the roof. Once. Twice. Three times.

“Furnace brick,” she said. “Soft in the middle, hard at the skin. It carries heat sideways when the weather turns wet. Beneath it is iron plate, patched with slag. The slag is tired. It will split before winter.”

“You can tell that by tapping?”

“I can tell because the roof answered.”

She handed him the hammer. Eli struck the brick. The note was hollow near the edge.

“Loose,” he said.

“Why?”

“Different pitch.”

“Again.”

He listened. Beneath the hollow note was a faint trembling.

“Water behind it.”

Wren nodded. “The city speaks before it breaks. People merely prefer the break. It is more dramatic.”

She led him down into the workshop and opened a cabinet he had mistaken for part of the wall. Inside lay hundreds of narrow wooden strips, each marked with a word in faded ink.

Wren selected one.

“Vey,” she said.

“What does it mean?”

“Pressure that wants out but has not chosen where.”

“That’s a word?”

“It is now.”

She placed another strip beside it. “Orn. Pressure trapped by a flaw in its vessel. Dangerous because it believes the vessel is the problem.”

“And this?”

“Kes. The quiet before a pipe bursts.”

Eli touched the worn letters. “You made these?”

“My teacher did. Her teacher before her. We carried the names in our mouths when the university began locking them out of books.”

“Why lock out names?”

“Because names let people work without permission.”

Wren opened the boiler’s inspection hatch. Steam breathed into the room.

“The Disciplines are not magic in the way professors claim. They are agreements. Old agreements, polished until everyone forgot they were agreements. A scholar recites the sanctioned name of heat, and heat obeys because the university has taught the world to recognize that voice.”

Eli thought of the graduate assistants, their silver badges, and the blue fire leaping from their hands.

“Then why can’t I do it?”

“Because no one gave you the words.”

“No one gave me the truth.”

Wren glanced at him. “Those are not always the same thing.”

Beneath the boiler she found a blackened slate. Carved into it was a circle crossed by three lines.

Eli’s hand went to the brass key beneath his shirt.

“You know it.”

“I know what it used to mean.”

“Tell me.”

“Before the university, knowledge belonged to whoever needed it. Millers knew grain. Masons knew which stones would bear a tower. Midwives knew the thousand differences between pain and danger. Not everyone knew everything, but knowledge traveled.”

She rubbed the symbol with her thumb.

“The first Chancellor gathered those people here. He called the university a commons and promised to preserve what was being lost. He wrote down their methods, warnings, and names. Then he discovered that a written thing could be withheld.”

“He charged for access.”

“At first, only for ink and instruction. Then examinations to prove who was worthy. Then degrees to prove who had passed. He said he was protecting knowledge from fools.”

“And was he?”

“Some fools were kept away. So were the poor, rivals, and anyone who asked who had appointed him guardian.”

Wren closed the cabinet.

“The Foundational Texts were assembled from thousands of voices. The university erased the voices and kept the commands: Principles of Matter, Axioms of Rhetoric, Canon of Healing. They became laws because the Chancellor declared them laws.”

Eli pictured the pale codex beneath its glass, blank until his hand touched it.

“What does it want from me?”

“Perhaps it wants you to stop asking what it wants.”

“That isn’t helpful.”

“It is the most helpful answer I have.”

A bell began ringing overhead. Not the measured bells of lectures, nor the bright chime announcing a successful defense. This was a harsh, repeating clang that seemed to strike the whole city.

Wren went still.

“Tuition decree.”

By the time Eli followed her outside, the Commons had filled. Students poured from the lower colleges in dark robes, carrying placards that flashed with projected letters: DOUBLE FEES, HALF MEALS. Adjunct lecturers marched beside them, credentials pinned to their coats like medals from a war they had lost. Artisans emerged from alleys and machine sheds. Someone had overturned a registrar’s cart, and enchanted forms fluttered through the air.

On the bridge above, mounted proctors formed a line. Behind them stood a dean in crimson velvet, his face enlarged by a speaking spell.

“Return to your assigned wards,” he commanded. “The revised tuition ensures academic excellence and protects the value of your degrees.”

A stone struck the bridge rail.

The dean’s smile did not change. “Further disorder will be recorded as evidence of unfitness.”

A girl near Eli raised her voice. “My brother sold his years for a degree! He still can’t afford bread!”

The crowd answered with a roar.

The proctors lifted their staffs.

Fear moved through the people—not sudden, but practiced. Students lowered their signs. Adjuncts stepped backward. The university had taught them that punishment proved order, and order proved truth.

Wren seized Eli’s sleeve.

“Come away.”

“They can’t just—”

“They can do exactly what they have always done. That is why they call it tradition.”

The first staff discharged. Blue light cracked across the street. People scattered on rain-slick stones. A boy fell, and no one reached him until Wren pushed through the crowd and dragged him behind a coal cart.

She pressed a cloth to his bleeding forehead and spoke softly in the old names: pressure, vessel, breath, bone. The words did not flare. They summoned no obedient miracle.

But the boy breathed.

Eli looked toward the Chancellor’s citadel. Beneath his shirt, the codex seemed to stir against his ribs.

A key, he thought.

Not a curse. Not a weapon.

A key asked what door a person had been taught not to see.

For the first time, Eli wondered whether the university’s greatest secret was not that its laws were false, but that everyone outside its walls already knew better ones.

## Chapter 3: The Broken Scholar

The Infirmary occupied the oldest hill in Veritas. Its upper towers gleamed with healing sigils, but beneath the licensed wards and student clinics lay rooms the university did not advertise.

Wren sent Eli there at dusk with a brass token stamped with the maintenance guild’s old mark.

“Ask for the ward below the ward,” she said. “If they tell you there isn’t one, you’re close.”

“And if Kael doesn’t want to see me?”

“He hasn’t wanted to see anyone in six years. You’re not asking him to be well. You’re asking what he saw.”

The token opened a freight lift in a linen storeroom. Eli descended through damp darkness, past pipes furred with mineral crust. The lower ward smelled of iron, boiled herbs, and rain trapped in stone. Patients sat behind narrow windows, staring at things that were not there. One woman whispered the same sentence to an empty corner. A boy’s shadow moved half a second after him.

At the corridor’s end, a nurse barred Eli’s way.

“Authorized visitors only.”

He held up the token.

Her expression tightened. “That guild hasn’t had access here since the Chancellor’s grandfather.”

“Then tell me where Kael is.”

“Kael is not someone you visit.”

“Wren says otherwise.”

The nurse looked away. “Room nine. Don’t touch him. Don’t answer if he asks a question.”

“Why?”

“The last person who did spent three weeks believing she was a staircase.”

Room nine had no bed. Kael sat on the floor with his back against the wall, knees drawn to his chest. He was younger than Eli expected, perhaps thirty, though his hair had gone white at the temples. A doctoral candidate’s blue cord hung frayed around his neck.

On the wall before him, someone had drawn a crooked doorway in charcoal.

The drawing opened.

Not physically. The stone remained stone, but the air folded inward, revealing a black depth beyond the marks. Something on the other side breathed.

“Don’t,” Eli said.

Kael’s fingers twitched. The doorway vanished.

“You saw it,” Kael said.

“Saw what?”

“The brace.”

Kael’s voice was dry from disuse. His eyes lifted, pale and fever-bright. Tiny lines of light crossed his irises like diagrams.

“Everything has braces,” he said. “Spells, buildings, arguments. They hold the shape in place. If you see them, you can tell what a thing is carrying.”

“And if you can’t?”

“You become a staircase.”

Eli crouched several feet away. The codex beneath his shirt warmed against his ribs.

“I need to know about the Foundational Texts.”

Kael laughed once, without amusement. “Everyone needs to know about the Foundational Texts. That’s why they’re foundational.”

“Wren says they were made from older knowledge.”

“Wren is right.”

The answer came so quickly Eli forgot to breathe.

Kael leaned his head against the wall. “The Principles of Matter are not principles. They’re commands. The Axioms of Rhetoric are permissions written in the grammar of certainty. The Canon of Healing was taken from midwives, bone-setters, apothecaries, and plague workers, then rewritten so the methods obey the seal of a degree.”

“Why does that matter?”

“Because commands can be revoked.”

The lines in Kael’s eyes shifted. For an instant Eli saw the ward differently: a lattice beneath the walls, symbols tied into the university’s foundations. The door was held shut by three red strokes. The nurse’s token had a knot of authority in its center. Even Eli’s body was outlined by trembling braces.

At his chest, the codex appeared as a blank space in the lattice.

Kael stared at it. “You have one.”

“A book?”

“A refusal.”

Kael reached for a shard of slate. “They broke me during the Trials. My Discipline was textual reconstruction. I could find what had been removed from a passage, what a sentence was hiding. They made me read the First Law until I could see its framework.”

“What happened?”

“I saw the first lie.”

His hand shook.

“They weren’t laws of the world. They were locks. Someone wrote, ‘Only the accredited may command,’ and built every later sentence around it. Repetition made the agreement heavy. Degrees made it portable.”

Eli thought of Wren treating the injured protester with old names while licensed healers watched.

“Can you undo it?”

“Not by arguing with the command. You name what the command was placed over.”

Kael drew a small circle on the floor. Inside it he wrote an angular mark. The mark flared, and a brass graduate assistant’s badge appeared in his palm.

“This claims mastery over citation,” he said. “It commands words to point toward authority.”

He closed his eyes. “Paper.”

Nothing happened. His face tightened.

“Not the sanctioned name,” he whispered. “The true one.”

Eli listened. Pipes muttered in the walls. A drain struggled beneath the floor. Beneath the badge’s enchantment he sensed a brittle scrape, like a page dragged over stone.

“Borrowed voice,” Eli said.

The badge went dark.

Its silver emblem flaked away. The brass softened, sagged, and dropped from Kael’s hand as an ordinary piece of stamped metal.

Eli stared at it.

“It isn’t enough to know the command,” Kael said. “You must know what it concealed.”

“Then help us.”

“Us?”

“People who are done asking permission.”

Kael studied him. “You want a weapon.”

“I want a way to keep people from being helpless.”

“That is a more dangerous weapon.”

He stood. His knees buckled, and Eli caught his arm. Beneath Kael’s skin, the scaffolding of his body flashed into view: fractured braces, missing supports, a black Discipline wound threaded through his spine.

“Don’t mistake standing for healing,” Kael said.

“I won’t.”

At the ward entrance, the nurse rose in alarm. “He cannot leave.”

Kael looked at her. The lines in his eyes brightened.

“Can’t,” he said, “is usually a command.”

The nurse’s mouth opened, then closed.

Eli guided him toward the lift. Behind them, the charcoal doorway remained crooked on the wall, waiting.

At the lift, Kael stopped and looked upward, toward the city of towers, bells, degrees, and locked doors.

“Tell Wren I remember the first name.”

“Which one?”

Kael’s broken smile held no comfort.

“The name before permission.”

Then he stepped into the lift beside Eli, and together they began to rise.

## Chapter 4: The Commons Converge

The steam tunnels beneath Veritas had never been designed for meetings. They carried heat from the furnaces to dormitories, lecture halls, infirmaries, and towers above. Pipes crossed overhead in sweating bundles. The air tasted of iron and boiled stone.

But no professor owned them.

Eli entered through a service hatch with Kael leaning against his shoulder. Behind them came two expelled students, three of Wren’s apprentices, and Mira, an adjunct Rhetorics lecturer dismissed that afternoon for “unauthorized interpretive sympathy.” She carried her papers in a leather satchel and wore one shoe.

“You said there would be thirty,” she told Eli.

“There may be thirty.”

“There are eleven.”

“The others may be late.”

Dax, broad-shouldered and blackened to the elbows from the forge, stood beside a pressure valve. A hammer hung at his belt. Someone had scored a line through the university seal painted on the brass wheel.

More people arrived in twos and threes: a lift mechanic from the southern wards, an old mason with a sack of tools, two kitchen workers, a boy with ink-burned fingertips, and several adjuncts who looked as if they had come directly from being frightened.

They gathered beneath the largest pipe, where the ceiling rose high enough to stand upright. Someone had chalked the three-crossed-circle symbol onto the wall.

Kael stared at it.

“Do you know that mark?” Eli asked.

“I know it was covered.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“It is, to a scholar.”

Dax heard him. “We don’t need scholars.”

Mira turned. “You need people who know how to make a city listen.”

“We need people who know how to make a city burn.”

The gathering fell quiet.

Dax stepped forward. “They doubled tuition. Cut meals. Threw students into the street for speaking during a lecture. They burned guild workshops because old women knew how to fix pipes without kneeling to a professor. And you want to make the city listen?”

“I want the city to survive,” Mira said.

“Survival is what they call obedience when they’re hungry.”

A murmur moved through the tunnel. Eli felt the group pulling apart, like pressure gathering behind a flawed seam.

Mira set her satchel on a crate. “The university is rotten, yes. But it is also the infirmary, the libraries, the waterworks, the archives. Destroy it and you destroy the people who depend on it. Reform is possible. We can demand open examinations, adjunct representation, restored meals, and recognition for practical disciplines.”

“Recognition,” Dax said. “You still want them to put a seal on us.”

“I want protection.”

“You want permission.”

Mira flinched.

Eli looked at the gathered faces: tired, wary, and bright with the dangerous hope that came before courage or disaster. He thought of Kael in the ward, and of Wren’s warning that names let people work without permission.

“We’re arguing about whether to ask the lock to open,” he said, “or smash the door.”

Dax’s hand tightened around his hammer.

“Neither,” Eli continued. “We show everyone the door was never locked by nature.”

No one spoke.

Eli drew the blank codex from inside his coat. Its pale cover seemed to drain color from the tunnel.

“The university says its power comes from truth,” he said. “It doesn’t. It comes from commands repeated until everyone forgets they were commands. Only the accredited may command. Only the examined may heal. Only the cited may speak. These are not laws of the world. They are permissions someone wrote down and taught us to fear.”

Mira studied him. “What replaces them?”

“What we already have.”

The lift mechanic raised a hand. “We don’t have degrees.”

“No,” Eli said. “You have hands. You know which cables sing before they snap. You know how to draw heat from a cracked boiler. You know which herbs stop a fever and which ones kill. None of that becomes true when a professor stamps it.”

“Knowledge without standards becomes chaos,” said one adjunct.

“Then make standards together. Write them where everyone can read them. Teach them openly. Correct one another. Let no one turn understanding into a locked room.”

Dax gave a humorless laugh. “And when the proctors come?”

A blue light flickered at the far end of the tunnel.

A graduate assistant stepped through the hatch, robes immaculate despite the soot, a citation badge shining at his throat. He was young, but wore authority like armor.

“By decree of the Chancellor,” he announced, “this unauthorized assembly is dissolved. All present will identify themselves for disciplinary review.”

The badge pulsed.

“You will listen,” he said.

Several people lowered their eyes.

Kael moved first. He crossed the tunnel with a stiff, uneven gait. Eli reached for him, but Kael shook his head. His gaze fixed on something beneath the assistant’s robes.

“Do you know what it says?” Kael asked.

“I know what it commands.”

“No. You know what it repeats.”

The badge flashed.

Kael lifted a trembling finger. “Citation. Authority. Direction. Borrowed voice.”

Black lines appeared beneath the skin of his neck, ink leaking through paper. Eli saw the braces around his body, fractured and overstrained.

“Kael,” he warned.

“Name what it hides,” Kael whispered.

Eli looked at the badge. It did not contain authority. It contained the demand to be mistaken for authority.

“Borrowed voice,” Eli said.

The badge went dull.

Its silver emblem sagged, softened, and slid down the assistant’s robe as a lump of ordinary metal. The blue light vanished. The words in the tunnel straightened.

The assistant stared at the ruined clasp.

Dax drew his hammer.

“Don’t,” Eli said.

The hammer stopped.

The graduate assistant backed toward the hatch, suddenly only a frightened young man in a fine robe. No one touched him. He fled anyway.

For several heartbeats, the only sound was steam moving through the pipes.

Then the old mason laughed. The kitchen workers joined him. One expelled student covered her mouth. Mira looked from Kael to the dead badge.

“You didn’t destroy it,” she said.

Kael swayed. Eli caught him.

“No,” Kael murmured. “We told it what it was.”

The words passed through the assembly.

Not destroy. Name.

The mechanic stepped toward the chalked symbol and laid her palm over its three crossed lines. “My mother knew the old pressure songs. The university called them superstition.”

“My grandfather built bridges without a degree,” said the mason. “They took his measurements and published them under a professor’s name.”

The boy with ink-blackened fingers lifted his chin. “I can read three dead languages. The registrar says it’s worthless because I learned them from a sewer archivist.”

Dax looked around at them, his hammer lowered.

“So what?” he asked. “We make a club?”

“We make a commons,” Eli said.

The word seemed larger than the tunnel.

Mira opened her satchel and set her lecture notes on the crate. “I know the traps in sanctioned rhetoric—the ways a sentence makes obedience sound inevitable.”

Dax glanced at the papers. “I know fire. And metal. And how not to burn the people standing beside me.”

One by one, the others offered what they knew.

The pipes hissed overhead, carrying heat toward a city that had never thanked them. Beneath the university, among brick and steam, the gathered people began to speak their knowledge aloud—not as applicants or supplicants, but as heirs returning to an inheritance that had been stolen and renamed.

Eli listened.

For the first time, the tunnel did not sound like a hiding place.

It sounded like the beginning of an answer.

## Chapter 5: Blood and Tuition

By morning, Decree Nine had been nailed to every door in Veritas.

Mira read it aloud in Wren’s workshop while the others crowded around the stove.

“‘All persons without recognized degree or guild license must register with the Office of Academic Belonging within three days. Failure to register constitutes outlawry. The teaching, practice, or circulation of unlicensed knowledge is hereby classified as heresy.’”

Dax snatched the decree from her. “They’ve called maintenance heresy?”

“They’ve called us heretics,” Mira said.

Kael studied the page. His eyes moved across the letters without resting. “The seal is reinforced. The words are meant to enter the reader.”

Eli felt the command beneath the sentences, cold and patient.

Only the accredited may remain.

Wren folded the decree and fed it into the stove. The flames turned blue. For one breath, everyone lowered their eyes.

Then Wren struck the stove with her tongs.

“Look up.”

They did.

The raids began before noon.

Proctors descended into the Undercroft in lacquered armor, graduate assistants behind them carrying citation rods and bundles of white paper constructs. The first guild workshop burned beneath the old baths. Annotated oil soaked the benches and burned hotter whenever someone tried to smother it.

At the next, the proctors tore apart looms and arrested anyone who could not produce a registration token. At the third, a professor of Applied Ethics declared the eviction “a pedagogically essential correction.”

Smoke rolled through the lower streets.

A runner reached Wren’s workshop, soot-blackened and gasping. “They’re coming here.”

“Back tunnel,” Wren ordered. “Everyone who can walk, walk. Everyone who cannot, is carried.”

The workshop emptied into the old maintenance passage. Its only open exit ended at a massive steam gate beneath Foundry Road, an iron door designed to contain pressure from the city’s oldest mains.

Eli crouched beside the mechanism. The locking wheel shuddered under his hands.

“Vey,” he said. “Pressure seeking release.”

“Orn,” Wren answered. “The left hinge is holding it.”

A blast struck the other side. Steam shrieked through the seams.

Kael stared at the door. Black lines showed beneath the skin of his throat, the hidden braces of his body tightening. “Its command is broken,” he said. “It says hold, but the hinges no longer mean hold.”

“Can you unwrite it?” Mira asked.

“Not while it is holding us and the city.”

Another impact shook the passage.

Dax shoved open the inner gate. “Move!”

Wren seized Eli by the collar as he reached for the wheel again.

“Go.”

“I can fix it.”

“You can open it. You cannot make iron young.”

She placed her tongs in his hands.

“Take the names,” she said.

Blue fire splashed across the workshop behind them. A paper construct crawled over the ceiling, its seal-face opening like a mouth.

Wren stepped between it and the gate.

“Wren!”

She looked back once. Her face was lit by fire and steam, older than the city and steadier than stone.

“Kes,” she called.

The word passed through the chamber: the quiet before a pipe bursts, the instant in which a choice remains possible.

Then Wren drove her shoulder into the wheel.

Eli dragged Kael through the passage. Mira followed, shouting for the others to keep moving. Behind them, the gate groaned.

“Vey!” Wren cried.

The wheel turned.

“Orn!”

The iron screamed.

Eli looked back. Wren stood in the narrowing opening, both hands locked around the wheel, steam lifting her hair. A citation rod flashed in the workshop.

The gate slammed shut.

They stumbled through the drainage galleries until the smoke thinned. No one spoke. Behind the wall, the main gave one enormous exhalation.

Then silence.

Dax struck the stone with both fists. “We left her.”

“She made us leave,” Mira said.

“She made herself stay.”

Eli still held the tongs. Their handles had blistered his palms. Kael gently pulled them away.

They emerged at dusk beneath an abandoned cistern. The survivors counted themselves in the rain.

Wren was not among them.

They found her body in the square outside the old guildhall.

The proctors had bound her upright to a maintenance post, her hands fixed around a broken wrench. A sheet of parchment covered her chest.

CITED FOR HERESY: WREN, FORMER MASTER OF LOWER MAINTENANCE.

Beneath it were the charges: unauthorized instruction, obstruction of lawful scholarship, conspiracy against accredited order.

Dax reached for his hammer.

Eli stopped him.

Wren’s hands remained visible beneath the parchment. The fingers were blackened, the nails split, but they held the wrench as though she were still repairing something.

Kael stared at the sheet. “They’ve made her into an argument.”

“No,” Eli said.

He tore the parchment down.

The paper writhed, trying to name itself law. Eli looked at Wren, at the wrench, and at the people gathering in the rain.

“Her name was Wren,” he said. “She knew vey. She knew orn. She knew kes. She knew how stone fails, how pressure lies, how a frightened child breathes. She taught without permission because permission was never required for truth.”

The parchment went still.

“This is not a citation,” Eli said. “It is borrowed voice.”

The letters faded.

Around the square, people began speaking. Names for hinges, fever, mortar, grain, river current, smoke—names the university had buried beneath seals and fees.

Eli laid the dead parchment over Wren’s feet.

“Take her down.”

Together, they cut the ropes.

That night, beneath the guildhall, they lit no funeral pyre. Fire was too easy to claim as evidence. Instead they gathered around Wren’s tools and spoke until dawn, each person adding a true name to the inheritance.

Eli stood at the center, the blank codex beneath his coat.

“When we go to the Chancellor,” he said, “we will not beg him to recognize us.”

His voice shook, but did not break.

“We will carry Wren’s names to his door. And we will ask him what, exactly, gives him the right to close it.”

## Chapter 6: The Lexicon Foundry

The Lexicon Foundry stood between the old law courts and the river, a fortress of black brick threaded with copper pipes. Its chimneys breathed white vapor over the rooftops. Every breath smelled of burnt sugar, hot metal, and sweat.

For three days, the Commons had watched it.

Students entered through the eastern gate wearing gray labor collars. They emerged hours later with trembling hands and empty eyes. Between those passages, the Foundry took what the university called *cognitive surplus*—attention, memory, invention—and distilled it into degree ink.

At dawn, its bell rang once.

Dax tightened his grip on an iron pry bar. “That’s our bell.”

“It’s their bell,” Mira said. “We’re borrowing it.”

“Borrowed voice,” Eli murmured.

The phrase had become their signal. Not a slogan. A diagnosis.

Thirty volunteers crouched in the drainage culvert behind them. Wren’s apprentices carried hammers; expelled students carried stolen citation rods; two kitchen workers bore sacks of flour to blind the proctor-spiders. Mira held a ring of copied keys.

Kael stood beside Eli, braced against the wall. His face was gray. The black wound threaded through his spine pulsed whenever the university’s wards stirred.

“You should stay here,” Eli said.

Kael gave him a thin smile. “I am staying here. Until the Foundry is inside me.”

“That isn’t reassuring.”

“It wasn’t intended to be.”

The eastern gate opened for a coal cart. Dax threw the first flour sack.

White powder burst across the gatehouse. Guards shouted. One proctor raised a citation rod, but Mira spoke three quick syllables, shaping them into a sentence that left no room for interruption.

“Your authority is presently under review.”

The rod flickered. Its blue flame died.

The Commons surged.

Eli ran beneath the gate and into the central hall. Pipes crossed overhead like the ribs of a buried animal. Along the walls, brass cylinders churned with cloudy gold fluid. Scraps of paper, hair, fingernails, and fragments of handwriting floated inside—pieces of students rendered into ink.

The Press of Words occupied the far end of the hall. It was taller than a chapel, built from ironwood and silver gears. A blank parchment slid beneath its descending head. Each strike produced a degree: Bachelor of Principles, Master of Applied Rhetoric, Doctor of Licensed Matter.

Around it, indentured students fed the machine.

Some were no older than sixteen.

“Stop the press!” Dax roared.

The workers looked up. A shaved-headed boy shook his chains. “We can’t!”

“You can,” Eli said.

He saw the mechanism as Wren would have seen it: not a spell, but a pressure system. The press inhaled before every strike. A vey gathered in the upper chamber, seeking release. Below, the left brace trembled.

“When it rises, move!” Eli shouted.

A graduate assistant appeared on the catwalk, robes snapping around his ankles. His silver badge gleamed.

“By Decree Nine,” he called, “all unauthorized persons are subject to—”

Kael lifted one hand.

The badge split down the center.

Silver peeled from brass. The university seal became a circle, then a smear, then nothing. The assistant stared at his bare coat. Dax climbed the stairs and struck him with the pry bar.

The press descended.

Eli thrust Wren’s tongs into the upper chamber.

“Vey,” he said.

The machine screamed. Steam burst from its joints. The press halted an inch above the parchment, shuddering with trapped force.

Eli turned toward the left brace.

“Orn,” he said. “Pressure trapped by a flaw in your vessel.”

The brace split.

Every gear in the Foundry lurched.

Mira and the kitchen workers broke the students’ chains while rebels fought the guards. Some freed students stumbled away, unable to stand after months of harvested thought. Others seized hammers and joined the attack.

A rack of blank degrees toppled near Kael.

The parchments were not empty. They were waiting, each one a mouth without a voice. Kael touched the first sheet.

His body convulsed.

“What do you see?” Eli asked.

“The permission,” Kael gasped. “Under the fibers.”

He moved from parchment to parchment, fingers leaving dark smudges. The blank degrees curled inward and turned gray.

“Only the accredited may command,” the parchments whispered.

Kael pressed both hands against the rack.

“No,” he said. “Only the commanded must obey.”

The rack exploded.

Parchment filled the air like dead birds. The chimneys belched black smoke. Somewhere below, a pressure vessel burst, and the floor heaved.

Then the Citation Elemental came through the smoke.

It had once been a legal paragraph. Now it wore a body of burning quotations, each sentence chained to the next in red ink. Its head was a sealed citation mark. Its hands ended in sharpened brackets.

“Unauthorized source,” it said.

One bracket struck Dax and hurled him into a vat.

The other went for Eli.

Kael stepped between them.

The bracket entered his side.

For a moment there was no sound. Then Kael screamed, and every letter in the elemental’s body blazed white.

Eli caught him as his knees folded. Blood ran black between Eli’s fingers.

“Go,” Kael said.

“No.”

“The Foundry is collapsing.”

The main pipes had begun to buckle. Freed students poured toward the eastern gate, carrying friends and armfuls of blank parchment. Three rebels lay beneath the fallen catwalk. Others were dragged away by proctors arriving through the smoke.

Mira seized Eli’s shoulder. “We have to leave now.”

The elemental turned toward them.

Eli looked at Wren’s tongs, at Kael’s blood, at the press straining against its jammed command.

He remembered Wren’s voice: *You can open it. You cannot make old iron young.*

“Kes,” Eli said.

The instant held.

He drove the tongs into the press’s fracture.

The machine released its pressure all at once.

Steam tore through the hall. The elemental shattered into strips of burning paper. The press split from crown to base, and the Foundry’s great windows burst outward over the river.

Eli, Mira, and Dax dragged Kael through the gate as the roof collapsed behind them.

They made it three streets before the bells began.

Not the Foundry’s bell. Every bell in Veritas.

Citizens emerged onto rooftops. Workers stopped along the bridges and turned toward the black plume rising over the river. A parchment degree struck a statue’s face and slid down its stone cheek, blank and harmless.

The university had lost a machine.

The city had seen it bleed.

Behind them, proctors captured seven rebels. Ahead, Kael’s blood darkened the stones.

Eli held Wren’s tongs in one hand and Kael’s wrist in the other.

“Keep moving,” Mira said.

So they did, beneath the ringing bells, while all across Veritas people began asking what else could be broken.

## Chapter 7: The Ivory Ward Falls

By noon, the Lexicon Foundry’s smoke had spread across half the sky.

Beneath it, in a disused tram depot, the Commons counted their losses. Seven captured. Three missing. More injured than they had clean cloth for.

Kael lay on a door used as a stretcher. A kitchen worker named Sella kept pressure on his wound while he stared past her shoulder.

“He needs beds, water, somewhere clean,” she said. “Not another tunnel.”

Mira unfolded a map on the floor. It bore the ivory-and-blue seal of her old department.

“The Ward of Rhetorics has all three.”

Dax looked up. “And professors.”

“Most of its guards went to the Foundry. By morning they’ll return.”

Eli crouched beside her. The map showed lecture halls, private staircases, a central auditorium. Under the blue corridors, faint dotted lines marked an older building.

“What’s underneath?”

“Cisterns. Supposedly filled when the Ward expanded.”

He traced a line connecting them to the kitchens. “Covered, not filled. They still need ventilation.”

Mira met his eyes. “A way in?”

“And a way to make staying inside unpleasant.”

They left at dusk.

Mechanics entered through a laundry conduit. Mira led expelled students toward the public gates. Dax drove a cart beneath a stolen hygiene-department canvas, with Kael and the wounded concealed inside.

Eli walked beside the wheel. Wren’s tongs knocked against his thigh.

The Ivory Ward rose above the street in pale stone and blue glass. Over its doors, gold letters proclaimed: **REASON ADMITS ALL WHO ARE WORTHY.**

Two graduate assistants guarded the gate.

“Department?” one demanded.

Mira lowered her hood.

The assistant recognized her. “Your dismissal remains in force.”

“So does the drainage problem in the west cellar.”

He raised his citation rod. A sentence stretched across the gateway, bright and rigid:

**UNAUTHORIZED ENTRY CONSTITUTES CONFESSION OF UNFITNESS.**

Dax swung his pry bar through the glowing words. They rang like struck wire but held.

Then smoke billowed from the laundry grating.

The second assistant turned. Mira caught his wrist; two students dragged the first off balance. Dax hooked the bar around the rod and wrenched it away.

The sentence vanished.

They pushed the cart inside.

An alarm sounded. Black marks scurried down the gateposts: living footnotes, each riding a forest of ink-thin legs. Their brackets caught at sleeves and skin.

“Don’t let them attach!” Mira shouted.

A student swept them aside with a broom. Another emptied a laundry bucket, sending a dozen spinning toward the drain. Eli pulled one from Dax’s collar; it left a neat red asterisk in his neck.

“Keep moving.”

Beyond the vestibule, the auditorium opened in steep tiers of polished wood. Professors gathered on the upper balcony. Graduate assistants blocked the aisles.

The department chair lifted a gold-collared chin.

“Kneel.”

The word struck Eli behind the knees. Beside him, two students fell. He caught a bench and held on.

Mira’s voice shook. “She needs you looking at her. Look at your hands.”

Eli looked. Soot under his nails. Wren’s worn tongs. His legs steadied.

A brick crashed through an upper window. One of the mechanics waved from the roof.

The chair glanced toward it. Mira pulled the kneeling students upright.

“West wall!” Eli called.

They fought their way behind a tapestry depicting the First Chancellor receiving a book from a kneeling mason. A service door lay beneath it, its lock covered by a brass plate of engraved regulations.

Dax wedged his bar under the plate. Eli gripped it with the tongs. Together they tore it loose.

Behind it was an ordinary latch.

The passage beyond smelled of wet stone. Below, the mechanics had opened the old cistern vents. Above, roof hatches stood ready. Eli pulled the rusted damper lever while Dax fed damp sacking onto the kitchen furnace.

Smoke climbed the forgotten shafts and poured into the auditorium.

“Low!” Eli shouted.

The rebels crouched beneath it. On the balconies, professors coughed between commands. Sentences broke halfway through their making. Footnotes stumbled blindly across benches.

Graduate assistants retreated toward the stairs.

Mira climbed onto a desk.

“You don’t have to defend them,” she called.

One assistant aimed his rod at her. His hand trembled.

“You’ll lose your place,” he said.

“They’ve already sold it twice.”

He looked toward the balcony. The chair was ordering him forward while backing toward her private door.

He lowered the rod.

Students surged from the side galleries. Some carried stools; others still wore examination gowns. A girl raised her bachelor’s seal and spoke a holding proposition. Pale lines closed around the chair’s doorway.

Another student caught a fleeing professor inside a loop of his own unfinished argument.

“Alive,” Mira called. “We hold them. Nothing else.”

Dax passed up curtain cords. The graduate assistant who had threatened Mira helped bind his supervisor’s hands.

At the highest landing, the chair unclasped her tenure seal.

“You were promising,” she told Mira.

“I was useful. You kept renewing my contract because I was cheap.”

The seal struck the stair. Others followed, bright disks falling onto wood.

By nightfall, the gates stood open.

People arrived carrying bandages, bread, blankets, tools. Three librarians brought a trolley of books and asked where they should put them. Sella chose a sunlit classroom for the wounded. Kael slept there at last, his breathing shallow but even.

The professors remained under student guard in their examination chamber. Mira wrote their names beside an inventory of surrendered seals.

“A headquarters,” Dax said.

“A responsibility,” she answered.

Eli helped lift a broken door above the entrance. Someone had painted across it:

**THE COMMONS OF SHARED KNOWLEDGE**

Below the sign, volunteers waited to be given work. A young student asked Eli what degree she needed to help.

“None,” he said, and handed her the other end of the ladder.

## Chapter 8: The Citation Inquisition

At dawn, Eli was teaching a girl how to close a radiator valve when the courtyard fountain stopped falling.

Water hung above the basin in clear, crooked ropes. A pigeon remained suspended between the gateposts, its wings spread. The girl reached toward it.

“Inside,” Eli said.

Beyond the open gates stood three people in academic robes. No soldiers flanked them. Their hoods were lined with gold, and the paving stones beneath their feet had sunk into shallow impressions, as though the city were bowing.

The oldest raised one finger.

The fountain fell upward.

Its water struck the windows of the second-floor infirmary. Glass burst inward.

Eli grabbed the girl and ran.

By the time he reached the stairs, the alarm bell had begun sounding. Each stroke repeated before the previous one finished, until the air seemed solid with bronze.

“Distinguished Chairs,” Mira said from the landing.

Blood ran from her ear. She carried the inventory of surrendered seals.

“How many?”

“Three. That we can see.”

Downstairs, someone shouted that the gates were closed. Someone else shouted that there had never been gates.

Eli looked back.

Where the entrance had stood was an unbroken wall.

***

The first Chair walked through the wall without disturbing its plaster.

“Unlawful occupancy,” she said.

Her robe was gray, her hands soft and unadorned. Wherever she looked, worked things forgot their making. A bench slumped into branches. Nails slid from a door as rusty ore. The new sign above the entrance came apart into strips of bark.

Dax hurled a hammer.

It became a dark lump before reaching her and struck the tiles with a dull thud.

“Laundry passage!” Eli shouted. “Take everyone down!”

Mira seized a student who was trying to raise a shield of luminous clauses.

“Help carry the wounded.”

“We can hold—”

“Carry them.”

From the courtyard came a single measured footstep, repeating.

The second Chair had trapped six defenders beneath the arcade. Eli could see them through the window: the same raised arms, the same falling brick, the same red spray returning to a boy’s mouth. Each time the footstep sounded, they began again.

There was no opening in it Eli could understand.

He made himself turn away.

In the infirmary, Sella was strapping Kael to his door-stretcher. His eyes were open.

“They’re correcting the building,” he whispered.

“Can you stop them?”

Kael tried to lift one hand. Black blood soaked through his bandage.

“No.”

Eli took the foot of the stretcher.

Together they carried him into the stairwell while beds behind them collapsed into unjoined timber.

***

At the laundry landing, they found the third Chair waiting.

He wore spectacles without lenses.

Sella stopped. Her hands loosened on the stretcher.

“What are we carrying?” she asked.

“Kael.”

She looked at the wounded man with polite alarm.

“I don’t know him.”

Behind her, a student dropped a bundle of blankets. Another stared at his own hands.

The Chair smiled.

“Your association has been dissolved.”

Eli felt a loosening inside his head. The people around him became faces seen on a tram, strangers whose burdens had nothing to do with him.

Then Kael cried out as the stretcher tilted.

Eli caught it.

“Wounded man,” he said. “Heavy door. Stairs.”

Sella blinked.

“Hold your end.”

She tightened her grip.

They went down together.

Above them, Mira began speaking names. Not an argument, not an appeal: names, repeated sharply, each followed by a task.

“Dax. Brace the door. Neris. Take the children.”

The Chair’s smile thinned.

At the bottom of the stairs, two students stood guarding the captured professors. One had drawn a knife.

“They’ll join them,” he said.

“Then take them with us,” Eli answered.

“There isn’t room.”

Dax shouldered past him and broke the chain linking the prisoners to the wall.

“Walk fast, your lordships.”

***

The laundry conduit was too narrow for the stretcher until they tore away its side rails.

Eli stayed at the opening, helping shoulders and knees through the brick throat. Beyond it, mechanics passed lanterns toward the old cisterns. The city still had spaces its rulers had neglected to correct.

Mira was last.

“Come on.”

A scream rose upstairs. The looping footstep had stopped.

“There are people in the courtyard.”

“Eli.”

“Get Kael out.”

She gripped his sleeve. For a moment he thought she would drag him bodily into the conduit.

Then someone called for her from the darkness.

“Don’t make this a bargain,” she said.

She let go.

Eli turned toward the stairs and found Chancellor Voss standing at their foot.

He wore no hood. His black gown was fastened with a small white seal.

“The courtyard is empty,” he said. “My colleagues have concluded their examination.”

Eli backed toward the laundry door.

Voss glanced at it. The hinges drew together, sealing the gap.

“You’ve been using other people’s knowledge,” he said.

“So have you.”

“Yes. But I acknowledge my authorities.”

He lifted his hand.

“Plagiarism.”

The word entered Eli without sound.

Wren’s face disappeared first.

He remembered a woman bending over a pipe, but her features were smooth as an unfinished carving. Then the workshop vanished. He knew he had lost something there; he could not have said whether it was a person or a tool.

He reached inside his coat. His fingers found a book he did not recognize.

“Unattributed material,” Voss said, “will be removed.”

Eli tried to speak his name.

Nothing came.

The tongs slipped from his other hand and struck the floor.

That sound remained. Iron on stone. He knew what iron did when heated. He knew the ache of gripping it too long.

“Vey,” he whispered.

Voss paused.

Pressure seeking release.

“Orn.”

A flaw holding what it could not bear.

“Kes.”

The instant before breaking.

He said them again, touching the pipe beside the door. Warm metal. A trembling joint. Someone had taught him to listen here.

He could not remember her face.

He kept listening.

Voss lowered his hand.

“How curious.”

Proctors emerged behind the Chancellor. One forced Eli’s wrists together; another searched his coat and held up the pale codex.

Voss took it himself.

“Bring the tools as well,” he said.

Eli struggled when they pulled him away from the pipe.

“Where?” a proctor asked.

“My sanctum. This one requires reeducation.”

They dragged Eli through the ruined hall beneath the stripped place where the Commons’ sign had hung.

“Vey,” he said against a mouthful of blood.

And again, before he could forget.

## Chapter 9: The Unwritten Truth

The Chancellor’s sanctum had plumbing.

Eli held to that fact while the proctors fastened his wrists to a chair. Behind the white walls, water knocked through narrow pipes. Somewhere beneath the polished floor, a valve needed packing.

He could not remember his name. He knew how to mend the valve.

Voss dismissed the proctors and set the pale codex on a lectern. Beside it he placed the tongs.

Around the circular room, books lay open beneath glass. Lines of script lifted from their pages and threaded through the ceiling: commands feeding the university above. Eli saw words he could read without remembering where he had learned them.

ONLY THE ACCREDITED MAY COMMAND.

Voss followed his gaze.

“Yes,” he said. “An instruction. Not a natural law.”

He removed his black gown and folded it over a chair.

“You knew,” Eli said.

“Every Chancellor knows.”

The answer hurt more than denial would have.

Voss touched the glass above one book. Beneath the dark lettering, smaller marks crowded the scraped parchment.

“The first faculties gathered practices from hundreds of settlements. Well-diggers. Midwives. Furnace keepers. People who could do extraordinary things without understanding why.”

“And you erased them.”

“My predecessors organized them.”

“You scraped away their names.”

“Names conferred claims.”

The pipe knocked again.

Voss leaned against the lectern. Without his gown he looked like a tired man waiting for a difficult student to understand.

“Before accreditation, a quarrel over irrigation could empty a river. An inexperienced healer could command a fever downward and stop the heart with it. Knowledge does not make people good.”

“No.”

“Nor careful.”

“No.”

“Yet you would give its powers to everyone.”

Eli pulled against the wristbands. They tightened.

“I would let them learn.”

“And when they refuse correction? When your blacksmith decides anger qualifies him to move mountains?”

A memory flickered: a hammer becoming a lump in midair. A man bracing a door instead of running.

“He stayed to help.”

“This time.”

Voss opened the codex.

Its pages remained blank.

“I can restore what I removed,” he said. “Your memories. Your education. You have an unusual aptitude. Properly trained, you could prevent suffering rather than provoke it.”

There should have been a face inside that promise. Someone Eli wanted back. He reached inward and found only an empty place, terrible in its shape.

“What was her name?”

Voss said nothing.

“The person who taught me.”

“Memory can be returned after we have established—”

“You don’t know.”

Voss’s mouth tightened.

The codex darkened beneath his hand.

WHAT DO YOU PERMIT?

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Voss said, “A question is not a constitution.”

Eli watched the letters.

“Who gets to answer?”

“Someone must.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

A thin line appeared beneath the question, then another. Not new writing: old strokes showing through, like the crowded marks beneath the foundational Text.

Eli leaned forward.

The wristbands bit.

He remembered a voice saying that standing was not healing. Another insisting there must be beds, water, clean rooms. He could not place their faces, but the instructions remained attached to hands: lifting, washing, holding.

“Vey,” he whispered.

Voss straightened.

“Pressure seeking release. Orn. Pressure trapped by a flaw.”

The valve behind the wall shuddered.

“Those terms are not sufficient to govern a city.”

“They’re sufficient to tell us something needs fixing.”

“Attend to me.”

The command caught Eli beneath the chin. His head lifted against his will.

Above them the suspended script brightened.

For an instant he saw what the words required: not merely obedience, but agreement that the man before him owned the right to demand it.

Voss had taken his memories and called the remaining emptiness proof of theft.

“You borrowed it too,” Eli said through clenched teeth.

The pressure faltered.

“All of it. From people whose names you took off the page.”

“Authority requires continuity.”

“Then put them back.”

Eli turned his wrists until blood slicked the bands.

“Who taught the first Chancellor?”

Voss’s gaze moved toward the books.

There: hesitation.

“Who held the stone while someone learned its weight? Who died finding out which water was poisoned?”

The codex’s pages stirred without wind.

PERMISSION IS NOT TRUTH.

Eli read the words aloud.

The bands loosened.

He freed one hand and laid it on the lectern. Voss reached for his white seal.

“Kneel.”

Eli’s knees jerked, but the chair held him.

He gripped the wood and listened to the pipe.

“Kes,” he said. “The instant when choice remains possible.”

He had been taught that. Not invented it, not earned possession of it. Someone had spent time with him beside a failing pipe.

“Wren.”

The name came with grief, though not her face.

“Wren taught me. Kael showed me what had been scraped away. Mira taught people to look at their hands when they were ordered to kneel.”

Each name steadied another fragment.

“Sella carried him. Dax held the door.”

Voss’s seal cracked across its center.

“You have no idea what you are undoing.”

“No,” Eli said. “I don’t know all of it.”

He pushed the codex toward Voss.

“Help us find out.”

For a heartbeat the Chancellor looked down at the offered pages.

Then he raised his hand.

“By the authority vested—”

“By whom?”

The unfinished command hung between them.

Under glass, the scraped names surfaced. Hundreds of cramped signatures, workshop marks, crosses made by hands that could not write. They broke through the grand lettering without replacing it with another law.

The white seal fell apart.

Voss caught the lectern. Above him, the threads of script slackened and went dark. Books settled onto their supports with ordinary papery sighs.

Eli waited for another command.

Voss looked at his empty hand.

Behind the wall, the faulty valve knocked again. This time the Chancellor flinched.

Eli picked up the tongs.

“Where’s the access panel?” he asked.

## Chapter 10: What Knowledge Is Worth

“The panel,” Eli said again. “Where is it?”

Voss pointed beneath the lectern.

Eli knelt, lifted the cover, and closed the valve with Wren’s tongs. The knocking in the pipe subsided. Behind him, the Chancellor whispered a command that accomplished nothing.

Above them, Veritas began to discover what had happened.

Lifts stopped between floors. A bridge sustained by a professor’s Discipline settled onto its ancient stone piers, cracking two. In the Infirmary, healers reached for splints when spoken formulas failed. Their knowledge remained; obedience had gone out of the world.

The city did not collapse. Workers hurried to the failing machinery with ladders, ropes, and tools. They had always done more of its holding together than anyone admitted.

Eli took the codex from the lectern.

“Are you going to stop me?”

Voss looked at his empty hand. “I don’t know how.”

It was not an apology. Eli opened the door.

***

They found the Commons beneath the old cisterns. Sheets divided the chamber into a kitchen, a ward, and sleeping places. Mira stood over a list of names. Sella knelt beside Kael. Dax, his arm bound against his chest, was mending a broken stool.

Mira crossed the chamber and gripped Eli’s shoulder.

“Your name,” she said. “Can you say it?”

He tried. Something caught, then yielded.

“Eli.”

She shut her eyes briefly.

“Wren?” he asked. “I remember her name. Not her face.”

“We have stories,” Mira said. “That isn’t the same. But we have them.”

Kael turned his head. “The braces?”

“Gone dark.”

“Not all braces were theirs.” His breath caught. Sella steadied him. “Remember that.”

Voss came down later under guard. Without his seal, he had difficulty persuading anyone even to move aside. Mira assigned people to watch him and demanded the locations of the prisons.

The seven captured at the Foundry were found in a records vault. Four defenders emerged from the courtyard when the repeating instant broke; two had died inside it. Three Foundry names remained missing. Mira kept spaces for them on the list.

***

The argument over the library lasted three days.

“Open every door,” Dax said. “Let people take what was stolen.”

“And when the surgical books disappear into someone’s cellar?” Sella asked.

“Then make copies.”

“With which paper? Which hands?”

Former professors asked to supervise access. Workers who had been forbidden to read refused. Students wanted examinations abolished; builders wanted some assurance that whoever repaired the cracked bridge understood a load.

Eli listened from a bench near the wall. When someone proposed giving the Commons council permanent custody of the archives, he stood.

“We haven’t earned the right to lock those doors either.”

“Someone has to keep order,” a professor said.

“Yes,” Mira answered. “For how long? Answering to whom?”

They began again.

The reading rooms would open. Books would be catalogued before removal, copies made as supplies allowed. Training would be public. Examinations could demonstrate competence, but failures could be challenged before different examiners. No office would be held for life.

Dangerous work would require supervised practice. That decision took longest. Nobody wanted another license to rule; nobody wanted an untrained enthusiast operating on a child.

They wrote the first rules in pencil.

Eli declined a permanent place on the council.

“You don’t trust us?” Dax asked.

“I don’t trust being grateful forever.”

Dax considered that, then scratched out the proposed exemption for the rebellion’s founders.

***

Some professors left. Others stayed and found students still willing to listen when listening was a choice. A mathematician who could no longer fold distance helped survey the damaged bridge. Two masons corrected his measurements.

Voss surrendered keys and explained the locked archives. Mira would not let useful work settle the question of what he had done. Hearings were announced; witnesses began arriving.

Kael survived. He worked an hour at a time, marking erased attributions in the foundational volumes. Sella made him stop before pain turned his writing crooked. He complained, then handed her the pen.

Dax helped rebuild the workshops burned under Decree Nine. In Wren’s, they salvaged three walls, a cupboard, and half the bench.

Eli scrubbed soot from the pipes.

People came asking what they should learn first. He had no answer large enough for them, so he began with what lay at hand.

***

One evening, a girl crouched beside a sweating pipe. Ink stained her cheek.

“What’s it doing?” Eli asked.

“Making that noise.”

“Listen.”

She leaned closer, keeping her skin away from the hot metal as he had shown her.

“It wants to get out.”

“That is vey. Pressure seeking release.”

“And if it can’t?”

“It may become orn. Pressure trapped by a flaw in its vessel.”

“What do we do before that?”

“Find kes. The last instant when choice remains.”

He showed her the valve. Together they loosened it, and steam sighed into the vent.

The girl laughed.

Eli still could not picture Wren standing at this bench. He remembered impatience in her voice, the weight of the tongs placed in his hand. Some mornings that was enough. Others it hurt worse than forgetting.

“Can I teach my brother?” the girl asked.

“Yes. Tell him who taught you.”

“You?”

“And Wren.”

“Who taught Wren?”

Beyond the workshop, water travelled through old stone. Above it, the library doors stood open, though people still argued about who held the keys.

Eli offered her the tongs.

“I don’t know,” he said. “Let’s find out.”
