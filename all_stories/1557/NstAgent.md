## Chapter 1: The Degree-Less

The pipe began screaming just before the bells announced evening tuition.

Eli pressed an ear to its sweating flank. Above, in the Great Library, readers would be lifting their feet from the heated floors and complaining to servants. Down here the scream meant a split coupling, a flooded sleeping niche, perhaps another body carried into the sump.

“Not yet,” Eli told it.

They wedged a stolen wrench around the valve and leaned. Their left boot skidded through paper pulp. A hundred years of discarded scholarship lay underfoot in the Undercroft: failed theses, superseded catalogues, petitions nobody upstairs had answered. Eli burned the readable pages last. It was a foolish economy, but there were so few things left they could refuse to do.

The valve turned. The screaming stopped.

Behind the pipe, something clicked.

A narrow door stood in the brickwork, its mortar newly fractured by the shuddering main. Eli had passed this wall for three winters without seeing a door. Now a brass plate showed through the lime: FOUNDATION ACCESS. Beneath it, a scanner blinked amber.

Eli held their wrist near the glass out of old habit.

DEGREE NOT FOUND.

“Yes,” they said. “We’ve met.”

Once, a student bracelet had answered for them. Once, they had crossed Veritas by the elevated tram instead of its drains, carrying an introductory textbook beneath towers where tenured professors flew their department banners. A Bachelor of Mechanics could lift an engine without touching it. A doctor could command iron to remember the mountain. Eli had completed four terms and learned to mend a boiler with their hands.

When their mother’s washing stall failed, the bursar offered another loan. Eli had left instead. Knowledge acquired: insufficient. Debt retained: all.

The door shifted as the cooling pipe contracted. No magic; merely a bent latch. Eli put the wrench into the gap.

Inside, cold air smelled of stone rather than mildew.

Their pocket lamp revealed empty shelves, a chair bolted to the floor, and a book lying on a lectern. There was no title on its grey cover. A copper lead ran from its spine into the wall, like a charging cable for one of the library’s speaking catalogues.

Eli looked back. Nothing moved in the passage.

A book from a sealed archive might buy a month’s food. Or ten years at compulsory labor. They opened it anyway.

Blank pages. Thick, faintly warm.

They turned a dozen leaves, disappointed enough to laugh. “What are you worth, then?”

A small black mark appeared beneath their thumb.

Eli snatched their hand away. The mark lengthened, branched, and became a line of cramped script.

WHO ASKS?

They listened for a concealed speaker. In lectures, devices like this had tested definitions and charged a fee for every incorrect answer.

“Nobody who can pay.”

The letters faded. New ones surfaced.

THAT WAS NOT THE QUESTION.

Eli should have closed it. Instead they took the carpenter’s pencil from behind their ear and wrote their name.

The paper drank the graphite.

ELI, BY WHAT AUTHORITY DOES FIRE OBEY THE GRADUATE?

They remembered the examiner walking between desks, a flame resting on his cuff like a trained bird.

“The foundational Texts,” Eli whispered. “The Disciplines express their eternal laws.”

They had copied that sentence forty times after asking why an uncredentialed cook could understand heat but never summon it.

WHO GRANTED THE PERMISSION?

“Permission isn’t the same as law.”

No reply appeared.

Eli touched the page. If the book was a teaching machine, its lesson sounded like something that would get a teacher arrested.

From outside came a delicate tapping.

A proctor-spider descended through the broken mortar, eight jointed legs folding around a porcelain abdomen. Its lens turned toward Eli. A strip of paper slid from its mouth.

“Remain available for assessment,” it said.

Eli threw the lamp.

Glass burst against the lens. In darkness, they dragged the book from the lectern. The copper lead snapped. Somewhere overhead a bell began ringing out of time with all the others.

They ran.

At the passage junction, blue light spilled down the stairs. Three graduate assistants came beneath the low arch, gowns hitched above spotless boots, their silver degree badges shining.

“Seal the lower exits,” one said. “Unregistered presence in a restricted collection.”

Another raised two fingers. Their badge flashed; bricks crawled across the passage ahead of Eli, knitting themselves into a wall.

There had been no bricks there a heartbeat earlier.

Eli ducked into the waste chute as a band of light whipped past their face. Heat blistered an ear. They slid through old index cards and landed shoulder-first in a sorting cage.

Above, metal feet scraped. More spiders.

The cage gate was locked. Eli hauled at it until their injured shoulder failed, then stared at the padlock. They knew its cheap little keyway. They had repaired three like it last winter.

Their hands shook too hard to set the bent wire.

“Move your thumb,” someone said.

An old woman crouched beyond the bars. White hair escaped her cloth cap; a maintenance guild chain hung over a patched coat.

“Your thumb, child. You’re blocking the catch.”

Eli moved it. The wire caught. The gate opened.

She pulled them into a coal recess and closed a panel behind them. A spider’s feet ticked past inches from Eli’s cheek.

Only when the sound faded did she uncover her hooded light.

“Wren,” she said. “Guild master, when there was enough guild to master.”

“Eli.”

“I heard.” Her eyes dropped to the book clutched against their chest. “Unfortunately, so did other things.”

“You know what this is?”

“I know that room was supposed to stay shut.”

Eli waited for her to demand the codex. Instead she examined their burned ear with work-rough fingers.

“I have water and a place to sit. Can you walk?”

“They’ll search the workshops.”

“They always do.” She lifted a loose floor grille. Warm lamplight showed below, and a ladder. “Mine has a back way.”

Eli crouched beside the opening. Under their palm the codex was warm as bread.

“It asked me whether the Texts were permissions.”

Wren’s hand tightened on the grille.

For a moment Eli heard only steam moving through the walls.

“Did you answer?” she asked.

“No.”

“Good. Come down before you try.”

She steadied the ladder as Eli lowered one foot onto it.

“And don’t mistake a new question for a safe one,” she said. “People have asked that before.”

## Chapter 2: The Names of Things

Wren’s workshop had six chairs and only one place to sit. The others held valve wheels, bundled wire, and a kettle missing its bottom.

“Visitors?” Eli asked.

“Optimism.”

Wren cleared the remaining chair with her foot. She washed Eli’s ear, bound their shoulder, and gave them water in a graduation mug with the crest scratched off. Above the ceiling, proctor-spiders clicked along the pipes. Neither spoke until the clicking passed.

The grey codex lay shut across Eli’s knees.

“Is it tracking me?” they whispered.

“Possibly. Or they’re tracking the hole you left.” Wren knelt beside a leaking joint. “Hand me that rag.”

Eli reached for the wrench instead.

“Rag first. Listen.”

Under the hiss ran a low, uneven knocking. Wren laid two fingers on the pipe and murmured something Eli almost understood: a word with the shape of a held breath, followed by a sound like rain striking slate.

The knocking slowed.

No badge shone. No parchment stirred under her coat.

Wren loosened a screw. Water spat into the rag, then settled to a trickle. She tightened the joint with two easy turns.

“You have a degree,” Eli said.

“Not even a certificate of attendance.”

“But you just—”

“Helped it remember where the water ought to go.” She offered them the damp rag. “Feel. Cooler now. Less pressure.”

Eli touched the pipe. They had known its shudder through three winters of repairs, known which valves lied about being closed. They had never heard it answer.

“What did you say?”

“Its name.”

“Pipes don’t have names.”

“Yours do. Burst nuisance. Old bastard. The one above your bed.”

Despite their ear, Eli laughed.

“Those are complaints,” Wren said. “A true name means you’ve paid better attention.”

She made Eli put a palm against the joint. First came its place in the line, then the water’s weight, then the strain of iron against the brackets. Wren spoke the sounds again, slowly. Eli tried them and got only a mouthful of breath.

“Too much wanting,” she said. “You’re giving an order.”

“That’s what a Discipline does.”

“Yes.”

Wren fetched a shallow bowl and held it beneath the drip.

“The guild kept names before Veritas had examination halls. Pipe, pressure, stone. Not every stone alike. Not every pressure asking the same thing. We taught by walking the works together.”

“Why didn’t anyone teach us?”

“The people paid to teach you have a different arrangement.”

She tapped the scratched crest on Eli’s mug.

“The first Chancellor came to the commons with notebooks. Listened to masons, midwives, millwrights. Promised a house where no knowledge would be lost.”

“The Founder gathered wisdom from chaos,” Eli recited.

“He gathered witnesses. Then dismissed them.”

Wren’s voice stayed level; that made it worse.

“He had their knowledge copied into the first Texts. Cut out the listening, the obligations, the particular things people knew. Made commands instead. Crude things, but easy to repeat. Then he tied permission to his seals. The originals became superstition. Teaching without his leave became theft.”

“How do you know?”

“My master told me. Hers told her. It isn’t proof you could submit at a hearing.”

Eli looked at her hands, at the quiet pipe.

The codex warmed against their knees. When they opened it, writing surfaced.

WHAT DOES PRESSURE KNOW OF A SEAL?

Wren went still.

“It heard you,” Eli said.

“Perhaps it heard the pipe.”

“Was it his?”

“I don’t know. There were foundation rooms none of us could enter. People who asked why sometimes stopped coming to work.”

Eli traced the edge of the page without touching the words. In the vault they had thought the book an examiner, then a curse they had carried into somebody else’s shelter. Now they pictured a locked door, and something narrow turning inside it.

“A key,” they said.

Wren watched them.

“Not an answer. A way to get at one.”

A great clang interrupted her reply. Then another. Not the library bells: someone was beating the street grates overhead.

Wren hooded her lamp.

Through the workshop’s rear passage they climbed to a disused coal hatch. Eli peered between its slats into Founders’ Square.

A proclamation filled the public screens beneath the carved faces of dead deans. NEXT TERM’S TUITION: INCREASE OF ONE QUARTER. CONTINUED ENROLMENT CONSTITUTES CONSENT.

Students crowded the tram pillars. Apprentices had come from the market, still wearing leather aprons. Someone held up an empty purse on a broomstick.

“No more debt!” a young woman shouted.

Windows opened above the square. Behind them hung the embroidered banners of tenured households, each professor’s grant of streets and rents stitched beneath a departmental crest.

A man in doctoral violet stepped onto a balcony. His tenure seal caught the floodlights.

“Kneel pending review.”

The command pressed Eli’s injured shoulder downward. Across the square knees struck paving. A few students remained standing, bracelets flaring as they resisted. Their neighbors reached up for help.

“Noncompliance may affect dependent bursaries,” the professor said.

One standing student looked toward a small boy beside her. Her bracelet dimmed. She knelt.

The others followed.

Eli gripped the hatch until a splinter entered their thumb. Four terms of lectures had called this civic harmony. They had pictured power as the ability to raise bricks, summon flame. Here was its finer work: making someone choose who would suffer for their courage.

Wren drew them back before the patrol drones descended.

Below, in the workshop, Eli opened the codex again. The question remained.

“I want to learn the pipe’s name,” they said.

“To bring that balcony down?”

Eli remembered the people underneath it.

“To start with, so the rooms below it stay warm.”

Wren set the bowl beneath the joint and moved aside.

This time Eli listened before they spoke.

## Chapter 3: The Broken Scholar

By morning, Eli could distinguish the pipe’s knocking from the knocking inside their own impatient skull. They still could not make it answer.

“Someone else heard the things beneath the commands,” Wren said, watching them listen. “Kael. Came down here asking why a load-bearing charm needed a Chancellor’s signature. Doctoral candidate, then.”

“What happened?”

“The Trials. Afterward, the Infirmary.” She turned a washer between her fingers. “I bring him clean socks. They don’t give degrees for remembering feet.”

“Will you take me?”

Wren looked at the grey book beside Eli’s bowl. “You may not like what an answer costs.”

“I’ve seen what the questions cost already.”

“That isn’t the same.” But she fetched her coat.

They followed a condensate tunnel beneath the tramway, Eli carrying a bundle of laundry against their sore shoulder. Above them, wheels screamed toward the lecture districts. The Infirmary’s foundations smelled of boiled linen and wet limestone. Wren admitted them through a maintenance wicket with a key worn almost smooth.

At the lower ward, a screen displayed RECOVERY IS AN INVESTMENT IN EXCELLENCE.

Beneath it sat people who did not move when the door opened.

Some wore examination gowns. One woman’s hands hovered above her knees, fingers curved around something invisible. A young man stared at a cup while its tea rose and fell in a thin brown column. An attendant passed without looking at either of them.

“Residual manifestation,” he told Eli, catching their glance. “Don’t encourage it.”

His narrow parchment degree hung in a glazed locket: Bachelor of Applied Pneumatics. With two fingers, he pulled a current of warm air across the room. The windows clicked shut.

Wren laid the laundry on a trolley.

“Same socks, same visit,” she said.

The attendant returned to his screen.

Kael occupied the last bed. His hair had been cut unevenly; a white streak crossed it above one temple. He sat facing a blank wall, one bare foot tucked beneath him.

Wren put the socks in his lap.

“Brought company.”

Nothing.

Eli crouched, careful of their shoulder. They had expected someone older. Kael could not have been thirty.

“I left after four terms,” they said. “Mostly owing money.”

His eyes stayed on the wall.

“I found something under the Library.”

They opened the codex. The question about pressure still marked its page.

Kael’s hand closed around Eli’s wrist.

“Don’t hold it toward the door.”

His voice sounded unused, each word scraped clean before delivery.

Eli lowered the book. Kael looked at them, then at Wren, with the distressed concentration of someone trying to recognize a room after a fire.

“You see it?” Eli whispered.

“The margin.” His finger moved beside the page without touching it. “They couldn’t quite overwrite the margin.”

“Who?”

“Whoever came next.”

He shivered. Wren unfolded a blanket over his knees and waited.

“The Trials asked me to reconcile contradictory axioms,” he said eventually. “I looked beneath them. There were older instructions scraped away. Then older ones. The Texts aren’t the world describing itself.”

“Commands,” Eli said.

Kael nodded. “With names attached. Who may compel. Who must obey. They wanted me to make the contradictions hold.” His grip tightened once, then loosened. “Something gave.”

From the next bed came a small splash. The column of tea had fallen.

“Now I see the bracing,” Kael said. “Every spell. Little ladders pretending to be sky.”

Eli thought of the square, the knees striking stone.

“Can you break them?”

Kael turned toward the windows.

“Can you open that?”

Eli tried the nearest catch. It would not move. No lock, only warm glass and air thick as wool.

“The degree claims the air,” Kael murmured. “It doesn’t know it. Put your hand there. Where it’s cold.”

They found a seam of chill along the sill.

“Don’t say ‘air.’ That’s a category. Feel this air. What it carries. Where it’s going.”

Eli closed their eyes. Soap. Damp stone. The distant bitterness of chimney smoke. A draft trying to reach the warm room, stopped at the glass. They remembered Wren’s held-breath syllable, but this was not a pipe. They let the sound change.

“Not yet,” Kael said gently. “You’re asking it to hurt him.”

Eli opened their eyes. Across the ward the attendant rubbed his face, exhausted.

They tried again.

This time Kael spoke with them, supplying a low, wavering ending. The sill chilled beneath Eli’s palm.

Glass cracked across the attendant’s locket.

He stood abruptly. His parchment curled inward, ink draining into a black bead at its foot. The window swung open. Ordinary cold entered, lifting the woman’s motionless fingers.

“My degree,” the attendant said.

He raised his hand. Nothing followed it.

Eli felt no triumph.

“Is it gone?”

“The permission,” Kael said. “Not what he learned.”

The attendant stumbled toward the nurses’ stair, calling for an examiner.

Wren was already gathering the laundry. “That alarm will bring more than nurses.”

Kael looked at his bare foot. “I can’t do the stairs quickly.”

“We’ll go slowly,” Eli said.

“What do you want from me?”

They could have said answers. Instead they remembered all those people kneeling, each afraid to be the last standing.

“Help finding others. Help making it possible to refuse.”

Kael pulled on Wren’s socks. His hands shook so badly that Eli had to steady one heel.

“No Trials,” he said. “Nobody proves they deserve to be let in.”

“No Trials.”

He stood between them, thin and unsteady. Wren opened the service door. Behind them the ward’s screen continued to promise recovery; ahead, the tunnel smelled of rust and moving water.

Kael took the first step himself.

## Chapter 4: The Commons Converge

By the time they reached the steam-tunnel commons, Eli’s shoulder throbbed in time with the pipes.

The passage ran beneath Veritas like a second city. Condensation jeweled the brick. Copper veins crossed the ceiling, trembling with heat from boilers and laboratories above. Someone had painted doors in chalk: a kettle, a loaf, an open hand. Wren led them down a ladder into a chamber where a disused pressure reservoir had been hollowed into a meeting room.

The room smelled of iron, wet wool, and onions. Forty people had gathered around a central table: expelled students in patched gowns, adjuncts without departmental colors, laundresses, porters, glassblowers, masons, and smiths. Their faces turned toward Eli, then Kael, and finally Wren, as if she were the only one who could explain why a broken scholar had been brought among them.

“Explain,” said a woman near the brazier.

“Not yet,” Wren answered. “Let them sit.”

They sat. Kael folded himself onto an upturned crate, socks planted carefully on the stone. The climb had left his hands shaking. Eli took the place beside him and set the grey codex beneath the table, its weight pressing against their knee like a second pulse.

Mira arrived last, carrying lecture notes tied with red thread. She wore the plain black coat of Rhetorics, but its silver collar had been cut away. Without it, she looked younger and more tired than the portraits in the Ivory Ward.

“I am Mira Senn,” she said. “Adjunct lecturer, formerly of Civic Rhetoric. My contract was not renewed after I suggested a speech might be persuasive without a seal.”

A few people laughed. It was not a happy sound.

At the edge of the room, a broad apprentice with soot ground into his palms leaned against a pipe. “You dragged us underground for introductions?”

“Dax,” Wren said.

“Dax wants to know whether this is a rebellion or another seminar,” he said. “I have attended enough seminars.”

“We are here to decide what we are doing,” Eli said.

Their voice came out rough. Forty faces did not soften. They had all seen promises made by people who owned doors.

Mira placed her notes on the table. “The tuition decree can be challenged. The guilds still have standing. The city councils can petition. If we document the coercion in Founders’ Square, gather testimonies, and send them beyond the university walls—”

“Send them where?” Dax asked. “To the Chancellor? His clerks will grade our complaints.”

“To the public.”

“The public is kneeling.”

“Because they believe the professors are stronger than they are.”

“They are stronger.” Dax pushed away from the pipe. “My master refused a faculty requisition. A professor of Metallurgy turned his hammer to glass. We should burn the lecture halls, foundries, and dormitories. Let the tenured lords learn what fire does to parchment.”

A murmur passed through the chamber. Some startled. Some pleased.

Eli remembered the brick barrier in the archive, the graduate assistant’s lifted hand, the world becoming a sentence. They remembered the attendant’s locket cracking in the infirmary window, and the terrible ease with which power had left him.

“Burning it will not tell us what to build afterward,” Eli said.

Dax’s eyes narrowed. “You have a better idea?”

“I have a question.” Eli stood, guarding their shoulder. “What happens when a professor loses a degree?”

No one answered.

Kael did. “The permission fails. The learning remains.”

The room changed around that sentence: a held breath, a chair scraping, hands shifting from weapons to listening.

Mira looked at him. “You’re Kael Arendt.”

“Was.”

“You wrote the reconciliation thesis.”

“I wrote what the Trials showed me.” His gaze drifted to the pipes. “The Texts are commands written over older commands. They call themselves foundations because they buried the foundation. A degree does not make a person know a thing. It gives them a sanctioned way to make other things obey.”

“Then why did you let them break you?” Dax asked.

Kael’s mouth tightened. “I did not let them.”

Silence settled, heavier than steam.

Eli put both hands on the table. “The university wants us to believe knowledge descends from sealed rooms. It does not. Wren knows how to keep half this city from exploding. Dax knows metal by its color before it reaches the anvil. Mira knows how to make frightened people hear one another. None of that began with a degree.”

Mira’s expression was guarded. “You are proposing reform.”

“No. Reform asks permission to change the rules. I propose we stop pretending the rules are truth. We find the names buried beneath them. We teach each other. We make knowledge common again—without seals, tuition, or asking whether someone deserves to learn.”

Dax gave a humorless smile. “And the university simply watches?”

“No,” said Wren. “It will object.”

As if summoned, boots rang on the ladder above.

The chamber erupted into motion. Someone killed the brazier. Mira snatched up her notes. Dax reached for an iron bar. A young man in a graduate assistant’s blue sash descended into the dark, one hand raised. His badge shone at his throat: a brass square engraved with Applied Pneumatics.

“By authority of the Faculty Senate,” he began, and the air tightened.

Kael rose. His knees nearly failed. Eli caught his elbow, but Kael shook them off and stared at the badge. Around it, invisible lines appeared to him—hooks of citation, loops of obedience, a scaffold telling the air which lungs to frighten.

“Do not,” Eli whispered.

“I am not taking his knowledge,” Kael said. “Only the claim.”

The assistant thrust out his palm. A gust struck the room, scattering papers and driving people against the walls. Kael lifted one trembling finger.

“Air,” he said, “before the permission.”

The badge made a sound like a page tearing.

Its engraved sigil loosened. The brass square split into black threads, and the threads unwound through the air, each carrying an echo of command: yield, certify, obey. Then they vanished.

The gust died. The assistant stared at his empty throat. He still breathed and stood, but when he raised his hand again, nothing answered.

Dax crossed the room and caught the badge’s broken chain. “He’s ordinary.”

“No,” Mira said, looking at Kael. “He is unlicensed.”

Kael swayed. Eli caught him. Wren pressed a cup of water into his hands.

Around them, the gathered people began to speak—not slogans, but particulars. A porter named the pressure valve beneath the north dormitory. A laundress described the infirmary’s heat-stones. An expelled mathematician drew a map of hidden conduits. Dax explained how to temper iron without Metallurgy’s blessing. Mira took out a clean sheet and began writing every name.

Not credentials. Names.

The assistant slipped away through the hatch, carrying no badge and no visible wound. Nobody followed him. The commons had learned something more dangerous than how to fight.

They had learned that a command could be refused.

Eli looked from face to face. Supplicants had come down into the steam. They did not look like supplicants now. They looked like heirs before a locked house, discovering the deed had never belonged to the landlord.

Above them, Veritas continued to lecture, examine, rank, and collect.

Below, the first lesson of the new school began.

## Chapter 5: Blood and Tuition

The decree arrived at noon, printed on sheets of red paper that crawled like hot leaves through the city.

Mira found one nailed to the steam-tunnel door. She read it aloud because the letters shifted whenever anyone looked away.

“By authority of the Chancellor and the unanimous consent of the Tenured Convocation: Decree Nine. All persons without recognized degree shall register with the Office of Civic Instruction within three days. Failure to register constitutes outlawry. The teaching, keeping, or practice of unlicensed knowledge is heresy. Unregistered persons may be detained, fined, stripped of shelter, or corrected.”

At the bottom, beneath the Chancellor’s black seal, someone had added a line in smaller type: TUITION ARREARS SHALL BE COLLECTED FROM THE BODY.

No one spoke for a moment. Water ticked inside the walls. Somewhere beyond the brick, a pump began its laborious cough.

Dax tore the decree down. The paper bit his palm.

“Now we burn it,” he said.

“Not yet,” Eli answered.

They looked at the red sheet in Dax’s hand and felt the old debt stir in them: the bursar’s ledger, their mother’s empty washing stall, the four terms that had become a number no honest work could erase. The university had always called debt a chain. Now it had found a way to call the chain a law of nature.

Wren took the paper from Dax and folded it carefully. “They want us to run angry. Angry people forget doors.”

“Then tell us which door,” Mira said.

Before Wren could answer, every lamp in the commons went blue.

The first blow struck the tunnel grate. The second buckled it inward. Through the widening bars came the white masks of proctors, each mask stamped with a degree seal. Behind them marched graduate assistants in lacquered coats, carrying citation rods whose tips crackled with borrowed definitions.

“By Decree Nine,” called a voice, amplified until the pipes trembled, “surrender all illicit texts and submit for registration.”

The commons erupted.

Mira thrust her ledger into a student’s hands. “Take the names to the north branch. Do not let them become evidence.”

Dax seized a hammer. Kael stood too quickly, swayed, and caught the wall. The broken scaffolds of the proctors’ spells sprang around him in pale lines, too many and too bright.

“Three teams,” he said. “The front is a binding lattice. The left passage is trapped. The old condensate run is open, but only if the main valve is held.”

Wren’s gaze met Eli’s. She understood before anyone else did.

“No,” Eli said.

“Little sparrow,” she replied, almost gently, “you’ve never been good at doors.”

She ran for the wheel chamber.

The rebels scattered into the tunnels as the grate burst apart. A graduate assistant raised his rod. “Unlicensed—”

Dax threw a wrench into his mouth.

The spell broke with a wet flash. For one bright instant, the assistant looked less like a lord of knowledge than a young man who had been handed a weapon and told it was his birthright.

Then the proctors came through.

Eli shoved Kael toward the condensate run. Behind them, books burned in their shelves. The fire was unnaturally clean: it consumed not pages but the right to remember what the pages contained. Guild apprentices dragged tool chests through smoke. Someone screamed when a shanty built against the boiler wall folded under a professor’s gesture.

At the wheel chamber, Wren had both hands on the iron valve. The steam door beyond it had begun to close, its gears shrieking. It separated the rebels from the north branch, but the pressure gauge was already in the red.

“Wren!” Eli shouted.

“Keep moving!”

“I can help.”

“You can carry the names.”

A proctor appeared at the far end of the passage. Wren kicked a lever. The door shuddered lower. Steam screamed through a crack and wrapped her in white.

Eli started back. Kael caught their coat.

“She’s holding the pressure,” he said. His face had gone gray. “If you break her grip, the door opens and the whole run goes.”

Wren looked through the steam. Her patched coat was darkening with water. The guild chain at her throat flashed once in the blue light.

“Eli,” she called. “What is the name of a door?”

The question struck harder than the alarms.

“A promise,” Eli said, remembering the first lesson in the workshop. “That it will hold what must not pass.”

“And when it fails?”

“That it tells the truth about the pressure.”

Wren smiled. “There you are.”

The door dropped another handspan. The proctors raised their rods. Eli heard Mira shouting from the north branch, heard Dax cursing, heard Kael whispering the skeleton of a spell that could not save the person who had taught him to stand.

Wren turned the wheel with the last of her strength.

“Go!”

Eli went because Wren had made the command a kindness. They stumbled into the north branch with Kael, Mira, Dax, and the others. Behind them came a sound like a mountain cracking. The blue lamps went dark.

They reached an abandoned cistern beneath the city by nightfall. No one celebrated their escape. They counted heads. They counted twice.

Wren was not among them.

At dawn, a bell summoned every resident of Veritas to Founders’ Square. The rebels went disguised beneath soot and borrowed cloaks. In the center of the square stood Wren’s body, bound upright to the old public notice frame.

Her keys had been removed. Her guild chain had been nailed above her head. A placard covered her chest.

HERESY CITED. DEBT COLLECTED. LET THIS BE A LESSON TO THE UNLICENSED.

The words blurred. Eli thought at first it was smoke, but tears had made the ink run.

A proctor beside the frame spoke to the crowd. “The woman known as Wren refused registration, obstructed lawful instruction, and perished in the consequences of her own ignorance.”

Eli’s hand found the grey codex beneath their coat. Its cover was warm. When they opened it, the blank page held one line in Wren’s cramped hand, though Eli had never seen her write it:

A NAME IS NOT A LICENSE.

Dax made a noise low in his throat. Mira seized his arm before he could cross the square. Kael stared at the scaffold of the citation, seeing perhaps how many commands had been piled upon a dead body to make murder appear educational.

Eli stepped forward until the proctor’s mask turned toward them.

“Read your lesson,” the proctor said.

Eli looked at Wren’s face. Even in death, she seemed impatient with foolishness.

“I am reading it,” Eli said. “You taught me that knowledge survives its owners.”

Then they turned from the square.

“We go to Voss,” Eli told the others. Their voice shook, but did not break. “Not to beg. Not to register. We carry Wren’s names to his door, and we make him hear what he has tried to bury.”

## Chapter 6: The Lexicon Foundry

The Lexicon Foundry worked through the mourning bells.

From the cistern’s inspection shaft, Eli could see its chimney cutting the moon into strips. Each exhalation glittered with unused letters. Below, behind a barred loading gate, students in debt-grey tunics pushed carts of white sheets toward the presses.

“Not paper,” said Tamsin, who had escaped through a coal chute that morning. She held out her hands. The fingertips were smooth, without whorls. “They take what you do all day. Solving, copying, remembering. The vats draw it through the cuffs. Then they make parchment.”

Dax stopped wrapping his injured palm.

“And you want me not to burn this place?”

“There are sixty people chained to the benches,” she said.

Eli spread her rough floor plan on the cistern stones. Their shoulder ached beneath its old binding. They had wanted a straight road to Voss, something grief could walk without thinking. Instead there were doors, guards, people who would drown if they broke the wrong pipe.

“The waste culvert gets us under the pressing floor,” Eli said. “We open the loading gate first. Nobody touches the vats until the cuffs are off.”

Kael studied the drawing without reaching for it.

“You can stay here,” Eli told him.

“I know.” He took a breath. “I need you to mean it even when we get there.”

“I will.”

***

The culvert smelled of wet wool and pennies. Mira crawled ahead with Tamsin; behind them came Dax and six volunteers carrying cutters, wedges, and shuttered lamps. Eli kept the grey codex buttoned inside their coat, away from the water.

Above the culvert hatch, something struck at intervals. Each blow came with a whispered word.

*Qualified. Qualified. Qualified.*

They emerged beneath the Press of Words.

It rose through two floors, iron uprights enclosing a bed of moving type. Brass pipes fed it from luminous vats. At the benches, students sat with copper cuffs around their wrists, copying arguments displayed on cracked electric screens. As each line was completed, light travelled from cuff to vat. A girl slept upright, her chained hand still twitching.

On a gallery overhead, a master in a fur-trimmed academic hood drank from a porcelain cup.

Eli looked for somewhere to put their anger. There was nowhere yet.

Mira and two volunteers slipped toward the gatehouse. Tamsin bent beside the nearest student.

“Keep writing,” she whispered. “Until you hear the gate.”

Dax fitted cutters around a cuff’s hinge.

Eli crouched at the press’s return valve. Wren would have listened first. They laid their good hand on the pipe and felt the impatient knocking inside.

Not *obey*. Never that.

They spoke the name Wren had taught them for water held hot beyond its willingness, a name made partly of breath and partly of the pause before a lid rattled. Then they eased the valve a quarter turn.

The knocking slowed. A tensioning bar lifted just enough for their wedge.

Eli drove it home.

The next sheet stopped halfway through the press.

*Qual—*

The machine shuddered. On the gallery, the master lowered his cup.

Then the loading gate thundered upward.

“Now!” shouted Mira.

Cutters snapped. Students rose, dragging chains; others stayed seated until Tamsin took their hands. Dax struck hinges with the back of his hammer. Eli pulled the sleeping girl from her bench, and pain burst through their shoulder.

A bell began ringing inside the walls.

Kael stood before a drying rack hung with blank degrees. Fine gold lines crawled across their empty surfaces.

“They’re not empty,” he said. “They already say who may command. They’re waiting for names to fasten it to.”

His fingers shook. Eli stayed beside him rather than touching him.

“Can you?”

“I can see where to begin.”

He spoke softly, naming the fibres, the water, the hands that had carried them. Then his voice caught on another word, and he drew one finger through the air as though loosening a stitch.

The gold lines fell black.

Every sheet on the rack sagged into ordinary pulp.

The press struck once against Eli’s wedge. Its permissions had lost their backing. Across the floor, brass pipes tore free of their enchanted brackets; a flywheel dropped onto its axle with a deafening crack. Eli dragged Kael beneath a bench as the gallery stairs folded.

The master’s cup shattered beside them.

Out of the broken gallery poured a white, angular shape.

It unfolded into a figure made of numbered slips, every edge carrying a moving quotation. Where its head should have been, a seal opened and closed like a mouth.

“Citation Elemental!” Mira shouted. “Don’t answer it!”

“Authority for this assertion?” it demanded.

A fleeing student turned at the voice. Eli shoved him toward the gate.

The creature swept downward. Kael pushed Eli aside, and a strip of paper passed through his upper arm as if flesh were a sentence to be corrected.

He fell without a cry. Black characters spread from the cut.

Dax hurled a bucket of cloudy vat-water into the elemental. Its slips clotted together. For a moment it could only recite the same syllable.

Eli hooked Kael’s uninjured arm over their good shoulder.

At the gate, a second shutter was descending. Three volunteers braced it while Mira drove the freed students underneath. Beyond them, proctors poured from the gatehouse, which had another entrance the floor plan had not shown.

“Leave it!” Eli shouted.

Two volunteers dropped flat and rolled through. The other three were caught on the far side as the shutter slammed down. Through its grille Eli saw a proctor force their hands against the iron.

Dax seized the bars.

“We can’t lift it,” Mira said. “Dax. We can’t.”

Eli had to call his name twice.

***

Forty-three students reached the cistern. Tamsin counted them, then counted again.

Kael lay on a coat while Mira bound his arm. The black letters remained visible through the linen. He was awake, but each time he tried to speak he tasted ink.

Aboveground, the foundry bell continued ringing. Its steady shift-note had become an alarm, audible from the castle colleges to the market viaducts. No new degrees would leave its gates tonight.

Eli sat beside Kael, listening to forty-three people breathe and thinking of three pairs of hands against a grille.

Across Veritas, windows opened toward the silent chimney.

## Chapter 7: The Ivory Ward Falls

By noon, the Foundry’s alarm had become a second weather over Veritas. It rang from the bell towers in broken intervals, trembling windows and sending pigeons from the roofs. Notices appeared everywhere: **THE DEGREELESS HAVE ATTACKED THE LEXICON. REPORT UNLICENSED PERSONS.** Then the ink ran, as if the walls themselves had begun to sweat.

In the abandoned cistern, Mira read the notices while Tamsin counted bandages.

“Three captured,” Mira said. “The Foundry is shuttered. No new degrees until the Press is repaired.”

Dax leaned against a pillar, his wounded palm wrapped in cloth. “Then we strike again.”

“Where?”

“The Ivory Ward.”

The name entered the cistern like a draft. Even the freed students stopped murmuring.

The Ivory Ward was a department, a fortress, and a threat made architectural. Its towers were faced in white stone polished by generations of scholarship. Its lecture halls had no doors for the uncredentialed. Professors there could turn an accusation into a chain, a question into a wound, a pause into silence.

Mira stared at the black coat she had worn when she taught there. “I was dismissed from the Ward,” she said. “I was not invited back.”

“We aren’t asking permission,” Eli said.

Kael lay beneath a folded coat, his upper arm blackened with crawling citations. “Architecture,” he rasped. Ink flecks gathered at his mouth. “The building has a grammar.”

“Can you help us read it?” Mira asked.

“Not with a spell. With a map.”

They went at dusk through the laundry conduits: forty-three freed students behind Eli, Mira, Dax, and Tamsin. The students carried hammers, sacks of lime, crowbars, lamp oil, and rope. Some still wore the Foundry’s numbered collars. Tamsin had cut the numbers away, but pale bands remained around their throats.

At the rear wall, Mira pointed to a drain no wider than a coffin.

“This carried wash water from the old debating rooms. The professors sealed it when students began using it to leave unseen.”

Dax raised his crowbar. “Sealed things can be opened.”

The first graduate assistant found them before the grate came loose. He stepped from the fog wearing seven silver badges and a smile sharp enough to cut paper.

“By authority of the Department,” he said, “kneel.”

The command struck the stones. Knees buckled. Two students fell.

Eli felt pressure descend on their shoulders, cold and precise. The grey codex thudded against their ribs and warmed.

But Mira shouted, “Smoke!”

Tamsin kicked lime into the assistant’s face. Dax smashed a lamp against the wall. Damp coal fed into the vents, turning the passage white-black. The assistant’s badges flared uselessly in the haze.

“Down!” Mira called.

They crawled through the opened drain while students hauled rope around the assistant’s ankles. When the smoke cleared, he hung upside down from a laundry beam, still demanding that someone kneel.

Inside, the Ward defended itself with paper.

Footnotes crawled from the walls in black-legged clusters, each carrying a citation mark like a hooked spear. One fastened to a student’s sleeve and began reciting a paragraph into her skin.

“Don’t answer it,” Mira said. “It needs a response.”

She swept three creatures into a copper bin. Tamsin slammed the lid; Dax wedged it shut with a chisel.

More poured from the lecture hall. Eli saw the room’s design: tiered seats, a speaking dais, high windows, and brass tubes that made one voice fill every corner.

“It can make every corner hear every other one,” Eli said. “Break it.”

Students tore down velvet banners and stuffed them into the tubes. Dax struck the resonance plates with his hammer. Tamsin opened every window. Wind entered from twelve directions, scattering the professors’ voices into a roar.

Six professors appeared along the balcony in ivory robes, gold tenure seals at their throats. Professor Pell, whose Discipline was Persuasion, lifted two fingers.

The students nearest the balcony turned suspiciously toward one another. A woman accused the man beside her of stealing bread. He raised his fist.

Eli seized a bucket and flung water across the floor. The false accusation reflected in it as a red thread running from Pell’s mouth to the students’ ears.

“Look! He is pulling it!”

Mira seized the thread with both hands—not with a Discipline, but with the grip of a laundress wringing cloth. It snapped.

The students looked up at Pell. Then they turned their own Disciplines on him.

They did not burn him. They did not tear out his voice. The freed students had learned how to sharpen attention, bind a statement to a witness, and make a promise impossible to evade. Forty-three voices spoke together.

“Stay.”

The word became a net.

Pell fell to his knees, held by every student he had once forced to listen. The other professors tried to flee. Ropes dropped from the galleries. Doors jammed under wedges. A mop handle blocked one latch; a nail disabled another hinge; a chair wedged beneath a doorknob. Ordinary tools defeated extraordinary authority.

At last, Pell tore the tenure seal from his throat and laid it on the floor. One by one, the others followed. The gold disks struck stone with small, defeated sounds.

Outside, the Ward’s white lamps went dark. Its great mouth sigil cracked from crown to chin.

Mira climbed the dais. For a moment she looked like the lecturer she had been, waiting for permission to begin. Then she faced the packed hall.

“This is not a new department. No one will be tested for the right to enter. No knowledge will be owned because someone stamped a name upon it.”

“What will we call it?” a student asked.

Eli looked at the broken windows, the ropes, the surrendered seals, and the people filling every tier.

“The Commons.”

The answer passed through the Ward—not as a spell, but as agreement.

By midnight, volunteers arrived from every street. Artisans brought food. Adjuncts brought ledgers. Former students brought keys. From the professors’ offices came university maps, debtor lists, and sealed letters naming those taken at the Foundry.

Kael was carried into the faculty lounge, where Mira made a bed from lecture cushions. Dax guarded the door. Tamsin assigned rooms to the freed students.

Eli placed Wren’s guild chain beside the grey codex on the central table.

The Ivory Ward had fallen. For the first time, Veritas had a headquarters where knowledge entered without a seal—and where the doors were held open from the inside.

## Chapter 8: The Citation Inquisition

The first warning was a spoon striking a cup three times with only one movement of the hand.

Eli watched it from the faculty lounge doorway. The volunteer holding the spoon frowned. Tea rose from her cup in a brown thread, returned to the kettle, and rose again.

Outside, the bells began to toll backward.

“Off the stairs,” Kael said.

His voice was scarcely more than breath. Eli turned. The black writing beneath his bandage had straightened into rows.

“Get everyone off the stairs.”

Dax came running from the entrance. Behind him, the corridor lengthened. A student running at his shoulder grew smaller without falling behind.

On the public screen above the hearth, the Commons’ chalk notice vanished. Three names appeared beneath a university crest.

DISTINGUISHED CHAIR OF TEMPORAL LOGIC.
DISTINGUISHED CHAIR OF HISTORICAL MEMORY.
DISTINGUISHED CHAIR OF SUBSTANTIAL FORMS.

Then: CITATION INQUISITION. REMAIN AVAILABLE FOR CORRECTION.

Mira smashed the screen with a poker.

The voice continued from its broken speaker.

“Evacuate through the laundry drain,” she shouted over it. “Leave the food. Take the wounded and the lists.”

Eli snatched the codex from the table and looped Wren’s chain around their neck. The links were cold against their collarbone.

Below, someone was pounding on the front doors. Not the invaders. The wood had turned clear as glass, and through it Eli could see volunteers trapped inside the thickness of the panels, their hands flattened against grain that still looked like oak.

Dax swung his hammer.

“Stop!” Eli caught his wrist. “You’ll hit them.”

A woman in a silver academic hood stepped through the wall beside the doors. Her tenure seal hung from a chain of little articulated bones. She touched a banister; iron softened and curled around Dax’s hammerhead.

“Property resumes its proper form,” she said.

The hammer became a lump of ore.

Dax stared at his empty grip. Eli pulled him toward the service passage as the floor behind them rose into a white partition.

Their barricades had taken hours. The Chair made a prison with one finger.

At the laundry stairs, Mira was tying ropes around people’s waists so that they could not be separated in the stretched corridors. Tamsin and two students carried Kael on a door. He kept his eyes shut, naming turns from memory.

“Where are we going?” one bearer asked.

“The drain,” Tamsin said.

“What drain?”

He looked at the door in his hands with bewilderment. Others stopped around him. A girl began crying because she could remember her room here but not why she had come to it.

Above the landing stood an old man in a hood lined with handwritten dates. He leafed through an invisible book.

“The occupation,” he said, “did not occur.”

Mira drove her poker through a water jug and splashed its contents over the bearers.

“Cold,” she said. “Wet. You are carrying someone. Take the next step.”

Tamsin took it. The others followed.

Eli wanted to thank Mira, but the reason kept slipping away. They pressed a link of Wren’s chain hard into their thumb.

A scarred hand. A kettle without a bottom. Rag first.

“Keep going,” they told Dax.

He seized their sleeve. “Together.”

From the landing, Eli could see the last volunteers crowding toward the open drain. Behind them came six professors in stained ivory robes, driven along with their former captors. Mira refused to leave anyone for the Chairs.

Then the stair between Eli and Dax repeated itself.

Dax stepped down, arrived above, stepped down again. His mouth formed the same furious word.

A tall professor carrying a pendulum descended through him as through a projection.

Eli backed against the laundry valve. The old binding pulled at their shoulder. They knew this pipe: hot wash water, not steam. They opened the waste cock and flung the scalding stream across the steps.

The professor recoiled. The pendulum jerked.

Dax stumbled free.

“Go!” Eli shouted.

This time he obeyed. He dropped into the drain after Mira; Tamsin’s lamp and Kael’s pale face were already disappearing beyond the grate.

Eli reached for the rope.

It became a line of black type.

“An interesting vocabulary,” said Chancellor Voss.

He stood on the landing without an escort. His dark robes were dry. At his throat, the Chancellor’s seal held a small, depthless light.

The three Chairs lowered their heads.

Eli tried to say Wren’s name.

Voss raised one hand.

“Plagiarism.”

There was no pain at first. Only an immense relief, as though a debt had been forgiven.

Then Eli could not remember their mother’s face.

They knew there had been a washing stall. They could picture steam rising from linen, but the person lifting it had become an empty place. Four terms at Veritas vanished into a clean white interval. Their name hovered beyond reach.

“Unattributed acquisition,” Voss said. “The penalty restores what has been improperly claimed.”

Eli opened their mouth. Nothing in it belonged to them.

Even grief was losing its object.

Against their chest lay something cold with many links.

They held it. Felt where one link had worn thin against another. Remembered a hand placing a rag over a leaking joint.

A true name means you’ve paid better attention.

Eli breathed the particular name of water held hot beneath iron. Not to burst the pipe. They had no command to give. They followed its sounds back to a bowl, a damp sleeve, a woman waiting while they learned.

Then the name of weight passing from stone to stone.

Then air caught at a window.

Their mother’s face did not return. But the emptiness acquired edges. Someone had been taken from it.

“Who taught you those?” Voss asked.

Eli tried again.

“Wren.”

The name stayed.

Voss stepped closer. He pulled the grey codex from inside Eli’s coat and opened it. His expression changed before he closed the cover.

“Not the holding cells,” he told the Chairs. “My sanctum. This one requires reeducation.”

Hands caught Eli’s arms. Their injured shoulder flared, and they clung to the pain because it was theirs.

As they were dragged up through the emptied Ward, Eli kept saying Wren’s name. Voss walked ahead with the book. Beyond the broken windows, the university loudspeakers announced that no occupation had occurred.

## Chapter 9: The Unwritten Truth

The Chancellor’s sanctum had a leaking window.

Rain gathered beneath the leaded glass and fell into a silver basin. Each drop sounded indecently ordinary. Eli watched it while the Chairs fastened their wrists to a reading desk.

“Outside,” Voss said.

The Chair with the pendulum hesitated. Voss looked at him, and the three withdrew.

Beneath the desk’s glass lay pages sewn together with copper wire. Their margins branched into cables that disappeared through the floor. Old vellum met new circuit board; between them, a little fan struggled against centuries of dust.

Voss set the grey codex beside an empty copper cradle.

“The foundation-access copy,” he said. “We thought it destroyed.”

“You left it under a library.”

“Institutions forget things.”

“People, too.”

He glanced at Eli’s face, assessing damage as a physician might assess a bruise. Eli tried to summon their mother and found the same unbearable absence.

Voss drew up a chair.

“You have seen the permissions beneath the Disciplines. Good. We need not waste time defending scripture.”

“So you know.”

“Of course I know. The Texts were composed. Revised. Enforced. Gravity has never required a professor’s signature.”

The admission came without shame. Eli had imagined breaking something to reach it.

“Then open the gates.”

“And give every angry apprentice the power to change iron into blood? You have met my colleagues. Imagine their gifts without even the restraint of examination.”

“I’ve met the people your examinations broke.”

Voss’s mouth tightened. “A city cannot run on kindness. Its bridges must hold. Its boilers must not explode because someone feels entitled to understand them.”

“Wren kept your boilers from exploding.”

“And perhaps deserved a degree.”

“She deserved to live.”

Rain ticked into the basin.

Voss opened the codex. It was blank from where Eli sat.

“I can restore your standing,” he said. “Give your vocabulary a protected place. A Chair of Practical Nomenclature. Workshops with funding, apprenticeships without debt. Help me repair the selection.”

Eli saw the trap almost tenderly: Wren’s hands cleaned and folded into a crest. Her words made into questions somebody else could fail.

“No Trials,” they whispered.

“What?”

“A promise I made.”

Voss slid the book into its cradle. Copper touched copper. Under the glass, small lettering brightened around the stitched pages.

“Then let us discover what you actually know.”

The desk tightened around Eli’s wrists.

Pain shot through their shoulder. The basin, the window, the pale place where their mother should have been—everything began to recede.

Eli caught a chain link between two fingers.

Water held hot beneath iron.

They spoke its particular name. Not an instruction to the desk. A way back to a workshop where someone had waited for them to get the sound right.

The pressure stopped increasing.

Voss bent over the codex.

On its open page, letters emerged.

WHAT DOES PRESSURE KNOW OF A SEAL?

He put a hand across them.

“An interrogative device,” he said. “Not an oracle.”

“Then answer it.”

“The world does not answer questions. We make arrangements so we can live in it.”

Eli looked down through the glass. Beneath Voss’s sleeve, a repeated clause pulsed along the copper branches: BY THE SOLE AUTHORITY OF—

They remembered Kael at the Infirmary window, showing them where a command ended and air began.

“You made an arrangement,” Eli said. “Then said it was the world.”

Voss raised two fingers. The rain stopped between window and bowl.

“An effective arrangement.”

Eli named the water at the window: not Wren’s held heat, but rain finding the gap where old lead had pulled from stone. They stumbled over the ending. Watched. Tried again.

One drop fell.

Voss’s fingers trembled.

“Careful.”

“Yes,” Eli said. “That was the first thing she taught me.”

They named the stone carrying the window’s weight. The air moving toward the tired fan. Each name required a pause, attention to something that had been here before the Chancellor entered and would remain after him.

On the page beneath his hand, ink spread between his fingers.

A NAME IS NOT A LICENSE.

The desk loosened. Eli pulled one wrist free, leaving skin against the clasp.

“You want my authority,” Voss said. He sounded suddenly certain, almost relieved. “That is what this is.”

“No.”

Eli turned the book so both of them could read it.

“Wren taught me. Kael helped me hear where I was wrong. Mira wrote things down so strangers could use them. None of that makes me the owner.”

The clause under the glass flickered. Eli could see more now: the first Chancellor’s name overwritten by a successor’s, then another’s. Voss’s sat uppermost, hammered deep by repetition.

“The boilers will still need tending,” Voss said.

“Yes.”

“People will make mistakes.”

“Yes. And you can tell them what you know. They should be able to ask how you know it.”

Eli laid their freed hand on the page, beside his.

They spoke the names learned in the commons: a joint that needed room to move, weight shared across an arch, breath passed from one mouth into another’s hearing. They included the hesitations, the corrections. Nothing complete. Nothing theirs alone.

The copper wires began to lift from the vellum.

Voss spoke a word Eli could not understand. His seal flared. For an instant the room seemed to bow toward him.

Then the basin overflowed onto his shoe.

He stared down.

Under the glass, SOLE split across a seam. The names of the dead Chancellors faded beneath it. Voss’s seal became a dull piece of metal against his throat.

Eli freed their other wrist.

Across the room, bolts withdrew from the sanctum doors. The fan kept turning. Rain kept coming through the neglected window.

Voss gripped the desk, waiting for something to obey him.

Eli tore a strip from their sleeve and pushed it into the gap beneath the lead.

“Hold the basin,” they said.

For a long moment he did not move.

Then he picked it up.

## Chapter 10: What Knowledge Is Worth

By sunrise, Veritas had forgotten how to glow.

The change traveled outward from the Chancellor’s tower in quiet failures. A professor in the Hall of Metals reached for the heat in a crucible and found only an ordinary flame. Three miles away, a registrar’s degree-lamp went dark. In the infirmary, a surgeon’s Doctorate of Severance became a stamped sheet of vellum, and the surgeon—after a long, frightened pause—continued stitching with hands that had always known how.

The city did not collapse. It stumbled.

Some professors wept. Some shouted that the commons had murdered civilization. Others stood in their lecture rooms, holding their dead seals, and discovered that their voices still carried without making the windows tremble. Students emerged from colleges to find that their examinations no longer hovered above their heads like verdicts. The degrees had lost their supernatural grip, but not their ink, their debts, or the habits built around them.

In the sanctum, Eli watched dawn gather in the basin beneath the patched window. Voss still held it, both hands around the brass rim. Without his seal he looked smaller, though not harmless: an old man in a Chancellor’s coat, surrounded by books that had once been treated as weapons.

“Do you mean to imprison me?” he asked.

“I don’t know.” Eli wrung water from the torn cloth. Their shoulder ached; their wrist had stiffened into a red line. “That is the trouble with winning.”

The doors stood open. Beyond them, guards and professors waited in the corridor, no longer sure whose orders mattered. Mira arrived first, with Tamsin and Dax behind her. Kael came supported between two students, his citation-wounded arm bound tightly. His face was pale, but his eyes were awake.

Dax saw Voss and flexed his bandaged hand. “We could settle one question quickly.”

“We have enough quick answers,” Mira said. “They built this place.”

Voss looked at her. “And what will you build?”

Nobody answered at once.

They carried the grey codex down from its copper cradle. It had stopped writing questions. Its blank pages were not blank anymore, though. Names filled them in many hands: the woman who could mend a cracked gear by listening to its teeth, the boy who knew which mushrooms poisoned a boiler crew, the Foundry worker who could tell when pulp had been overboiled by its smell. Beneath those were the names of the captured volunteers, the dead, and the debts still being demanded by bursars who had not yet understood that their magic was gone.

The Great Library became the first argument.

Its professors claimed the books required guardians. The freed Foundry students answered that guardianship had been the word used for centuries when possession was meant. Dax proposed opening every locked door and letting people take what they needed. Mira asked who would preserve a book once rain entered through a broken roof, or who would translate a text whose language no one in the room knew.

“Open everything,” Tamsin said, “but don’t pretend access is the same as welcome. Put tables in the streets. Copy the books. Teach people how to mend them.”

Kael, seated beneath a library arch, rubbed at the black writing in his arm. “And record uncertainty. If a page says the moon causes fever, write beside it that this is a claim, not a command.”

“That sounds like another Discipline,” Dax said.

“It sounds like caution.”

“It sounds like a badge someone will sell.”

The argument moved through three rooms and changed shape each time. They would need librarians, but not lords. Teachers, but not gatekeepers. Standards, but not Trials. A person might know less than another person without being less human, less entitled to ask, or less welcome at the table.

Mira took a piece of slate and wrote: NO SEALED KNOWLEDGE.

Tamsin added: NO UNANSWERABLE AUTHORITY.

Kael stared at both lines. “Those are promises,” he said. “Not machinery.”

“Machinery can be inspected,” Eli said. “Promises can be broken.”

“Then we inspect the promises,” Mira replied.

They agreed to establish open houses in every ward, with books copied into common rooms and lessons offered without fees or credentials. Anyone could teach, but no one could claim mastery merely because others had agreed to kneel. Questions and corrections would be written beside the lessons. The arrangement was imperfect before the ink dried.

Voss listened from a chair near the broken window. At last he said, “You will recreate the university. Committees, archives, examinations—only with different names.”

“Perhaps,” Eli said.

“Then you have not escaped us.”

“No,” they said. “We have learned that escape is not a door. It is noticing who keeps making the locks.”

They did not execute Voss. They did not make him Chancellor of the old order’s ruins, either. He was placed under public guard in a disused tutorial hall, where he would answer questions about the Texts and the machinery that enforced them. He complained that this was a trial.

“It is an accounting,” Mira told him. “You can leave when the people you ruled decide you have answered enough.”

Weeks later, Eli returned to Wren’s workshop.

The pipes still crossed the walls like thick iron roots. The workbench still bore the burn mark where Wren had set down her kettle. Someone had swept the ash from the floor, but no one had repaired the little window. Eli had left it that way. Air came through. Rain came through. The room remembered that walls were not the same as safety.

A child named Sella crouched beside the lowest pipe, listening with her cheek against the metal. She was the daughter of a laundry worker and had arrived with a question about knocking sounds.

“What is it called?” Eli asked.

“Pipe.”

“That is what it is made of. Not what it is doing.”

Sella frowned and listened again. Beneath the knock ran a tremor, regular as a small heartbeat.

“Pressure?”

“Closer.” Eli placed two fingers against the iron. “Don’t repeat me. Hear it.”

The pipe shivered under their hands. Somewhere beyond the wall, a valve turned. The tremor changed.

Sella’s eyes widened. “It says it is holding.”

Eli smiled, though the answer left a hollow place behind their ribs. “That is its true name today.”

“And tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow you listen again.”

Outside, Veritas argued in a hundred uncredentialed voices. The old seals were dead. New ones were already being proposed. Eli could not tell whether knowledge had been freed or merely invited into a longer conversation.

They took up Wren’s chain, felt the worn links warm in their palm, and leaned closer to the pipe with the child.

Together, they listened.
