“Then we move now. He’s already threatened this fountain.”

She turned to the porter. “Take the paper packet and the terminal to the maintenance kiosk by the lower tram arch. Bring the corrected file up on its screen. Do not call it received there until the kiosk reads every line back.”

“I can carry it,” the student said.

“You can read it. The porter can keep it dry.”

The porter tucked the packet beneath his coat. “And the queue?”

Follow turned toward the people waiting. “Keep to the basin. No opening at another fountain until the correction is verified. If pressure drops here, send someone to tell me before touching the cabinet.”

The student looked toward the bailiff. “What if he shuts it after we leave?”

“Then the witnesses know who did it,” Follow said. “And you know the gauge reading.”

She stepped away from the cabinet. The bailiff shifted to block her path, but he did not seize her. His authority was strongest when everyone pretended it was weightless. Follow walked past him anyway, shoulder brushing the cold edge of his armor. Pain pulled through her injured arm, sharp enough to blur the court for a breath. She kept moving.

They crossed beneath the arcade toward the lower tram arch. The porter went first, easing sideways through the outer crowd, while the student followed with the terminal held against his ribs. Follow stayed between them and the bailiff’s line of sight until the fountain court opened behind her. Only then did she hear the bailiff call, “Return for questioning.”

She did not turn around.

At the arch, the university’s old wall narrowed beside a modern service lane. Tram cables hummed overhead. A maintenance kiosk stood beneath a painted saint whose stone face had been replaced by a camera lens. Its screen flickered when the porter pressed the local access rune.

“Power is intermittent,” he said.

“Use the hand crank.”

He opened the panel and began turning. The screen brightened by degrees, displaying a blank receipt field. Follow placed the paper packet on the metal shelf but kept her hand over it.

“To the receiving station,” she said. “Read the source.”

The kiosk spoke in a flat mechanical voice: “Unverified junction report. Infirmary branch. Corrected isolation position: isolation valve before the regulator. Pressure reading attached. Awaiting witness confirmation.”

The student’s shoulders loosened.

“Again,” Follow ordered.

The machine repeated the message. This time, the porter checked each phrase against the paper. At the line concerning the valve, he stopped it.

“The label says isolation valve after the regulator,” he said. “Not before.”

The student corrected the file. The screen flashed red, then accepted the alteration.

Follow waited. “Now read it back with the correction.”

The kiosk did so, word for word.

Only then did she let go of the packet. “Now it has been received here. Not verified.”

A small icon appeared beside the message: SECOND OBSERVER REQUIRED.

The student looked toward the upper service stairs. “I can go back to the junction.”

“Not alone,” Follow said. “The bailiff may call that interference.”

The porter raised his hand. “I’ll watch the court. Send someone from the queue.”

Follow faced the tram arch, where the service stairs climbed toward the fountain court and the signal lines above it. “Then we find someone who can see the valve and name what they see.”

Behind them, the kiosk continued repeating the corrected map into its local memory. Ahead, the city’s cables throbbed with messages belonging to lords, scholars, laborers, and now, increasingly, one another. Follow started up the stairs, carrying no degree and no seal—only a verified beginning and a rebellion learning how carefully it had to know.


Follow climbed with her injured arm tucked against her ribs and her good hand sliding along the service rail. The stairs had been built for barrels, not bodies; each step was narrow, worn hollow by generations of porters hauling coal, copper wire, and contraband lecture notes between the university-city’s levels. Above, the fountain court showed through the grated landings in brief squares: pale stone, the shutoff cabinet, the queue gathered around the basin. Tram cables crossed the opening like black vines, humming whenever a carriage passed beyond the wall.

The kiosk’s receipt stayed warm in her pocket, its local acceptance stamped across the paper in blue pixels. She touched it once, then pulled her hand away. Receipt was not verification. A machine had repeated the corrected map, but no machine had yet seen the valve, matched the painted label, and sworn that the water could be trusted. The distinction seemed fussy only to people who had never watched a wrong instruction travel faster than a cry.

At the next landing, a service speaker crackled. “Unauthorized movement from the lower arch is subject to review.”

Follow stopped beneath it. The speaker bore the carved seal of the College of Civic Measurement, a professor’s crown worked in brass above a camera lens. Once, she had believed such seals meant someone had done the measuring. Now she knew they often meant someone had inherited the right to object.

She pressed the call plate. “This is a public maintenance route. I’m returning to the fountain court to obtain an independent witness for an unverified junction correction.”

The speaker clicked. No answer came.

“Record that,” she said, and continued upward.

At the final landing, the service door stood ajar. Voices carried through the gap. The bailiff was speaking to the queue in the controlled tone of a man who considered volume a form of law.

“No further handling of the cabinet will occur until the authority of the report is established.”

Follow pushed the door open with her shoulder.

“Agreed,” she said. “And no closure will occur until the authority of the live gauge is established.”

Heads turned. The kitchen porter was not there; only the student stood near the inspection door with the terminal hugged to his chest. His face brightened when he saw her, then tightened as he looked past her toward the stairs.

“Where’s the porter?” Follow asked.

“Watching the kiosk and the lower arch. He said he’d send anyone willing to observe.”

“Good. You stay here.”

The student glanced toward the exposed junction. “I can identify the valve.”

“You have already identified it. You cannot be the only observer after correcting the report yourself.”

The bailiff stepped from beside the cabinet. His blue seal cast a cold glow over the stone. “You have returned despite being instructed to report for questioning.”

“I have returned with a local receipt and a request for independent observation.”

“You are evading lawful procedure.”

“No. I’m refusing to let procedure replace evidence.” Follow kept her voice level, though her arm throbbed with every heartbeat. “Question me after the junction is witnessed. If you close the branch first, your questions will not restore pressure to the infirmary.”

A murmur ran through the queue. The bailiff’s gaze moved across the waiting faces, measuring them as if each witness were a possible obstruction. Follow saw his hand hover near the cabinet’s latch, but he did not touch it.

The terminal chimed. A message had arrived through the volunteer line. The student read it aloud. “The porter says someone from the laundry queue is coming up. They have watched the pressure gauge before and can reach the inspection door.”

“Not enough,” Follow said. “They must see the valve and name its position without being shown the corrected map.”

The student nodded, though uncertainty showed in the way he tightened his grip on the terminal. Follow stepped beside him and pointed toward the inspection door.

“You will display the photograph only after the witness has described the junction. Ask for the pipe order, the regulator, the isolation valve, and the branch. No prompting. If the description differs from the file, we stop and correct the file again.”

“And the fountain?” someone asked from the queue.

“Stays as it is,” Follow answered. “Running water remains running. No other fountain opens. We do not trade one mistake for a larger one.”

The answer moved through the court, repeated by people at the back until it became a collective instruction rather than her private command. Follow felt the rebellion’s shape changing around her. It had begun with anger at a professor’s seal and a bailiff’s gauntlet; now it depended on ordinary people refusing to claim knowledge they did not possess. That was harder than defiance. It was also harder for the aristocracy to counterfeit.

Footsteps sounded on the stairs below. The approaching witness paused once, then continued. Follow positioned herself where the inspection door, the gauge, and the witness’s path were all visible. She kept the corrected receipt folded in her pocket and the old map out of sight.

The bailiff looked at her. “You are making a public demonstration of a technical matter.”

“Technical matters become public when public water depends on them.”

Above the court, a tram glided across the open span, its glass sides reflecting towers, turrets, antenna dishes, and the banners of colleges that owned whole districts. The city carried degrees through its wires like hereditary magic. Follow watched the approaching witness cast a shadow across the stairwell and waited for the first independent description.

No fountain opened. No map was called verified. But the second observer was coming, and this time the truth would have more than one pair of eyes.


The witness reached the top landing with one hand pressed to the wall and the other gripping a laundry basket. Steam clung to her sleeves, carrying the sour scent of soap and hot metal into the fountain court. She was older than Follow had expected, with silver threaded through her cropped hair and a brass queue token hanging from her neck. The token identified no college, only the laundry ward’s communal workhouse. It had no enchantment that Follow could see.

“You’re the one who sent for me?” the witness asked.

“I am. Before you look at any report or terminal, tell us what you saw below.”

The witness glanced at the bailiff, then at the open inspection door. “I saw the gauge on the infirmary feed while waiting at the laundry cistern. It was holding steady. The pipe from the lower arch comes in along the floor. The isolation valve sits on the upright section, before the regulator. The regulator is above it, and the branch splits after both.”

The student made a sharp sound of relief.

Follow raised her good hand. “Do not react. Let her finish.”

The witness frowned at the exposed junction. “The public line runs beneath the branch. The infirmary line turns upward after the valve. If someone closes that valve, the upper branch loses water while the lower line may keep pressure.”

“Did you read that from a map?” Follow asked.

“No. I watched the maintenance crew replace the regulator after the old one burst. They made me move the laundry carts out of the way.” She pointed through the inspection door. “The valve handle is painted yellow. It is before the regulator, not after it.”

Behind Follow, the queue stirred. Someone whispered the words before and after as if testing their weight. The bailiff remained beside the cabinet, his blue seal dimmed but ready. Follow kept her attention on the witness.

“Walk to the door,” she said. “Do not touch the pipes. Describe what you can see from there.”

The witness set down her basket. Follow moved with her, keeping the route clear and watching the floor for the slick patches left by the fountain spray. Her injured arm protested when she opened the inspection gate, but she forced it wide enough for the witness to stand beneath the brass inspection lamp. The lamp’s lens woke as it detected movement, casting white lines across the pipes.

“There,” the witness said. “Yellow handle. Regulator above. Branch turns after both.”

The student held out the terminal. “Now look at the photograph.”

“Don’t show it yet,” Follow said.

The bailiff’s voice cut across the court. “The witness has already been coached by the crowd.”

Follow turned toward him. “She has described the junction without seeing the corrected file. That is the condition you demanded.”

“I demanded an authorized inspection.”

“You demanded a reason to keep the cabinet closed.”

His gauntleted hand lifted toward the latch. The queue compressed behind Follow, not retreating but making a wall of bodies between the cabinet and the basin. The fountain continued to run, its stream bright beneath the arcade. Follow heard the faint ticking of the live gauge inside the cabinet and understood that everyone else heard it too.

The bailiff stopped with his hand inches from the latch. “You are obstructing my authority.”

“No,” Follow said. “We are preserving the evidence your authority is supposed to protect. Step away from the cabinet until the live reading is recorded.”

For a moment she expected the blue seal to flare. Instead, the bailiff looked toward the terminal, then toward the witness, and left his hand suspended beside the latch. Follow felt no triumph. A seal did not become harmless merely because a crowd watched it. It became accountable.

“Record the reading,” she told the student.

He crouched beside the gauge and held the terminal’s camera over the inspection window. Numbers crawled across the screen. “Stable pressure. Infirmary branch live. Public return live.”

“Read the positions.”

“Regulator open. Isolation valve open. Branch receiving.”

The witness leaned closer without crossing the marked line. “That is what I saw from the laundry cistern.”

Follow nodded. “Now show her the corrected photograph.”

The student rotated the terminal. The witness studied the image, then looked back at the pipes. “The photograph matches the junction. But the caption is wrong.”

The student’s face drained. “Which caption?”

“Here.” She tapped the screen without touching it. “It says the isolation valve is after the regulator. The painted pipes say before. The drawing is corrected, but the text underneath is not.”

A murmur broke through the court, louder than before. Follow seized the terminal and enlarged the caption. The kiosk receipt in her pocket had repeated the same error-free line only because the student had corrected it at the lower arch. The photograph itself still carried the inherited description, ready to mislead anyone who trusted the image’s authority more than the metal before them.

“Read it into the record,” Follow said. “The witness identified the valve independently. The image agrees with the physical junction. The caption remains defective.”

The student began typing. His fingers shook, but the terminal accepted each word and sent it through the volunteer relay. Far beyond the court, the message would reach the gatehouse room, the kiosk, and the lower wards where people were waiting beside dry basins.

The witness picked up her laundry basket. “Does this mean the other fountains can open?”

“Not yet,” Follow said. “This junction is independently described, and the live gauge is recorded. The corrected file still needs the caption amended and acknowledged. No one gets to turn a partial truth into a new order.”

The crowd shifted, but no one argued. Follow saw the student append the witness’s statement, then transmit a request for the caption correction. On the terminal, a new status appeared: **SECOND OBSERVATION ATTACHED — VERIFICATION PENDING AMENDMENT.**

It was not victory. It was a door that could no longer be quietly locked.

Follow faced the bailiff. “You may question me now, if you wish. But the fountain remains open, the cabinet remains untouched, and the record travels with witnesses attached.”

He lowered his hand from the latch.

Beyond the court, tram bells rang through the university-city, passing over stone bridges and beneath the towers where professors ruled by inherited degrees. Follow listened to the water strike the basin and watched the corrected statement spread from screen to screen. Knowledge, she thought, was not the title stamped on a scholar’s ring. It was the courage to let another person look closely—and the discipline to admit when the label beneath the truth was still wrong.


The status on the terminal remained stubbornly amber: **VERIFICATION PENDING AMENDMENT**. Follow watched it pulse beside the witness’s statement while the fountain poured behind her, each clear stream striking the basin with the authority of something that did not need a degree to prove it was wet. Around the court, the queue had become a ring of observers. No one touched the cabinet. No one allowed the bailiff to touch it either.

“Questioning,” the bailiff said. “Now.”

Follow kept her eyes on the screen. “Ask where everyone can hear you.”

“That is not your decision.”

“It is when your questions may be used to remove the only person preserving the evidence.” She turned the terminal toward the student. “Start a public transcript. Include the cabinet status, the live reading, the witness’s independent description, and the defective caption. Send a duplicate to the relay room and the kiosk.”

The student’s fingers moved. A green transmission line appeared, then split into three smaller paths on the display. One traveled toward the gatehouse, another toward the lower arch, and the third climbed through the university-city’s municipal mesh. Follow imagined the signal passing beneath lecture halls and countinghouses, through copper conduits installed by engineering colleges and rune-lit routers maintained by apprentices who had never been permitted to sit for a degree. The aristocracy owned the towers, but not every wire running through their foundations.

The bailiff stepped nearer. His boots stopped at the chalk boundary around the cabinet. “State your name and academic standing.”

“Follow. No academic standing.”

A few people in the queue inhaled sharply. The words still had the power to make her feel exposed, though she had said them before professors, guards, and whole squares of angry students. In the university-city, a degree was not merely education. It was a coat of arms, a legal privilege, and often a literal force. A Master of Bridges could command iron to knit itself across a ravine. A Doctor of Civic Measure could make disputed numbers settle into official truth. Follow had dropped out before any certificate could grant her such power. She had only her hands, her scars, and the stubborn habit of checking what powerful people told everyone else to believe.

“Reason for unauthorized interference with a regulated installation?” the bailiff asked.

“I did not interfere with it. I prevented an unauthorized closure.”

“On whose authority?”

“On the authority of the live water, the open valve, and the people standing here.” She pointed toward the witness. “She independently identified the junction. The student recorded the pressure. You witnessed both. If you claim the evidence is invalid, state which observation failed and why.”

The bailiff’s blue seal brightened. A faint pressure gathered in the air, the beginning of a binding clause. The crowd shifted but did not retreat. Follow’s injured arm throbbed against her ribs, and she planted her boots more firmly.

The witness lifted her basket. “He can’t call it invalid because he dislikes who saw it.”

The student glanced up from the terminal. “The transcript is mirrored. The relay volunteers are asking whether they should attach the earlier photograph and the original caption.”

“Yes,” Follow said. “Attach both. Mark the caption as defective, not corrected. No one is to open another fountain based on the photograph until the amendment is acknowledged.”

A cheer rose from the back of the queue, quickly swallowed by the echoing court. Follow felt its heat and resisted the temptation to turn it into a speech. A rebellion could be fed by anger, but it could not be built from anger alone. The professors knew how to absorb a riot. They could declare it ignorance, summon bailiffs, and let the archives describe the crowd as an undisciplined mass. What they could not easily absorb was a record that named every error, preserved every witness, and refused to claim more certainty than the evidence allowed.

The relay terminal chimed again. The student read from the incoming message. “The lower-ward volunteers confirm the packet arrived. The kitchen porter says three dry fountains are being watched, but nobody is opening them. He asks what instruction to give the waiting households.”

“Tell them to gather witnesses and record the existing conditions,” Follow said. “Check the inlet, the regulator, the isolation valve, and the return. No handles moved. No cabinet opened. If pressure changes on its own, record that before anyone touches anything.”

She moved toward the wall console, then stopped at the chalk boundary. “I’m going to send that instruction through the public board.”

The student looked at her injured arm. “You should sit.”

“I will sit when the record can stand without me.”

He opened the console’s external channel. Follow pressed her palm to the reader. The device rejected her first attempt, displaying **UNREGISTERED ACADEMIC USER**. Laughter flickered through the court, not at her but at the absurdity of the machine. She removed the receipt from her pocket and held its blue acceptance stamp beneath the scanner. The console hesitated, then admitted her as a temporary public operator.

She dictated slowly. “No fountain opens on inherited text alone. A correction must match the metal, the gauge, and an independent observer. If the caption is wrong, mark it wrong. If the record is incomplete, leave the water where it is and keep looking.”

The words appeared across the public board in bright white letters. Beyond the arcade, tram windows reflected them as they slid past the court, carrying the message toward towers where tenured professors ruled behind jeweled doors. Follow watched the first acknowledgments arrive from the relay volunteers, then from the kiosk.

The bailiff’s binding clause faded without being spoken. He remained beside the cabinet, accountable to the transcript and the witnesses surrounding him.

Follow folded the receipt and returned it to her pocket. The caption was still defective. The fountains were still closed. But the lower wards now had a method instead of a command, and the rebellion had gained something more durable than applause: people willing to verify one another’s knowledge in public.


The terminal chimed while Follow was trying to fold her fingers without pulling at her injured arm. A message from the kiosk filled the screen: **AMENDMENT READY. LOCAL OPERATOR MUST CONFIRM SOURCE AND SCOPE.** Beneath it, the defective caption remained visible. She read both twice. The machine had not forgiven the error or buried it. For once, delay seemed useful.

“The lower arch,” she said. “I have to return to the kiosk.”

“You have not been released,” the bailiff said.

Follow leaned against the console until the sudden dizziness passed. “Then finish your questions here. Or walk with us and ask them on the record.”

“You admit distributing an unauthorized instruction?”

“An instruction to observe without touching anything. Record the whole sentence.”

The student read it back, including the prohibition on opening cabinets. Follow waited until the bailiff nodded at the transcription. “Any other question?”

His seal glimmered against his breastplate. “Your answers will be referred for review.”

“Include your proposed cabinet closure, and the readings taken while the cabinet remained untouched.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No. It is my statement for the same review.”

The student entered it. After a moment, the bailiff supplied his seal number when Follow asked. He did not declare her free, but he made no further demand that she remain. She would not mistake that for safety. It was enough to move.

She asked the laundry witness to stay with the fountain queue and report any change. Then she crossed the court with the student, taking the shallow steps beneath the arcade rather than the steep service stairs. The bailiff followed. At the arch, two more bailiffs shifted into their path.

“The fountain stays running,” Follow told them. “If you intend to interfere, state the reason to the witnesses before you act.”

One looked past her toward the waiting queue. Neither answered. She walked between them, close enough to smell rain in their wool capes.

Outside, the university-city rose in terraces of stone and illuminated glass. Trams crawled beneath bridges bearing the heraldic shields of endowed chairs. High above, a professor’s doctoral halo shone as he lifted a delivery carriage over his private gate; below him, porters dragged another carriage around a broken pavement. Follow watched only long enough to find her footing. Once she had imagined graduation would lift her out of that lower street. Dropping out had left her there, learning how much of the city depended on people its charters called unqualified.

At the maintenance kiosk beneath the lower tram arch, the porter had cleared a space between waiting households. He offered her a stool without comment. She sat before pride could object.

“Three reports,” he said, laying printed sheets beside the scanner. “Two incomplete. One has an outside gauge and a sight panel. Nobody has moved anything.”

“Keep them separate.”

“Already done.”

She looked up at him, grateful for the small correction.

The student placed his terminal beside the kiosk display. Follow fed her blue-stamped receipt beneath the reader. The original photograph appeared beside the replacement caption: **ISOLATION VALVE BEFORE REGULATOR; INFIRMARY BRANCH AFTER BOTH. THIS DESCRIPTION APPLIES TO THE OBSERVED INFIRMARY JUNCTION ONLY.**

She traced the words with her good finger.

“Read the witness’s statement against it,” she said.

The student read. The porter checked the photograph. Behind them, the bailiff stood where he could see both screens. Follow asked him whether the proposed wording differed from the junction he had witnessed.

“No,” he said.

“Put that in too.”

The kiosk demanded the source of the amendment. Its menu offered faculty authority, licensed survey, and delegated operator. Follow chose the last, then attached the live reading, the independent statement, and the photograph. In the explanation field she typed slowly: **Earlier caption reversed the order of valve and regulator. Earlier version retained as defective. No authorization for other sites.**

A final prompt asked for her academic credential.

She stared at it.

The porter reached past her, not toward the confirmation key but toward a small tab at the bottom. “Public receipt,” he said.

Follow opened it. The scanner accepted her stamp.

She had almost let the menu tell her she could not proceed. That frightened her more than the bailiff standing behind her.

The screen changed: **AMENDMENT ACKNOWLEDGED — OBSERVED JUNCTION VERIFIED.** Below, the three lower-ward reports remained unverified.

Someone cheered. Follow raised her hand.

“This one,” she said, tapping the confirmed junction. “Not those.”

The porter turned the display so the waiting households could see the difference. A woman near the front bent to read the restriction aloud to a child. The student sent the acknowledged amendment through the relay, then requested separate independent observations for the incomplete reports.

“Does this make you our professor?” someone asked.

Follow looked at the stool, the borrowed terminal, the receipt softening with sweat in her palm. She could imagine how easily gratitude might become a chair nobody dared question.

“No,” she said. “You found an error in what we sent. Keep doing that.”

She pushed the next report into the space between herself and the porter, leaving room for the student’s screen beside it. Above the tram arch, the tenured lords’ banners still hung. Here, beneath them, another pair of hands reached forward to help check the page.
