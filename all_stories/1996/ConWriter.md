The roof of the old public library was the only place in Bellwether where I could see the whole neighborhood without seeing anyone’s face. From up there, the streets looked like lines drawn by a tired hand: narrow, cracked, and shining with rain. Beyond the apartment blocks, the river pressed against its concrete walls. Every time it swelled, the city sent out an alert telling us not to worry. Every time it shrank, another pipe broke.

I had been told not to climb the library roof, which was exactly why I had chosen it for my experiment.

The rain gauge stood beside me in a plastic lunch container, its wires twisting into a circuit board I had soldered at the kitchen table. A tiny green light blinked whenever the sensor measured water. It was supposed to send readings to the neighborhood emergency network, but the network required a connection I couldn’t afford. So I had built a second device from an old phone, a bicycle dynamo, and the library’s abandoned rooftop antenna.

“Come on,” I whispered.

The phone screen flickered. One line of numbers appeared, then vanished. I leaned closer, shielding it from the rain with my sleeve. The gauge had recorded the water level behind the southern floodwall. That mattered because people in the low blocks—people like us—usually heard about danger after the streets had already begun filling.

I tapped the screen again. Nothing.

A hard gust shoved against me, and my sneaker slid on the wet roof. I caught the antenna pole before I went over the edge. For a second, my heart hammered so loudly that I couldn’t hear the rain. Then I pulled myself upright and stared down at the alley, where the library’s locked side door waited six floors below.

“Great,” I muttered. “Hero saves the world by falling off a building.”

My name was Hero because my mother had wanted something brave and my father had apparently been too tired to argue. At school, people treated it like a joke. I treated it like a dare. A name didn’t make anyone courageous, but it could make quitting embarrassing.

The phone buzzed.

I froze. A message had arrived from the city’s public-data server, not the emergency network. The subject line read: **South Floodwall Sensor Decommissioning Notice**.

I opened it. The city planned to remove the old sensor at the southern wall and replace it with a “centralized predictive model.” The explanation said the model would be more efficient. Beneath it was a list of affected streets. Mine was first.

I read the notice twice, then a third time, because the words seemed designed to slide away from ordinary understanding. The old sensor was ugly, unreliable, and mounted to a wall that leaked rust. But it measured what was happening now. A prediction could be wrong and still look impressive in a report.

My homemade gauge blinked green again.

I crouched beside it and pulled the memory card from its casing. The readings were there: water climbing faster than the city’s public dashboard showed. Not by much yet, but enough that the difference would matter if the rain continued. I opened the message app and typed to the neighborhood council’s public number.

**The southern wall sensor is being removed. My gauge is recording higher water levels than the city dashboard. I have proof.**

My thumb hovered over Send. If I sent the message, adults might laugh. They might ask who I was, where I got the equipment, whether I had permission to be on the roof. They might confiscate the device. Worse, they might believe me and discover that I had no plan beyond showing them a blinking light.

Then another line appeared in the city notice: **Community access to raw sensor data will end upon decommissioning.**

That decided it.

I attached the readings, the photograph I had taken through the library’s upper window, and a short explanation of how the gauge worked. I sent everything to the council, the local radio station, and the student newspaper. The message disappeared into the network.

For several seconds, nothing happened. Rain tapped the plastic container. The river moved beyond the buildings, brown and broad.

Then my phone chimed.

Not a reply. A delivery notice.

Someone had forwarded my message to two hundred and seventeen people.

I stared at the number until it blurred. My experiment was no longer mine. It was out in the neighborhood, traveling from screen to screen, carrying a warning I had been too frightened to say aloud.

Below me, a light came on in one of the apartment windows. Then another. I packed the gauge carefully into my bag and approached the stairwell door. The lock would still be there, and the roof would still be forbidden, but I had an appointment with the truth now.

The city was about to take away the evidence.

I intended to give everyone a way to make their own.


The stairwell door groaned when I pushed it open, and the sound seemed loud enough to wake every dead librarian below. I slipped inside, dragging my bag close so the rain gauge would not knock against the railing. Water ran from my sleeves onto the concrete steps. Behind me, the roof door swung once in the wind, then slammed shut.

I stopped on the landing and listened. No footsteps. No angry voice calling security. Only rain ticking against the narrow windows and the faint hum of the building’s old electrical system. I had climbed onto the roof through a maintenance hatch because the library’s main entrance was locked, but the hatch had led to a corridor on the fifth floor. If I could reach the public computers, I could make the readings something more durable than an attachment buried in two hundred and seventeen phones.

The fifth-floor corridor smelled of wet dust and old paper. Most of the library had been closed since the city cut its funding, but the building still held its shape: shelves behind plastic sheets, painted signs pointing toward rooms no one used, a mural of children reading beneath a sky that had faded almost white. I walked past it and found the stairwell leading down. My shoes squeaked with every step.

On the third floor, a motion light flickered on. I ducked behind a book cart and held my breath. A red security indicator blinked beside the elevator. I had never seen it active before. Maybe the city had remembered the roof existed. Maybe someone had noticed the message traveling through Bellwether and decided the girl trespassing above the library was easier to catch than the problem she had measured.

The light went out. I moved.

The public computer room was still unlocked. Six dusty machines sat beneath a row of windows, their screens black. I set my bag on the desk and connected the old phone to the nearest computer with a charging cable. The first three ports did nothing. The fourth recognized it.

A folder opened, full of numbers arranged in columns. I copied the readings into a plain text file, then added the time each measurement had been taken and the method I had used. I wrote slowly, checking every line twice. No dramatic claims. No “the city is lying.” Just water height, rainfall, location, and the difference between my gauge and the public dashboard.

At the bottom, I typed: **Anyone can compare these readings with the mark on the southern floodwall. Photograph the mark. Record the time. Upload both.**

That was the beginning of a verification network, though I did not call it that yet. I called the file **southwall_raw.txt**, because plain names were harder to misunderstand.

The computer’s browser still opened the city data portal. I uploaded the file to the public archive, but a warning appeared: **Account required. Authorized users only.** I tried the neighborhood council’s page. The same warning. My stomach tightened. The city had made public data available only to people who knew how to request permission for it.

Then I remembered the library’s old bulletin-board system. Years ago, students had used it to exchange homework and announcements. The server was probably ancient, but the library’s network still appeared in the computer’s list of available connections. I searched through the system folders until I found a directory labeled COMMUNITY_UPLOADS.

My fingers trembled as I dragged the file into it.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then the folder refreshed, and **southwall_raw.txt** appeared among notices for lost cats, tutoring sessions, and a knitting circle that had probably disbanded years ago.

I let out a breath that almost became a laugh.

I printed the file, the photograph, and a short set of instructions. The printer complained, swallowed one page, and finally produced twelve crooked copies. I folded them into my backpack, leaving one on the desk beneath the computer screen. At the top, I wrote the address of the community folder in thick black marker.

A metallic clatter sounded in the corridor.

I froze.

The elevator doors opened somewhere nearby. Voices carried through the stairwell—two adults, too muffled to identify. One voice said, “Check the upper levels.” The other answered, “The notice went out already. We just need the equipment.”

They were looking for my gauge.

I shoved the phone, memory card, and printed pages into my bag. The rain gauge was heavier than it should have been, its wires catching on the zipper. I yanked until the bag closed, then crawled beneath the computer desk as footsteps entered the room.

A flashlight swept across the floor.

I pressed one hand over my mouth and watched the beam pause on the page I had left behind. The person holding it picked it up. Paper rustled.

“Someone’s been using this,” a voice said.

The flashlight moved closer. My heart beat against the floorboards.

Then the building’s old alarm began to ring—not the security alarm, but the fire bell, shrill and sudden. The voices cursed. A door slammed. Footsteps hurried toward the stairwell.

I did not wait to learn why it had sounded. I slipped through the emergency exit at the back of the computer room, pushed through the heavy door, and followed the painted arrows down to the ground floor. The exit opened beside the library’s loading bay, where rain blew across the pavement in silver sheets.

I stepped outside with my bag under my coat. Behind me, the alarm continued screaming. Ahead, beyond the alley, Bellwether’s apartment windows glowed one by one.

The file was online. The instructions were printed. The evidence no longer depended on my being believed.

I crossed the alley toward home, already planning what the neighborhood would measure next.


Halfway across the alley, my phone buzzed again. I nearly dropped it. The rain had made the screen slippery, and the bag strap kept sliding from my shoulder. I ducked beneath the fire escape of the building opposite the library and wiped the phone against my shirt.

The message was from the city data server.

**SOUTHERN FLOODWALL SENSOR: DECOMMISSIONING WORK ORDER ACCEPTED. CREW DISPATCHED.**

Below it, in smaller letters, was an estimated completion window: **before the next public reporting cycle**.

I read it twice. The city had not merely announced the sensor’s removal. It had scheduled it. Somewhere, a truck might already be moving through Bellwether with a crane, a toolbox, and permission to erase the one instrument that could prove the dashboard was wrong.

My hand tightened around the phone. The memory card was in my backpack, tucked beside the printed instructions. The rain gauge was inside my backpack, pressed between the printed instructions and my mathematics notebook. I had protected the evidence from the library, but protection was not the same as using it. If the sensor disappeared before anyone checked my numbers, the city could call the difference a mistake.

I opened the neighborhood message app and scrolled through the people who had received my first warning. Most had not replied. A few had sent question marks. One person had written, **How do we know the gauge works?** Another had answered, **We don’t. That’s why she says to compare it.**

I stared at that exchange until a plan took shape—not a perfect plan, only one with steps I could actually complete. I needed people at the wall before the crew arrived. They could photograph the gauge, the water marks, and my device readings. If several people recorded the same thing independently, the evidence would survive even if the city removed its own sensor.

I created a new message.

**The southern sensor may be removed before the next report. If you live near the low blocks, please go to the floodwall with a phone. Do not cross barriers. Photograph the painted water mark, record the time shown on your phone, and compare the height with the instructions in COMMUNITY_UPLOADS. Upload everything, even if your reading disagrees with mine.**

My thumb hovered over Send. The words looked less brave now that they included other people. Asking strangers to walk toward a floodwall because a teenage trespasser had found a discrepancy felt dangerously close to making them part of my mistake.

Then I remembered the city’s warning: community access to raw data would end upon decommissioning.

I sent it.

The message began traveling. A delivery icon appeared beside one name, then another. The alley seemed to narrow around me. Rain poured from the fire escape in a steady curtain, and the library alarm finally stopped behind my shoulder. In the sudden quiet, I heard an engine turn over at the far end of the street.

I leaned out from beneath the metal stairs. A white municipal van stood by the curb, its side marked with the city seal. Two figures sat in front. I could not see their faces through the rain-streaked windshield, but one of them lifted a phone and looked toward the library.

My stomach dropped.

Maybe they were the same adults who had searched the upper floors. Maybe they were only workers responding to the alarm. Either way, the bag on my shoulder held equipment I had used without permission, and the phone in my hand held messages that could make the city ask exactly what I had been doing on its roof.

I turned away from the van and ran.

The route home passed beneath the elevated train, through a narrow passage that smelled of wet concrete and old cooking oil. My shoes slapped through puddles. The bag struck my hip with every step, and I kept one arm clamped over it. I expected a shout behind me, a hand catching my collar, or the van’s tires rolling slowly after me. Nothing came. That frightened me more than pursuit would have.

At the corner near our apartment, I stopped beneath a broken awning and checked the message app again. Three replies had arrived.

**I’m at the wall.**

**I can be there soon.**

**The mark is higher than the dashboard says. Sending photo.**

The third message included an image. It was blurred by rain, but the painted line on the concrete was unmistakable. Beside it, someone had placed a ruler and a phone displaying the time. The water had climbed past the level shown on the city’s public chart.

I saved the image in three places: the phone, the memory card, and the draft of an email addressed to the local radio station. Then I sent the verification instructions to every contact who had replied, asking each person to forward them without adding my name.

For the first time that night, the evidence had begun moving without me.

I crossed the final street toward home. The apartment building’s front light flickered above the entrance, and behind the glass I could see the dark outline of the notice board where rent reminders and heating complaints gathered. I slowed before going inside. My mother would ask why I was soaked, why the library had been on the news, and why my backpack sounded like loose metal when I set it down. The city might ask harder questions.

I held the strap tighter and climbed the steps. The sensor could be removed, but the measurements had already escaped into other hands. Tomorrow, Bellwether would have to decide whether a girl’s unauthorized rooftop experiment was a violation—or the first warning it had chosen to hear.


I pushed through the apartment building’s glass door and caught it before it slammed. The lobby smelled of wet coats and boiled cabbage. Water dripped from my sleeves onto the cracked tile, making a dark trail toward the mailboxes. I kept the backpack hugged against my chest. The rain gauge pressed into my ribs; the phone was in my pocket, the memory card sealed inside its plastic case, and the printed evidence lay between two notebooks so the ink would not smear. I had made it home with everything, but the thought did not feel like safety. It felt like carrying four separate alarms.

The notice board beside the mailboxes held a new sheet of paper. I leaned close. **CITY PROPERTY COMPLIANCE—UNAUTHORIZED ACCESS TO MUNICIPAL ROOF EQUIPMENT MAY RESULT IN COST ASSESSMENT AND REFERRAL.** Beneath the warning was a list of violations: tampering, unsafe entry, unapproved electrical use. No name appeared, but the library’s address did. My stomach clenched. They had not identified me, or they had chosen not to yet. Either way, the city had begun converting my actions into a file.

My phone buzzed before I could read further. An automated message from the library security system filled the screen.

**Incident recorded. Please contact facilities administration regarding roof access and equipment use.**

I almost laughed at the neatness of it. The sensor could be removed without permission from anyone who lived near the floodwall, but I could be questioned for plugging a phone into an abandoned computer. I opened the message app instead. The person at the wall had sent another photograph. This one showed the painted water mark, the ruler, and a second phone held at the edge of the frame. Different hand. Different angle. Same discrepancy.

I saved that photograph beside the first. Then I opened the email draft to the radio station and added a sentence: **Independent photographs are now arriving from more than one observer. Raw files and timestamps available on request.** I attached both images, the text file, and a scan of the printed instructions. Before sending, I copied the entire folder to the memory card. The transfer bar crawled across the screen while rain rattled the glass doors behind me.

A vehicle slowed outside. Through the wet panes, I saw the white municipal van pass the entrance without stopping. Its tires hissed over the street. I lowered the phone and waited until the van disappeared beyond the corner. It might have been searching for the gauge. It might have been heading toward the southern floodwall. I could not afford to build a plan around guessing, so I opened the neighborhood message thread and typed a new instruction.

**If a city crew arrives, do not interfere. Photograph the work order, vehicle, and sensor before removal. Keep a safe distance. Send copies to everyone.**

I added a second message for the person already at the wall: **Can you confirm whether the crew is there now?** Then I sent both. Asking people to document the removal felt different from asking them to stop it. A picture could be evidence without putting anyone in the path of a truck or a uniformed worker. My local objective had changed. I was no longer trying only to preserve my readings. I was building a record of what happened when the city erased its own comparison.

The stairwell door opened above me. I flinched and nearly dropped the phone. A man’s shoes appeared on the landing, followed by a grocery bag and a dripping umbrella. I moved aside, keeping the backpack closed with one hand.

“Everything all right?” he asked.

“Just caught in the rain,” I said.

He glanced at the notice board, then at my soaked clothes. “You should get upstairs before you catch something.”

I nodded. The ordinary kindness made my throat tighten. I climbed, listening to the building settle around me. On the second-floor landing, my phone chimed. The wall observer had replied: **No crew yet. Another resident is coming with a camera. Mark still visible.**

I stopped beneath the buzzing light and read the message twice. No crew yet meant the evidence source remained, but only for now. I sent the observer a request to photograph the sensor housing and its identifying plate without crossing any barrier. Then I forwarded the same instructions to the second person. The plan had three parts now: preserve my original equipment, collect independent measurements, and document the city’s removal if it came. None of it required me to return to the library roof tonight.

At our apartment door, I set the backpack on the floor before unlocking it. I removed the rain gauge first and wrapped it in the clean towel hanging beside the kitchen sink. The phone went on the table, still connected to its charger. I placed the memory card inside a small envelope and slid it beneath the printed evidence in my mathematics notebook. When I touched the papers, one crooked page caught on my sleeve. Across its top, in my own thick handwriting, were the words: **Measure it yourself.**

A knock sounded from the hallway.

I froze with the envelope between my fingers. The automated library message remained open on my phone, and the city’s work order waited somewhere beyond the rain. Whoever stood outside could be a neighbor, a building manager, or someone asking why a teenage girl had been on a municipal roof. I slipped the envelope into the notebook, pushed the notebook beneath the couch cushion, and kept the gauge wrapped against my chest.

Then I opened the door.


The person in the hallway was a woman from the third floor, someone I had seen carrying laundry past the mailboxes but never spoken to. Rain darkened the shoulders of her coat. In one hand she held a camera inside a clear plastic bag. In the other, she carried a folded sheet of paper.

“Are you the girl who sent the floodwall instructions?” she asked.

My fingers tightened around the door.

“I sent instructions,” I said carefully.

“That’s what I mean.” She looked past me toward the apartment, then lowered her voice. “I’m the other resident at the wall. The one with the camera.”

For a second, relief made me careless. I almost opened the door wider. Then I remembered the rain gauge against my chest, the phone on the table, and the notebook hidden beneath the couch cushion. I kept my body in the doorway.

“Did you get the photographs?”

“I got photographs of the mark and the sensor.” She raised the camera bag. “And I photographed the van when it arrived.”

My heart began beating faster. “The crew is there?”

“They were.” She unfolded the paper. “This was taped to the gate.”

I looked at the sheet without touching it. The city seal filled the top corner, followed by a work-order number and a description of equipment removal. Near the bottom, someone had written **temporary safety closure** in thick black letters. There was no mention of a faulty reading, a damaged sensor, or an emergency. The reason given was simply **scheduled infrastructure revision**.

“Did they remove it?” I asked.

“Not yet. They put barriers around the housing and left one worker behind. The other two went to get a truck with lifting equipment.” Her eyes moved to the gauge in my arms. “That’s yours?”

I nodded.

“Then you need to make copies before anyone can say the comparison never happened.”

The sentence struck me harder than the rain. I stepped back just far enough to reach the table without turning away from her. My phone’s screen had gone dark, but I woke it with my thumb. The radio-station email draft was still open. The two independent photographs waited in its attachments, along with the text file and scanned instructions. I added a third attachment: a photograph of the work order, taken over my shoulder while the woman held it flat.

“Can I include your images?” I asked.

“Yes. Include the original files, not just screenshots.” She glanced toward the notebook-shaped lump beneath the couch cushion, though she could not see it from the hall. “And include the time your camera recorded them.”

“I will.”

“Don’t use my name.”

“I won’t.”

She gave a short nod, as if that settled the most important part. “The worker asked whether anyone had been taking measurements. I told him people were documenting public water levels from a safe distance. He said the city had its own records.”

I felt the old anger rise, hot and familiar. “Their records are the reason we’re doing this.”

“Then send it before they finish.”

I looked down at the rain gauge. Its clear cylinder was clouded with droplets, and the small scale inside had been scratched during the trip home. It was not official equipment. It was a classroom instrument, cheap enough to replace and important enough to make me afraid. I carried it to the kitchen while keeping the door open with my heel. The woman remained in the hallway, visible in the reflection of the dark window.

I placed the gauge on a folded towel and photographed it from several angles: the scale, the scratches, the label I had written, and the water residue at the bottom. Then I photographed the notebook containing the memory-card envelope without removing the card. The printed evidence stayed inside the notebook, but I took pictures of each page and added them to the draft. Protecting the originals no longer meant keeping them untouched in one hiding place. It meant making enough trustworthy copies that losing one would not destroy the record.

“Your hands are shaking,” the woman said.

“I know.”

“You’re still a kid.”

The words should have embarrassed me. Instead, they made the apartment seem suddenly smaller—the couch, the damp shoes by the door, the city notice waiting in my messages. I had thought growing up meant becoming old enough that adults would listen automatically. Maybe it meant learning what to do when they did not.

“I’m a kid who has the readings,” I said. “That has to count for something.”

“It counts if somebody else can check them.”

I attached the gauge photographs and typed a final paragraph: **The city has posted a removal order at the southern floodwall. Independent observers photographed the discrepancy before removal. Please preserve the files and contact us through the neighborhood thread if you need verification.** I did not write my name. I did not claim certainty. I pressed Send.

The message vanished from the draft and appeared in the sent folder. A small confirmation appeared beneath it. For a moment, neither of us spoke. Outside, water ticked against the glass. Somewhere beyond the apartment walls, the municipal crew was preparing to lift away the instrument that had contradicted the dashboard.

The woman folded the work order again. “I’m going back to the wall.”

“Don’t go near the barriers.”

“I won’t. I’ll keep photographing.”

“Send everything to the group, not only to me. If my phone disappears, the record shouldn’t.”

She studied me, then nodded. “You’re learning.”

I almost told her I was learning because nobody had given me another choice. Instead, I reached for the printed evidence beneath the cushion, checked that the notebook was still dry, and slid it deeper into the fabric. The rain gauge remained on the towel. The phone stayed charging. The memory card remained sealed inside the notebook, and the photographed pages now existed in the radio station’s inbox.

When the woman descended the stairs, I locked the door and checked it twice. Then I opened the neighborhood thread and sent one more instruction: **Document the work order, the sensor housing, and the vehicle from a safe distance. No confrontation. Preserve original files.**

A reply appeared almost immediately.

**Understood. We have witnesses.**

I read the words until my breathing slowed. The city could remove its sensor. It could question me about the roof. It could call my gauge unauthorized and my conclusions premature. But the comparison was no longer mine alone, and neither was the next move.


The reply in the neighborhood thread had barely settled on my screen when another message arrived.

**Truck is here. Crew is opening the barrier.**

A photograph followed. The image was blurred by rain, but I could make out the white municipal vehicle, the orange cones, and the sensor housing behind a temporary fence. A second photograph showed the work order held at arm’s length. The identifying number was visible.

I saved both files immediately, then held the phone above the table while the transfer completed. The memory card remained sealed in the notebook beneath the couch cushion. The original printed evidence stayed there too. I had promised myself I would not carry every irreplaceable thing into the same place, and this was the first time that promise felt less like caution than strategy.

I made a new folder on the phone and labeled it **REMOVAL—COPIES**. Inside it, I placed the photographs from the wall, the gauge images, the work order, and the text file containing my readings. Then I opened the neighborhood thread and wrote: **Send original files to at least two people. Do not crop or edit. Include the full work order and the sensor plate. Keep your distance.** Before I could press send, a sound rose from the stairwell: quick footsteps, then the scrape of a shoe against the landing.

I slipped the phone into my pocket and opened the apartment door. The hallway smelled of wet wool and dust. The third-floor woman stood near the stairwell, one hand on the railing and the other wrapped around her camera bag. She had not gone far.

“The truck is there,” she said.

“I know. I just got the photographs.”

“Then you should stay here.”

“I am staying here.” I stepped into the hall and locked the apartment behind me. “But I need to give you a copy.”

Her expression tightened. “A copy of what?”

“The key records I sent. The work order, the gauge, the readings, and the photographs from the others.” I kept my voice low, though the building was quiet. “If the city takes my phone or asks for the originals, you need a complete set.”

I returned to the apartment long enough to take the duplicate printed pages I had photographed earlier. They were not the originals; I had printed them at the library before the roof incident, and their edges were slightly crooked from the old machine. Still, they showed the readings and the instructions clearly. I slid them into a clean envelope with a handwritten inventory: **Printed reading pages. Municipal work-order copy. Observer instructions.** At the bottom I wrote the time in the same relative language I had used in my notes—before removal, after arrival, during rain—because I had not trusted the building clocks or my own hurried guesses.

When I stepped back into the hallway, the woman examined the envelope without opening it.

“Where should I keep this?”

“Not with the camera. Somewhere separate.”

She tucked it inside the lining of her coat. “I can send scans from my room before I go.”

“Send them to the group and the radio station. Don’t send only to me.”

“You’re sure you want the radio station to have all this?”

“No,” I said. “I’m sure I want the record to survive me.”

The sentence hung between us. I heard how large it sounded after I had spoken it, too large for a hallway with peeling paint and a buzzing ceiling light. I was still a teenager in a borrowed apartment, still worried about a library security message and the possibility of a bill I could not pay. But the evidence had outgrown the size of the room. It belonged to anyone who could check it.

Her camera made a small electronic chirp. She looked down. “Another image just came through.”

“Read it.”

“Sensor plate photographed. The number matches the work order.” She turned the screen toward me. “They’re lifting the cover.”

My stomach pulled tight. The removal had become an action instead of a threat. I imagined the metal housing opened, wires exposed, the city’s official device reduced to a thing that could be boxed and carried away. I wanted to run toward the floodwall, to stand where the mark was visible and demand an explanation. Instead, I pointed at her phone.

“Save that original. Forward it before you leave.”

“I will.”

“And ask the others to photograph the empty mounting bolts after they remove it.”

She nodded, then descended the stairs. I followed as far as the second-floor landing, where the narrow window looked down over the entrance. Through the rippled glass, I saw rain striking the pavement and the woman emerge beneath her hood. She paused under the awning, checked her phone, and raised the camera toward the street—not toward me, not toward the apartment, but toward the ordinary public space where records could be made.

I returned upstairs and checked each protected item. The gauge remained on its towel. The phone was in my hand. The memory card and original papers were dry beneath the couch cushion. The duplicate envelope was moving toward another witness. I opened the thread and watched new files arrive one after another: the truck, the barrier, the plate, the workers’ gloved hands.

I opened a follow-up message to the radio station and typed one final line: **The sensor is being removed now. Independent records exist before, during, and after removal.** Then I sent it and placed the phone beside the gauge to listen to the rain.

The city could erase the instrument. It could not erase the moment when several people had seen what it measured.


The rain kept ticking against the window after I sent the follow-up. For several breaths I stood beside the table, listening to it and watching the phone screen remain silent. The sensor was gone from my hands but not yet gone from the record. That difference mattered. If the files stayed on my phone, they could still disappear with the phone.

I opened the neighborhood thread again. New photographs had arrived: the workers carrying a gray equipment case, the square hole in the floodwall where the housing had been, and three dark bolts shining with rain. Someone had photographed the empty mounting points from behind the barrier. Another person had typed, **No confrontation. Crew left with the case. Plate number visible in the last image.**

I downloaded every original file, checking that the transfer finished before opening the next one. Then I wrote to the third-floor woman: **I’m taking the copied files to the library computer room. I’ll make a public archive and send the link to the group. Keep your envelope separate and scan it when you can.** Her answer appeared quickly.

**Good. I’ll scan here. The envelope stays with me. Be careful crossing.**

I read the message twice. She was not asking me to surrender anything, and I was not asking her to become my shield. We were making separate copies that could be compared later. I put the phone in a sealed plastic bag, left the original notebook beneath the couch cushion, and took one last photograph of the notebook’s hiding place—not because the picture proved what was inside, but because it recorded where I had left it and when I had begun the transfer. I checked the gauge on its towel, then covered it with an upside-down bowl to keep more damp from reaching the scratched cylinder.

The apartment door clicked shut behind me. In the hallway, water had gathered beneath my shoes, making small dark islands on the worn floor. I locked my door and tested the handle once. Then I descended the stairs, passing the mailboxes and the lobby’s cloudy glass. Through it I could see the street where the rain had turned the pavement silver. A municipal truck moved at the far intersection, its rear lights fading behind the curtain of water. I could not tell whether it was the removal crew, but I did not need to know. The evidence of the empty bolts was already on my phone.

I pushed through the entrance and crossed beneath the narrow awning. The walk to the library was only a few blocks, but the wind drove rain against my face and made the phone thump against my ribs. I kept one hand over my coat pocket. At the corner, my phone vibrated. I stopped under a shop canopy and opened the message.

**We received your files,** the radio station wrote. **Please send an accessible folder rather than attachments alone. We can preserve the originals and have a producer review the work order. Do not come to the station. Use the public link when ready.**

I felt relief first, then suspicion. The message did not prove what they would broadcast, and it did not promise they would accept my conclusions. But they had asked for the records, not a polished argument. I forwarded the note to the neighborhood thread and continued toward the library.

The old library’s front steps were slick with rain. I wiped my shoes on the mat, showed my library card at the desk, and went to the public computer room. No one stopped me. The ordinary quiet of the room felt almost absurd after the floodwall: humming machines, damp coats, the soft clack of keyboards. I chose a computer near the wall and connected the phone with the charging cable from my bag. The screen asked whether I wanted to trust the device. My thumb hovered, then pressed yes.

I created a folder named **SOUTHERN WALL—RAW RECORD**, leaving every filename unchanged. Into it went the sensor-plate photographs, the work order, the readings, the gauge images, and the empty-bolt pictures. I added a plain text file explaining the sequence without claiming more than I knew: the independent measurement had disagreed with the public dashboard; the sensor had been removed under a scheduled revision order; observers had documented the site before and after removal. I included instructions for checking the image details and warned that the duplicate pages were not the protected originals.

When the upload bar reached the end, I opened the link in a second browser window to make sure strangers could access it. The photographs appeared one by one. The work-order number was readable. The empty mounting bolts showed exactly where the sensor had been. I copied the link into the neighborhood thread and the radio station’s reply address. Then I printed the access instructions, folded them, and placed them beside the duplicate pages.

Outside, rain blurred the library windows until the street looked like a moving watercolor. I watched the upload page remain open behind me, impossible to mistake for a private message now. The record had a public door. It could be checked by people who did not know me, did not trust me, and did not have to accept my word.

My phone vibrated once more.

**Archive received,** the third-floor woman wrote. **Scanning my envelope now.**

I looked at the empty chair beside me and imagined no one’s thoughts, only the work waiting on the screen. I opened the folder again and began labeling the photographs by what they showed—not by what I wanted them to prove. Outside, the city kept raining on the missing sensor. Inside, I made the evidence easier to find.


The first label I wrote was wrong.

**Sensor removed while water rising.**

I stared at the words beneath the photograph of the empty bolts. Rain striped the library window, and the computer’s fan rattled against something loose inside its case. The photograph showed an absence. It did not show rising water. I had readings elsewhere, taken at other moments, with their own limitations. Putting the sentence there made the picture seem to prove something it could not.

I deleted the label and wrote: **Mounting points after removal. Submitted by neighborhood observer.**

It felt smaller. It was better.

My phone lit beside the keyboard. The third-floor woman had sent a link, followed by a message.

**Scanned in my room. Envelope still here. Sent this same link to the group and the station. Check against yours, but don’t replace either set.**

I opened her scans in a separate window. The first page appeared crooked on a dark background, one corner held down by the rounded handle of a spoon. I enlarged the work-order copy until the letters broke into squares. The identifying number matched the photograph in my folder. So did the line describing the scheduled revision. I checked each page against the inventory, touching the corresponding entry on my printed sheet.

On the last reading page, a mark that looked like a seven in my photograph looked like a one in hers.

For a moment I could not move my hand.

If I had copied a number wrong, every confident sentence I had written might carry that mistake forward. I pulled both images closer, then farther away. Neither became clear enough to settle it. My original notebook was still beneath the couch cushion, several wet blocks away.

I typed to her: **Last reading page, second column. I can’t distinguish one digit. Please keep your scan unchanged. I’m marking my transcription uncertain until I check the notebook.**

Her answer came while I was opening the archive notes.

**Agreed. Mine isn’t clear enough either.**

I added a correction at the top of the text file, naming the affected entry and withdrawing that number from the comparison for now. The raw image stayed where it was. My fingers shook as I saved the note. I wanted to explain that I was exhausted, that the photograph had been hurried, that I was doing work adults with offices and salaries should have done. None of those things made the digit legible.

A new message appeared in the neighborhood thread.

**Does this mean the whole thing is wrong?**

I pressed my palms against my knees before answering.

**It means one copied reading needs checking. The work-order number and removal photographs are separately visible. Please don’t use the uncertain reading in calculations.**

I read the answer twice, looking for excuses disguised as facts, then sent it.

The radio station replied through the same email chain I had already used. **Public folder opens. We have downloaded the source files. Your uncertainty note is visible. Producer review is still pending.**

I let out a breath slowly. They had preserved files. They had not confirmed my account or promised a broadcast. I copied that distinction into the neighborhood thread before anyone could turn acknowledgment into endorsement.

The printer clicked awake when I sent an updated access sheet. Across the room, someone sighed at its noise. I collected the page while it was still warm and saw how little room the address occupied beneath the title. All that running, hiding, and checking had produced a few lines strangers might walk past.

But strangers could not walk past a link they never saw.

I disconnected my phone, checked that the public folder still opened without my account, and signed out. Then I gathered my papers and left the computer room, carrying the updated sheet through the narrow passage to the library’s entrance hall. My wet coat had stiffened at the cuffs. Beyond the glass doors, water spilled from the awning in a continuous silver fringe.

At the desk, I held up the sheet.

“Could this go on the community board?”

The librarian took it without reaching for a pin. “What is it?”

“Public records about the southern floodwall sensor. Photographs, a work-order copy, readings. There’s an explanation of what hasn’t been verified.”

“Is it an official flood warning?”

“No.” The answer came quickly enough to surprise me. “And I don’t want it to look like one.”

She tapped the heading. “Put that on the page. People come here for information they can act on.”

I borrowed a pen and wrote beneath the title: **Community evidence archive—not an official warning or a replacement for emergency instructions.** Then I added that the link allowed viewing, not changes.

She read it again and handed me two pins.

The community board stood between a missing-cat notice and a flyer for free tax help. I pinned the sheet at a height where someone sitting down could read it. One corner curled forward; I moved the second pin until it lay flat.

I had wanted someone important to tell Bellwether I was right. Instead, I stood in a library entrance making it possible for people to find where I might be wrong.

Behind me, the computer-room door opened. I stepped aside, leaving the address uncovered.


I stepped aside as a man carrying a stack of picture books came through the computer-room door. He glanced at the sheet, then shifted the books higher against his chest and kept walking. I watched the address disappear behind his shoulder and become visible again. There was nothing more I could do to make him read it.

There was something I could do about the uncertain digit.

I borrowed the librarian’s pen once more and wrote on the back of my inventory: **Check original. Photograph whole page, then detail. Keep earlier files.** Underneath, I added: **Do not calculate from disputed entry.** The last line was already in the archive, but I needed it where my own impatience could see it.

“Going back out?” the librarian asked.

“My notebook’s at home.”

She looked toward the water streaming down the glass. “Don’t take the low crossing.”

“I won’t.”

I gave back the pen and pulled my hood tight. The station had the public link and copies of the source files; its producer could review them without me standing in a waiting room. I had no reason to go there, and they had specifically told me not to. I checked that the phone was sealed inside its plastic bag, pushed through the library doors, and descended the steps with one hand on the rail.

Outside, the rain struck harder than it had sounded indoors. I took the longer route toward the apartment building, staying on the higher pavement instead of cutting through the dip beside the loading bays. At the intersection, water hid the curb. I waited until a passing car’s wake had spread and flattened before crossing at the marked crossing, feeling ahead with each foot.

Halfway home, I caught myself rehearsing what I would write if the digit was a seven. A clean correction, the comparison restored, everyone relieved.

I stopped beneath an awning and made myself rehearse the other answer.

If it was a one, I would write one.

The sentence sounded childish. So did a promise not to lie, until telling the truth cost something. I was still a teenager with rain leaking into one shoe, but people were passing around words I had written. Wanting to be believed no longer seemed like a good enough reason to speak.

At the apartment building, I pushed through the heavy entrance door and wiped my soles on the frayed lobby mat. The mailboxes smelled of wet paper. I climbed the stairs, listening to my shoes squeak on every landing, then unlocked my apartment and shut the door behind me.

The bowl still covered the gauge on its towel. I left it alone. At the couch, I lifted the cushion and found the notebook where I had tucked it, its soft cover bent against the frame. Only then did I notice how tightly I had been holding my jaw.

I cleared a dry space on the table, washed my hands, and dried them carefully before opening the notebook. The page resisted at the binding. I held it with a clean spoon laid across the blank margin, copying the simple trick I had seen in the third-floor woman’s scan.

There was the entry.

A one.

I tilted the notebook toward the window. The faint diagonal beside it belonged to a mark showing through from the other side. In the photograph, shadow and pencil had joined into the shape I had read as seven.

“Oh,” I said.

The room offered no answer except rain against the sill.

I photographed the entire page first, including its edges and the neighboring entries. Then I took a close view and checked both images before moving anything. The one remained a one. I wrote a correction on a separate sheet rather than altering the notebook: the copied transcription was wrong; the original showed one; any comparison using seven needed to be withdrawn.

For several seconds my thumb rested above the neighborhood thread. I wanted to post the work-order photograph again first, to cushion the correction with something that had survived checking.

Instead, I sent the correction and both new photographs.

Then I wrote to the third-floor woman: **Original reads one. Images posted. Please keep your envelope and scans unchanged so the copies remain independently comparable.**

Her reply arrived while I was peeling off my wet coat.

**Kept as they are. I’ll attach your correction as a separate note.**

I sat at the table and uploaded the photographs to the archive, leaving the earlier images in place. At the top of the notes I replaced “uncertain” with the result and explained why the transcription had changed. I checked the public view, then sent the radio station the updated link with a short correction notice. Their review was still pending. I did not describe the update as anything they had approved.

A reply appeared in the neighborhood thread.

**So what can we still check?**

I pulled the inventory closer. Beside the reading comparison, I wrote **recalculate**. Beside the work order and removal photographs, I left the existing descriptions unchanged.

Then I began typing an answer, with the original notebook open beneath my hand.


**You can check the work-order number against both copies,** I typed. **You can check what the removal photographs actually show. You can check the notebook entries against the photographs, including my mistake. The reading comparison is withdrawn until I redo it. None of these records, alone, tells us whether a street is safe right now.**

I read it aloud. The apartment made my voice sound thin. Then I pressed send before I could add a sentence asking people not to give up on me.

A response appeared almost immediately.

**Then why keep the bad photograph?**

I looked at the earlier image on the screen, at the shadow that had helped turn one into seven. I hated that picture now. Deleting it would have felt like washing a stain from my hands.

**So someone can follow the correction,** I wrote. **If I replace it without keeping the old version, you have to trust my account of what changed.**

I set the phone beside the notebook and drew three columns on a fresh sheet: original entry, earlier transcription, corrected transcription. Copying slowly, I checked each line against the page rather than against my memory. The refrigerator shuddered into silence. Without it, I could hear a television through the wall and the small, uneven ticks of water striking the window frame.

The arithmetic took less time than my fear of it. I did it twice, covering the first working with another sheet. Both attempts agreed. The calculation that had depended on seven did not survive the correction. I wrote that plainly beneath the working, then stopped before turning the new result into another sweeping claim. The records still had gaps. Not every observation had been made at the same moment or under the same conditions.

My phone vibrated.

**I’m upstairs in my room,** the third-floor woman wrote. **Envelope put away. I can check your arithmetic from the posted images, but I won’t change my scans.**

**Please do,** I answered. **Keep your working separate too. I’ll bring a copy of mine once you’ve finished yours.**

I waited with my hands around a mug of water. I was tired enough to want agreement more than accuracy, and knowing that about myself did not make it disappear. When her next message came—**Finished. Bring yours**—I photographed my working, saved it without changing the archive, and put the notebook in the dry cupboard above the table. The gauge stayed beneath its bowl.

Then I took my calculation sheet, locked the apartment, and climbed the stairs.

The third-floor landing smelled of boiled cabbage and damp wool. She opened her door with a sheet in one hand. Behind her, a lamp lit a narrow table; her envelope was nowhere in sight.

“Yours first?” I asked.

“Side by side.”

We laid the sheets on the broad windowsill without exchanging them. Her figures were darker than mine, the subtraction set out differently. I followed each step with my finger held above the paper.

“The same,” I said.

“The arithmetic is,” she replied. “Not the claim you made before.”

Heat climbed into my face. “I know.”

She tapped the empty space below my conclusion. “Write that here, then. People will copy the bottom line.”

I borrowed her pen and added: **This corrected calculation does not support the earlier comparison. It does not establish current flood conditions.**

For a moment neither of us spoke. Downstairs, a door banged and someone called for a towel.

“I thought if I was careful enough,” I said, “I wouldn’t have to keep doing this.”

“Checking?”

“Being wrong in public.”

She capped her pen. “You found it. You told us. Now we can use the parts that hold.”

I looked again at our separate sheets. She had not signed mine or asked me to sign hers. Each showed its own route through the figures. I photographed them side by side with her permission, then photographed mine alone.

Back downstairs, I unlocked my apartment and returned to the table. The radio station had replied remotely in the existing email chain: the correction had reached their inbox; producer review was still pending. No invitation, no promise of airtime. I left that message in the correspondence folder and sent no announcement about it.

Instead, I uploaded my working and added a note that the woman had checked it independently. Beneath it I placed the conclusion we had checked, followed by the limits we had not removed. Then I returned to the neighborhood thread.

**The corrected calculation is available now. It does not support my earlier comparison. The work-order copy and removal photographs remain available for separate examination. Please link the correction wherever the old claim was shared.**

I pressed send.

A little later, someone replied with a link to another thread.

**Added it here. Removed the old number from my post.**

I opened the link and checked. My name was still there, attached to the mistake and its correction. So were the records, reachable without asking me.

I set the phone down and pulled my schoolwork out from beneath the inventory. There was barely room for it on the table. I moved the mug, squared the papers into two piles, and made room.
