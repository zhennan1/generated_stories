## Chapter 1: The Vanishing

The observation deck had been built for looking homeward.

Its forward windows curved around the ship’s prow in a broad, armored crescent, and beyond them the stars burned with the old, indifferent clarity of vacuum. Between two familiar constellations there should have been Earth: a blue-white point, too small for the eye to distinguish, yet large enough in every mind to contain oceans, cities, arguments, kitchens, graves.

There was only blackness.

Not the blackness between stars. This was deeper, a region that seemed to have rejected even distance. It had no rim, no cloud of dust, no fading corona. Light entered it and did not return. The stars behind it were gone as cleanly as words erased from a page.

Dr. Elara Voss stood with one hand against the glass. The pane was cold through her glove.

“Say it again,” Marcus Hale said.

He was strapped into the pilot’s crash couch, though the Meridian had stopped tumbling hours ago. His face looked carved from ash. A strip of dried blood darkened his temple where the emergency harness had caught him during the first alarm.

Anya Chen did not look up from the sensor console. “Earth is absent.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Anya’s fingers hovered over the keys. “There is no planetary mass at the expected coordinates. No gravitational signature. No reflected light. No debris field. No neutrino event, no radiation bloom, no displacement wave we can measure.”

Marcus swallowed. “Then what happened to it?”

She stared at the instruments as if they had insulted her. “I don’t know.”

The Meridian’s observation deck was littered with the evidence of a crew interrupted: a cracked tablet drifting beneath the ceiling, a silver emergency blanket caught in a ventilation grille, three mugs pinned beneath a fallen equipment case. Somewhere behind the bulkhead, a pump clicked at uneven intervals. The ship had lost two attitude thrusters, half its long-range array, and enough hull plating to make every pressure warning sound like a prophecy.

Elara turned from the window. “How long since the last confirmed transmission?”

“Seven hours, nineteen minutes,” Anya said.

“From Earth?”

“From everyone.”

Marcus released himself from the couch. He moved carefully, conserving motion as though gravity were a ration. “We need an inventory.”

Elara looked at him. “Now?”

“Especially now.”

“Marcus—”

“If the planet is gone, we need to know what remains.” His voice did not rise. That made it worse. “Water. Food. Oxygen. Propellant. Medical supplies. Battery capacity. Facts are still facts, Doctor.”

Anya’s mouth tightened. “You think a spreadsheet will bring it back?”

“No. I think it will tell us how long we have before we join it.”

He pushed away toward the hatch. At the threshold he paused, one hand gripping the frame. “No one goes into the transmitter bay alone. The outer corridor is still venting around the sensor blister.”

Then he was gone, carrying his clipboard like a shield.

Elara watched the hatch close. In the silence, she heard the shape of an old sentence forming inside her: We are the last.

She rejected it. A sentence could be grammatically sound and still be a lie.

At her workstation, she opened the linguistic archive. The files occupied nearly a terabyte of protected memory: recordings, dictionaries, field notes, children’s stories, ritual chants, arguments captured in kitchens and markets. Languages gathered from Earth before the expedition had left—some spoken by billions, some by fewer than a hundred people, some already preserved only in recordings.

She created a new directory.

FINAL ARCHIVE.

Her hand paused over the confirmation key. Final was a conclusion. Final implied an audience capable of arriving afterward.

She renamed it CONTINUANCE.

The first file she opened contained a greeting in a language from the northern coast of an ocean that no longer existed as a political boundary. A woman’s voice said, “I see you.”

Elara played it twice.

“I see you,” she whispered to the empty deck.

Behind her, the console chimed. Anya had begun another scan. Her dark hair had come loose from its tie, and one sleeve was stained with coolant. She had not left the sensor station since the disappearance. On the display, layers of false color crawled across a map of space: gravity readings, radiation, magnetic fields, all insistently ordinary except for the vacant wound where home should have been.

“You should sleep,” Elara said.

Anya gave a short laugh. “That’s your advice?”

“It’s not advice. It’s a biological requirement.”

“So is grief.”

Elara had no answer ready. She sat beside her, buckling into the auxiliary seat. “What are you looking for?”

“An edge.”

“Of what?”

“Whatever took it.”

The screen refreshed. Nothing.

“My brother was in Nairobi,” Anya said. “He was supposed to call me when we reached the relay. He complained that the city’s rain smelled like metal.” Her eyes remained on the data. “He always complained. I thought I had time to tell him I missed it.”

Elara said, “I’m sorry.”

Anya finally looked at her. “That phrase is doing a lot of work for you.”

“It’s a phrase that has survived because people need it.”

“Does it help?”

“Not always.”

“Then what good is it?”

Elara glanced toward the black wound. “Sometimes language isn’t a tool for fixing things. Sometimes it’s how we refuse to let a thing vanish without a witness.”

Anya’s expression broke—not into tears, but into something more exhausted. She turned back to the console.

Marcus returned forty minutes later with the inventory. Water: twenty-three days at strict rationing. Oxygen: nineteen, perhaps twenty if the scrubbers held. Food: thirty-one. Propellant: insufficient for a return trajectory even if there were a return to make. The Meridian’s reactor had entered emergency cycling and would not support full systems for more than six days.

He read the figures aloud. Each number landed with the dull finality of a stone.

“We can extend oxygen by shutting down the botanical module,” Anya said.

“The plants produce less than they consume now,” Marcus replied.

“And the archive?” Elara asked.

“Protected memory draws negligible power.”

“Good.”

Marcus looked at her. “You’re preserving dictionaries.”

“I’m preserving people.”

“They’re gone.”

The words hung between them. Marcus closed his eyes, briefly, as though he had stepped too near a flame.

“Perhaps,” Elara said. “But that isn’t the same as saying they never existed.”

No one spoke for a while. The pump clicked. A loose bolt drifted past the window, turning slowly in the starlight.

Then Anya sat upright.

“Wait.”

Marcus moved to her station. “What?”

“Don’t touch anything.”

“I wasn’t—”

“Quiet.”

She isolated the sensor feeds one by one. The background hiss fell away: cosmic radiation, ship vibration, the electronic murmur of their own failing systems. A thin line remained on the spectrum, barely visible beneath the noise.

Elara leaned closer. “A transmission?”

“Maybe.”

Anya magnified it. The line rose, vanished, and rose again.

Once. Twice. Three times.

The intervals were exact.

Marcus frowned. “Instrument echo.”

“It’s coming from outside the ship.”

“Reflection.”

“From where?”

Anya expanded the bearing solution. A coordinate appeared, pointing beyond the charted lanes, toward a region cataloged only as empty space.

The signal pulsed again.

Short. Short. Long. Short.

Not random. Not natural—not anything they knew.

Anya’s hand trembled above the controls. “There’s no star there.”

Elara listened to the rhythm. It entered the deck, the ship, the hollow place behind her ribs.

“What is it saying?” Marcus asked.

Elara did not pretend to know.

“It may not be saying anything,” she replied. “But something is repeating.”

The pulse came again from the dead zone.

This time, all three of them heard it.

## Chapter 2: The Signal

The pulse returned at 03:17 ship time.

Anya had been watching the sensor console so long that the display had become a second darkness, its blue figures swimming behind her eyes. When the first beat arrived, she thought it was another failure of vision. Then the second struck. Then the long one, stretched like a held breath. The final pulse came sharp and small.

Short. Short. Long. Short.

She froze with one hand above the controls.

Beyond the observation glass, the region where Earth had been remained an absence without edges. No dust caught the starlight. No reflected sun moved across the black.

On the far side of the deck, Marcus Hale was braced beneath an open maintenance panel, a cable clenched between his teeth. He pulled it free and looked over.

“What failed?”

“Nothing.”

“That’s not an answer.”

Anya enlarged the waveform. The pulse appeared again, a sequence of four narrow towers rising from the noise floor. The period between sequences was 11.8 seconds, constant to the limit of the ship’s instruments.

“It’s back,” she said.

Marcus approached, resting his hand against the console rather than trusting his legs.

“Same bearing?”

“Same everything.”

“A clock can be precise and still be broken.”

“Then let’s switch off every clock on the ship and see if this one keeps ticking.”

Elara Voss emerged from the archive alcove carrying a cup of water she had not touched. She had been listening to a woman describe how her grandmother called the children home at dusk. In the recording, a door squeaked. Elara had played that portion three times.

“What happened?”

“Her signal is back,” Marcus said.

“Our signal,” Anya said, then winced. “The signal.”

Elara set down the cup.

Anya called up the diagnostic layers. “I isolated the array’s internal noise. Now I want the auxiliary receiver running on its own battery. No connection to the ship’s data bus.”

Marcus studied her proposed configuration, then reached under the console and released the cable himself.

The screen went blank.

For several seconds there was only the auxiliary unit’s small amber light. Anya attached a portable display. A flat line appeared, trembled, rose into four unequal towers.

Elara let out a breath.

“External,” Anya said. “Unless both receivers have invented the same fault independently.”

“A reflection of something we’re transmitting,” Marcus suggested.

“Then shut the transmitters down.”

He did. One by one, their status lights went dark. Elara watched the emergency beacon indicator extinguish last. For the first time, the Meridian was making no attempt to tell anyone where it was.

The pulse returned.

Marcus restored the beacon without speaking.

Anya redirected the auxiliary dish, sweeping it away from the bearing. The pulse weakened, vanished, returned as she reversed the sweep.

“There,” she said. “Not in our wiring. Out there.”

The bearing crossed an uncharted dead zone beyond the mapped lane. No catalogued star occupied it; the telescope showed no planet, no scatter of debris. Anya laid the radio trace over the empty optical field.

“I can’t give you a distance. Or explain that carrier profile. But something is arriving from that direction.”

“A potential transmission,” Elara said.

“Potential,” Marcus repeated.

“Yes. That word belongs there.”

Her restraint cost her effort. Already she wanted to mark the pulses as units, to put spaces between them, to ask whether the silence counted too.

Marcus straightened. “A natural source can repeat.”

“Then we might discover a natural source.”

“With what fuel?”

The question brought the room back around them: loose insulation caught under a chair, the smell of heated plastic, blood drying at Marcus’s temple.

He opened his inventory. “Nineteen days of oxygen if nothing else fails. Six days of full reactor support. Water longer than that, which is generous of it.”

Anya looked away.

“We can reduce power,” he continued. “Keep the scrubbers running. Stay on a surveyed route. Leave the beacon on.”

“Wait for rescue?” Elara asked.

“Keep the possibility open.”

“And if nobody comes?”

“Then we make ourselves as comfortable as we can.”

He said it without cruelty. That was worse. Elara pictured nineteen days spent watching the oxygen estimate revise itself downward, recording words while the two people who could still hear her withdrew behind their stations.

“Show us a listening course,” she told Anya. “Not a pursuit. What would it cost to turn the high-gain array toward it?”

Anya brought up the ship model. The damaged antenna mount could no longer traverse its full arc. They would have to rotate the Meridian, then make a small correction burn to hold a safe attitude with the crippled thermal system.

The estimate took a minute.

“About eighteen hours of reactor margin,” Anya said. “Possibly more if the attitude jets leak again.”

“Eighteen hours we cannot get back,” Marcus said.

Elara nodded. “I understand.”

“Do you? This could be a pulsar behind an interference field. Something ordinary we’re too badly equipped to recognize.”

“It could.”

Anya pressed her palms against her knees. Her brother’s last message was still stored in her personal files. She had stopped opening it because she knew where his laugh came, and had begun flinching before it happened.

“If we listen closer,” she said, “it won’t tell us where Earth went.”

Neither of them answered quickly enough.

“Say it,” she demanded.

“We have no reason to think it will,” Elara said.

Anya swallowed. “All right.”

Marcus looked at the course display. “We set a power ceiling. No transmitting. No further burn without all three agreeing. If the array starts drawing beyond the estimate, we stop.”

Elara had prepared another argument. It deserted her.

“You’ll authorize it?”

“I’ll help you listen. That isn’t the same as believing.”

Anya entered the limits. Marcus checked them, then laid his thumb against the authorization plate.

The Meridian began to turn.

A loose fastener rolled across the deck and struck Elara’s shoe. She picked it up, suddenly unwilling to let anything else be lost, and put it in her pocket.

Beyond the glass, the empty bearing moved toward the center of their view. Nothing brightened there. No destination appeared.

The pulse came through the console speaker, thin and unlovely.

Elara sat beside Anya. Marcus remained standing behind them, one hand gripping the back of a chair.

They listened through the interval together.

## Chapter 3: First Grammar

Elara began by putting spaces where none existed.

On the observation deck’s auxiliary display, the four pulses stood above their trembling baseline. Short, short, long, short. Beneath them she drew a bracket, then another beneath the silence that followed.

“Why separate those?” Anya asked.

“Why join them?”

Anya rubbed her eyes. “Fair enough.”

They had slept in turns since the course alteration, although Elara remembered little of her allotted rest except the pressure of a harness and a dream in which somebody kept knocking. Now the receiver faced the empty bearing, and each sequence arrived 11.8 seconds after the last. She could predict it without watching the clock. Her body had begun to expect it.

She labeled the shortest pulse A, the long pulse B, and immediately disliked the apparent certainty of the letters.

“These aren’t words,” she said, mostly to herself. “Not yet.”

“Measurements,” Anya agreed.

“Candidates for measurements.”

From beneath a floor panel, Marcus said, “Let me know when you have a candidate for passing me the insulated wrench.”

Elara found it beside her chair. When she knelt to hand it down, warm air breathed against her face. Marcus lay shoulder-deep in the service trench, his temple cleaned but still swollen.

“Coolant valve?”

“Actuator. It keeps trying to open a valve that’s already open.”

“Perhaps it’s lonely.”

He took the wrench. “Then it can be lonely without burning out.”

For the first time since Earth disappeared, Anya made a sound that was almost a laugh.

They worked through the next watch. Anya built a model of the receiver’s noise, using off-bearing samples to distinguish the signal from interference. She left the original recordings untouched and displayed each filtered version alongside them.

“So we can see what we’ve persuaded it to become,” she said.

Elara glanced at her, but Anya was studying the screen.

There were features inside the pulses: small fluctuations, shoulders on the rising edges. Some survived every filter. Others disappeared when Anya changed a single parameter.

Elara tried grouping by duration, by spacing, by the shape of the edges. Four units could become two pairs; the long pulse could be two short units without a gap. The silence might divide utterances, or belong inside them. She marked each possibility on a separate layer until the display resembled a sentence repeatedly crossed out.

“What would make it grammar?” Anya asked.

“Differences that constrain other differences. Something that can occur here but not there.”

“And this?”

“One thing repeating. Not enough.”

They looked for familiar footholds anyway. Ratios yielded no recognizable constant. Counting subdivisions produced no reliable sequence of primes. Arranging samples into rows made stripes, then checkerboards, then nothing when the row length changed. No image held.

By the time Marcus climbed out of the trench, Elara had filled six pages with competing segmentations and translated nothing.

He placed three ration pouches on the console.

“Eat away from the electronics.”

They moved to the narrow table by the archive alcove. Marcus had warmed the pouches against a service pipe; the food was unevenly hot, a luxury that hurt Elara’s throat.

“My father called everything like this stew,” she said. “Even when it was rice.”

“Was he a bad cook?” Anya asked.

“An ambitious one. He thought naming something could finish the job.”

Anya turned her spoon over. “My brother used to read the instructions aloud in a terrible accent. As if the packet had arrived from another country.”

“Did it help?” Marcus asked.

“No.” Her mouth moved, briefly. “We ate it anyway.”

They finished without speaking, but nobody took a pouch back to a solitary station.

Afterward Elara asked permission to transmit.

Marcus’s face closed. “We agreed.”

“We agreed not to transmit while we established the listening limits. I’m asking to change that agreement. One acknowledgment. No repeated call.”

“An acknowledgment of what?”

“Reception. Nothing more.”

Anya brought up the power estimate before he could ask. A brief, unmodulated ping, directed along the receiving bearing, would cost less than another prolonged analysis run. The receiver would blank during transmission and recover before the next expected sequence.

“It won’t contain language,” Elara said. “We shouldn’t pretend we know how to say hello.”

Marcus studied the figures. “One. Then the transmitter goes back under lockout.”

All three entered their consent in the log. Anya preserved the raw feed on both sides of the transmission window.

The ping itself was inaudible. A green indicator lit, held for an instant, went dark.

They waited.

The next sequence arrived on schedule: short, short, long—

The last pulse did not come.

Elara gripped the edge of the console.

A pulse rose late, well inside what they had called silence. Then the familiar interval resumed.

“Recovery artifact,” Marcus said, but he leaned closer.

Anya checked the auxiliary receiver’s independent recording. The displaced pulse was there too. In the next cycle it occupied the same new position.

“Not a blanking fault,” she said. “At least, not one I can find.”

Elara heard her own breathing over the speaker hiss.

“It answered,” Marcus said quietly.

“Not an answer. A response.”

“You know that?”

“No.” She loosened her hands. “A change after our ping. We don’t know the distance. We don’t even know whether a reply could reach us that quickly. But if it’s connected, response is all we can call it.”

Marcus stayed through twelve more cycles. Before returning to the open floor panel, he checked that the recorder had enough storage.

Later, while Anya slept and the wrench sounded softly below the deck, Elara opened CONTINUANCE. A woman’s voice filled her headphones, speaking to someone outside the recording’s range. Elara did not open the translation. She listened to the rise at the end of a phrase, the pause that made room for someone else.

On her notes she had written: possible boundary shift following contact.

She crossed out contact and wrote transmission.

The correction should have comforted her. Instead she stared at the brackets she had drawn around the pulses, wondering how long a starving mind could mistake its own hands for something reaching back.

## Chapter 4: The Echo Chamber

By the second day, Anya had stopped calling it the signal.

“Listen to the tail,” she said. “Under the long pulse.”

Elara stood behind her chair. The observation deck smelled of hot insulation and the sour cloth of clothes worn too long. On the display, two spectral plots lay over one another: the incoming transmission and a fragment of sensor telemetry recorded when Earth vanished.

A ridge of frequencies roughly coincided.

“That’s in the receiver’s response band,” Elara said.

“It’s in both recordings.”

“Both recordings used this equipment.”

Anya moved the plots closer, as though distance on the screen were the obstacle. “There wasn’t a radiation bloom. We kept saying that. But perhaps we were looking for the wrong kind of remnant.”

“A remnant of what?”

“Everything.”

The word stayed between them. Elara looked toward the black observation window, where their own faint reflections concealed the stars.

“Show me with a different filter,” she said.

Anya did. The ridge broke into smaller peaks.

For a moment neither spoke.

“It’s damaged,” Anya said finally. “Of course it’s damaged.”

During the next watch, Elara found her whispering to the speaker.

“Not yet. I’m still here.”

“Anya?”

She lifted one hand for silence. Her lips moved through another sequence, almost keeping pace with the pulses.

“My brother always said my name twice when he wanted me to listen. Do you hear it?”

There was only a thin rasp, a hollow knock, the long wavering note.

“Play it without telling me what to hear.”

“You’ve already heard it now.”

“Yes,” Elara said. “That’s the problem.”

Anya turned down the volume, not off. Beside the controls lay an unopened ration pouch. The seal had collected a crescent of nail marks.

“We can hear speech in uncertain sound,” Elara said. “Once there’s a phrase in our heads, the sound begins to fit it.”

“You think I don’t know that?”

“I think you haven’t slept.”

“I was asleep when it happened.”

Elara had no answer that would not sound like permission to suffer. She fetched water instead. Anya accepted it without looking away.

By the following watch, certainty had spread beyond her chair.

Marcus came up to replace a scorched connector and stopped when the playback began. His fingers tightened around the part.

“What?” Elara asked.

“Nothing.”

But he waited for the sound to repeat.

Later, alone at her workstation, Elara heard her father clear his throat.

She turned so sharply that her knee struck the desk.

The speaker hissed. She knew the recording contained no throat, no lungs, no kitchen with an open window. Still, she replayed it. At the same point in the long pulse came the same small catch, the sound he made before correcting himself.

She listened six times before switching it off.

On Anya’s display, stacked waveforms began to resemble coastlines. Elara recognized a bay she had never visited, then saw how readily another alignment made it disappear. She said nothing about her father. The omission felt protective until she realized whom it protected.

The power warning sounded while she was trying to sleep.

She reached the observation deck to find its lights dimmed and Marcus braced over Anya’s console. Processing windows covered the display. Each contained a slightly different reconstruction of the same few seconds.

“End the jobs,” he said.

“They’re nearly converged.”

“You’ve pulled the reserve bus below its floor.”

“The main supply was limiting the run. I needed—”

“You bypassed the limit.”

A ventilation fan slowed overhead. Elara felt its vibration fade through the rail under her hand.

Marcus reached past Anya and pulled the terminal’s local isolator. The screens went black. From below came the sharper pitch of a pump taking over on the protected circuit.

Anya made a sound Elara had never heard from her. She hurled herself out of the chair and caught Marcus by the collar.

“Put it back.”

He seized her wrists. She drove him against the console; a loose cup struck the floor and bounced, loudly, twice. He pushed her away harder than he meant to. Her shoulder hit the chair frame.

“Stop!” Elara stepped between them.

Anya reached around her, fingers spread toward the dead display. “You switched them off.”

“The recordings are there,” Marcus said. His breath shook. “I switched off the processing.”

“You don’t know where they are.”

“Neither do you.”

The words brought a terrible stillness.

Marcus released the fist he had made. Anya sank against the chair, holding her shoulder. Elara wanted to touch her but could not find a place that did not look hurt.

“Can you move your arm?” she asked.

Anya moved it once and nodded.

They waited while Marcus checked the power panel. The protected circuit had held. Another few minutes at that draw, he said, and essential pumps would have begun dropping out. Restoring the reserve would cost them power they could not spare.

Elara picked up the cup. There was water inside it still, trembling against the rim.

“I heard my father,” she said.

Anya looked up.

“In the playback. I knew what it was, and I listened again anyway.”

“Then you understand.”

“I understand wanting to replay it.”

She set the cup on the table where they had eaten together. Only two days ago, her father had belonged to a story she could tell them. Now she had almost traded that memory for a noise she could summon with a control.

The signal had not offered him. She had supplied him herself.

Anya wanted a farewell she could interrupt. Elara wanted someone to speak from outside the ship. Even Marcus had paused beside the speaker, caught by something he would not name. The same sound accommodated all of them. Whatever its source, they were using it as a mirror, then mistaking recognition for discovery.

Marcus covered the isolator with his palm.

“We leave it behind,” he said. “Before it destroys us.”

## Chapter 5: A Desperate Code

Marcus kept his hand over the isolator after the argument ended. In the terminal's dark screen, Elara could see all three of them: Anya folded around her hurt shoulder, Marcus braced against the console, herself standing between them as though there were still a collision to prevent.

“Leave it behind,” he said again.

Elara looked toward the observation glass. Leaving required no maneuver. They could turn off the receiver and continue along the course they had already purchased with eighteen hours of their lives. The dark would look exactly the same.

“One more attempt,” she said.

Anya shut her eyes.

“Not another listening session. A systematic test. Every linguistic model we can run within the limit. Every mathematical framework we have the means to examine. We write down what would count as failure before we start.”

“You cannot exhaust mathematics,” Marcus said.

“No. But we can exhaust our methods. If nothing survives testing, we stop. We accept that there may be nothing here for us to understand.”

“And then?”

“Then we live on what remains.” Her mouth felt dry. “And die in silence, if that's what comes.”

The words frightened her more spoken aloud. She had meant them as an offer; they sounded like an instruction.

Anya opened her eyes. “No Earth telemetry.”

Elara waited.

“No searching for voices. No comparing it with the last recordings. If I ask to do that again, you remind me I said this.” Anya looked at the blank terminal rather than either of them. “I can't keep finding him and losing him.”

“All right,” Elara said.

Marcus lifted his hand from the switch, but did not restore it yet.

“I won't abandon you,” he said. “Either of you. But I won't tell you this is rescue because you need it to be.”

“We need you not to,” Elara answered.

***

He allowed one low-power workstation, running serial tasks under a hard cutoff he alone could reset. The receiver would record without playback. No transmitter access; no further burn. Before he restored the terminal, Anya ate half a ration under his silent supervision. When she could not tear the wrapper with her sore arm, he opened it and set it beside her.

Their first shared document contained no translation.

It contained rules.

Preserve the raw stream. Mark every transformation. Test against shuffled sequences. Do not alter a failed prediction after seeing the result.

Elara added: A familiar sound is not an identification.

Anya read it, then moved the cursor down a line.

Neither is an unfamiliar sound proof of intelligence.

They began.

Prime-number mapping occupied the first hours. They counted intervals in the smallest stable unit, tried grouping pulse lengths into factors, searched for a numerical scaffold beneath the displaced fourth beat. Nothing held across successive windows. One arrangement yielded a pleasing series until Anya included the pulses they had discarded as interference.

“There,” she said. “It only works if we remove what contradicts it.”

Elara struck out the result. The small act hurt disproportionately.

They tested recurrent units, nested boundaries, dependencies stretched across repetitions. Elara's notes filled with branching diagrams that seemed almost grammatical until a fresh segment arrived. Rules multiplied to accommodate exceptions. Soon the exceptions were doing all the work.

Marcus brought water in measured portions. He watched her delete an entire page.

“Anything?”

“A language in which every sentence requires its own grammar.”

“Is that possible?”

“It's possible to invent one. That doesn't mean we're discovering one.”

Later they searched for self-similarity: a pattern repeating within its own intervals, structures that might persist when enlarged or compressed. Anya's fractal plots grew delicate as frost, then lost their beauty beside the controls. Shuffled noise produced branching shapes too.

By then the workstation fan had become the loudest thing in Elara's world. She could no longer remember whether she had slept since Marcus cut the power.

They slept in turns.

When she woke, Anya had mapped the intervals onto pitches.

“Not voices,” she said quickly. “Relationships. Duration and frequency. A musical model.”

They listened once, at low volume. Four thin notes moved through the compartment, with the last arriving where the old silence had been. Elara found herself waiting for a resolution. None came.

“Again?” Anya asked.

Elara looked at the playback control.

“Test the next window first.”

The model failed to predict it.

***

The apparent coordinates emerged near the end of their allotted run.

A repeating group, treated as signed values, gave them two angles and a distance. Anya placed the result on the surviving navigation chart. A small white cross appeared in a region without catalogued objects, within the broad uncertainty of the signal's bearing.

For several seconds nobody moved.

“How many choices?” Marcus asked.

Anya's hand hovered over the display. “For the units? Several. For the origin, more.”

“And if you choose differently?”

The cross jumped. A second interpretation put it behind them; another placed it beyond the chart's useful range.

Elara leaned back. The first cross remained in her vision, a tiny destination burned into the dark.

Even that preferred reading offered no star, no station, nothing a ship could reach with their fuel. They saved the derivation with its assumptions attached. Marcus did not touch the navigation controls.

When the cutoff arrived, the workstation dimmed without protest.

Anya rested her forehead on her good hand. “I thought having rules would make it hurt less.”

Elara had no answer that would withstand their own tests.

On the passive recorder, the signal continued accumulating. She studied the intervals without playing them. She had spent her life asking what a speaker meant, what absent thing a sound could summon between two minds. Perhaps that was the wrong demand here.

A question did not have to contain the thing it asked for.

She almost said it aloud, then stopped. It was a suspicion, not a result. The dark had offered no translation, no destination, no promise that anyone waited inside it.

Beside her, Marcus steadied Anya's cup while she drank.

Elara opened a blank page. For once she entered no symbols from the signal. She wrote their three names, and sat looking at them.

## Chapter 6: The Abyss Gazes Back

The signal grew faint at 0430 ship time.

Not quieter. Quieter implied distance only to the ear. This was different: the pulses arrived thinned, their edges worn away, as though something had carried them through too much dark. The auxiliary receiver found them, lost them, found them again. Short. Short. Long. Short—then a pause so wide that Elara began to doubt the next pulse would come.

Anya sat beneath the receiver with both hands wrapped around an untouched cup of water. Her bruised shoulder was tucked close to her body. On the screen, the waveform trembled like a pulse under skin.

“It’s moving away,” she said.

“We don’t know that,” Elara answered.

“We know the amplitude is falling.”

“We know the amplitude at our receiver is falling.”

Anya gave a small, exhausted laugh. “You can put a fence around every sentence and still be abandoned inside it.”

Marcus stood at the compartment door, one hand braced against the frame. The ship’s limited power had reduced the corridor lights to a reddish dusk. He looked as if he had been assembled from shadows and practical decisions.

“Log it,” he said. “Then shut down the receiver.”

Anya’s head turned sharply. “No.”

“The cutoff is not negotiable.”

“You said you wouldn’t abandon us.”

“I said I wouldn’t abandon you. A machine is not you.”

“The machine is the only thing answering.”

Elara watched Anya’s fingers tighten around the cup. A white crescent appeared beneath each nail.

“There’s something you should know,” Anya said.

Marcus’s expression altered by less than a degree. “What did you do?”

“Nothing to the ship.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

Anya looked at the signal. “I transmitted messages.”

The compartment seemed to contract.

Elara felt the old reflex rise in her—the need to define terms, establish sequence, separate confession from accusation. “When?”

“After we changed course. Before the lockout. Sometimes through the acknowledgment channel. Sometimes through maintenance diagnostics.”

Marcus pushed away from the door. “You used the transmitter without authorization?”

“I used a few seconds at a time.”

“You used power we do not have.”

“I used words.”

“You used the reactor.”

Anya stood too quickly. Her face went pale, and she caught the edge of the console. “I said their names. My brother’s. My mother’s. I sent the things I never said when they were alive. I thought—”

She stopped. Her mouth worked around the next word as though it were too large to swallow.

“I thought they might be there.”

Elara remembered hearing her father’s throat-clearing in the static, and the shame that had followed—not shame for wanting him, but for wanting the wanting to be evidence. She crossed the space between them.

“Anya.”

“No. Don’t make it clinical.” Anya’s eyes shone. “Don’t tell me it was projection. I know what projection is. I know what grief does to an auditory cortex. I know every reason it wasn’t them.”

Marcus’s anger had become something colder. “Then why spend power on it?”

“Because knowing is not the same as believing.”

He flinched.

For a moment, the only sound was the ventilation fan struggling behind the wall. Then Marcus said, “The colony ship I served on was called the Pelagos.”

Neither woman spoke.

“It left Luna eleven years ago,” he continued. “Two thousand and six colonists. Agricultural modules, seed vaults, families. A nine-year transit to Tau Ceti. We lost contact in the fourth month.”

Elara had seen the name in old mission reports. Everyone had. The Pelagos had become a cautionary footnote, an unresolved communications failure.

“You were on it?” Anya whispered.

“Escort crew. We were three days out when the last packet arrived. No distress call. No debris. No gravitational event. Just absence.” He looked toward the bulkhead, beyond it, toward the black region where Earth had been. “I told myself I learned the correct lesson. That an empty channel was empty. That grief was a story we told after the facts.”

“And now?” Elara asked.

“Now the facts have started telling the story.”

The signal vanished.

Anya made a sound like something tearing. She shoved past Marcus toward the observation deck.

“Anya, stop.”

She did not stop. The deck’s viewport showed a wilderness without stars near its center, the dead zone swallowing even the weak points of distant light. Anya dropped to the manual communications panel and began undoing its cover.

“What are you doing?” Marcus demanded.

“Sending one more.”

“The transmitter is locked.”

“I can bypass the lock.”

“No.”

She seized a tool from the panel and struck the housing. Sparks snapped blue in the dimness.

Marcus caught her wrist. She twisted, struck him with her good shoulder, and he stumbled against the observation rail. The rail had been damaged during the course burn; its fasteners lifted with a metallic shriek.

“Let go!”

“You’ll kill us both.”

“Then at least someone will hear us!”

She wrenched free. The rail gave way. Marcus went backward, one boot sliding toward the open maintenance recess where a pressure seal had failed. Anya grabbed his sleeve. For one terrible second they hung joined at the edge, his weight pulling her after him.

Elara moved before thought. She seized Anya around the waist and locked her feet beneath the deck brace. Marcus caught the remaining rail with his free hand. The three of them became a single strained mechanism, breathless and shaking.

“Don’t let go,” Anya gasped.

“I’m not,” Elara said.

Marcus hauled himself up. Together they collapsed on the deck. No one spoke. The dead zone filled the viewport. It was not looking at them. It could not look at them. Yet Elara felt seen by the shape their terror made in its presence.

Minutes passed. Or an hour. The ship’s systems clicked and settled around them.

Then the receiver, still active in the analysis compartment, found one pulse.

Short.

A long silence.

Another pulse, fainter than the first.

Elara rose slowly and returned to the console. The others followed, not touching, but close enough that their shadows overlapped.

She replayed the recording once. Not as sound. As intervals.

The pattern did not resemble a voice. It did not point toward a place, or carry an image, or answer any question they had asked. It endured the silence between its pulses. It left room. It waited without promising that waiting would be rewarded.

“A pattern of patience,” she said.

Marcus looked at her. “That’s interpretation.”

“Yes.” Her voice trembled. “It is. But so was everything else.”

Anya wiped her face with the heel of her hand. “So what does it mean?”

Elara looked at the waveform, then at the names written on her blank page: Elara. Marcus. Anya.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Maybe that is the first honest translation.”

The signal pulsed again, remote and nearly gone.

“To translate it,” Elara said, “we have to decide what we want it to mean.”

## Chapter 7: The Grammar of Hope

The Grammar of Hope

Elara woke without knowing when she had slept. The analysis compartment was dark except for the receiver’s narrow green display, where the signal appeared as four pale marks and then a longer absence.

Short. Short. Long. Short.

The pattern did not announce itself. It did not demand attention. It waited through the ship’s shuddering fans, through cooling metal, through the shallow breaths of the two people sleeping beyond the half-closed hatch. It had waited while they named it, measured it, distorted it, loved it, and feared it. It had remained when their interpretations failed.

Elara placed her hand beside the display.

A language, she thought, required more than structure. It required a distinction between a sign and what the sign pointed toward. It required a world shared by speaker and listener, or at least the hope that such a world might be made. The signal had offered them no dictionary, no referent, no proof of intention. Every translation they had attempted had smuggled in their own hunger.

But language had never begun with dictionaries.

A child crying in the dark did not encode a proposition. A hand raised at a distance did not explain itself. The cry and the hand became meaningful because someone turned toward them. Meaning was not always hidden inside a sound. Sometimes it was made in the space between the sound and the answering body.

She opened a blank file and typed:

WE ARE HERE.

Then deleted it. Too declarative. Too much like a flag planted in a country that might not exist.

WE HEAR YOU.

Deleted that too. They did not know whether anyone was there.

At the bottom of the page she wrote three names: ELARA. MARCUS. ANYA.

The signal pulsed again.

When Marcus entered, he moved as though each joint had been tightened overnight. His shoulder was strapped where the observation-deck rail had bruised him. He carried a maintenance tablet and an untouched cup of water.

“You’re awake,” he said.

“So are you.”

“That’s less impressive. I was scheduled to be.”

She turned the display toward him. “I think we have been asking the wrong question.”

Marcus glanced at the marks. “We’ve asked several wrong questions.”

“We treated it as a language waiting to be translated. But perhaps it is not a message. Perhaps it’s an opening.”

“An opening to what?”

“To us. To whoever might be listening. To no one.” She heard how thin that sounded. “A signal can be meaningful without carrying information. A lighthouse doesn’t tell the sea where to go. It makes presence visible.”

Marcus leaned against the console. “You’re saying it’s a star.”

“I’m saying it may be nothing more than a light in the dark. But if we answer, then the relationship is real even if the source remains unknowable.”

He looked at her. “That sounds dangerously close to hope.”

“It is not rescue.”

“No. It might be worse. Rescue at least has procedures.”

Anya appeared in the hatch, pale and careful, her injured shoulder stiff. “What might be worse?”

“Elara has invented a new category of uncertainty,” Marcus said.

“I’m familiar with the old ones.” Anya lowered herself beside the receiver. “Explain.”

Elara described the failed translations, the noise controls, the impossible coordinates, the way the pulse persisted without offering a stable object to name. She told them she no longer believed it was Earth speaking through a wound in space. Saying it made her chest tighten, but the pain was clean; it carried no false comfort of recognition.

“Maybe it’s a test,” she said. “Maybe an artifact. Maybe a natural rhythm with no mind behind it. We cannot decide that from here. We can decide whether we will answer. Human language was never perfected before humans used it. We reached toward one another with broken sounds, gestures, names for things we did not understand. The reaching came first.”

Anya kept her gaze on the waveform. “If it isn’t Earth, then all those messages…”

“Were still messages,” Elara said. “They were yours. They meant what you meant when you sent them.”

Anya’s mouth trembled. “I wanted my brother to answer.”

“I know.”

“I kept thinking that if the signal was some remnant of the planet, he might be inside it. Not alive. I knew that. But present.”

Marcus set down his tablet. “Grief makes a door out of anything.”

Anya looked at him. “And you?”

His expression closed, then opened by degrees.

“I have been calling an orderly death a duty,” he said. “Preserve oxygen. Preserve water. Preserve power. Keep everyone calm. Let the Meridian go dark with dignity.” He looked from Anya to Elara. “But dignity is not purpose. I don’t want to simply die correctly.”

The receiver gave its four quiet marks.

“I want to do one thing that is not only an ending,” he said. “If we have enough power for one answer, we should spend it answering.”

Anya wiped her face. “No Earth references.”

“No assumptions,” Elara agreed.

“No promise that anyone receives it,” Marcus added. “No coordinates. No demand.”

Elara opened the archive. The languages of the vanished world waited in sealed files: greetings, blessings, laments, jokes, declarations made beside beds and graves. She played none of them. Instead she asked each of the others for a word.

“Presence,” said Anya.

“Witness,” said Marcus.

Elara considered the names on her page. “Answer.”

They built the message from those words, testing its shape not for beauty but honesty. A sequence of tones. A pause long enough to contain uncertainty. Then three spoken names, recorded in their own voices, followed by a declaration:

We exist.
We have loved.
We are here.
We are not afraid to answer.

It was not a translation. It did not pretend to be one. It was an offering made without knowing the hands that might receive it.

Marcus unlocked the transmitter control but did not activate it. “This will consume the reserve we need for full life support.”

“We know,” Anya said.

The ship groaned around them. Somewhere beyond the hull, the dead region waited without distance or shape.

Elara placed her hand over Marcus’s on the control. Anya added hers with her good arm.

For one breath, there were no survivors, no lost families, no vanished planet. Only three living beings agreeing that silence could be answered.

“Ready?” Marcus asked.

“No,” Elara said.

Anya gave a small, broken laugh. “That’s the most honest word we have.”

The signal pulsed once more, patient as darkness.

Marcus began the transmission.

## Chapter 8: The Last Word

The transmitter room had never been meant for three people. Marcus had removed the inspection panel and spread its screws in a careful crescent on the deck, while Anya sat beside the coding console with her shoulder braced and her knees touching the coolant manifold. Elara stood behind them, one hand on the bulkhead, listening to the ship breathe.

The Meridian's breath had grown shallow.

Every few seconds a pump coughed and recovered. The air smelled of hot insulation and recycled water. Above the console, the life-support gauge blinked amber. It no longer gave them a reassuring number, only a narrowing bar that changed whenever the failing relay stole another fraction of power.

Marcus held a probe between his teeth. “Relay C-seven is gone.”

Anya looked up. “Gone as in replaceable?”

“Gone as in it has achieved spiritual enlightenment.” He pulled the probe free. “The contact is fused. If I separate it, we lose the auxiliary bus.”

“And if you don’t?”

“We lose it when it fails on its own.”

Elara watched his hands. They were steady, though the left trembled whenever he reached above his shoulder. “How long?”

“For the relay? Minutes, maybe hours. For us?” Marcus glanced at the gauge. “Long enough to do what we came here to do.”

It was not an answer, but it was the kind they had learned to accept: exact about the immediate thing, honest about the rest.

Anya returned to the code. Her fingers moved slowly, from care rather than uncertainty. She had reduced their message to tones, intervals, and redundancies that could survive distance and interference. No picture. No language a receiver might mistake for noise. A compact grammar of pulses: identifier, pause, body, pause, repetition.

“Three copies,” she said. “The first in the simplest encoding. The second with parity checks. The third—”

“Is not necessary,” Marcus said.

“It is if the first two arrive damaged.”

“If the first two arrive damaged, we will be transmitting into a universe that has already misplaced us.”

Anya's mouth tightened. “Then let it misplace us accurately.”

Marcus looked at her. The corner of his mouth lifted. “Three copies.”

Elara carried words from the archive in her head. Some were thousands of years old; some had been spoken by people whose names survived only as database labels. A greeting from a language with no living speakers. A farewell shaped like a blessing. A word for the sudden happiness of seeing someone after believing them lost. A word for grief inseparable from love.

She had thought preservation meant keeping forms intact. Now she understood that it also meant allowing words to change hands.

At the composition terminal, she opened a blank document.

“Read it,” Marcus said.

“It isn't finished.”

“Nothing is finished.”

So she read the first version.

“We are three travelers from a vanished world. We do not know who receives this. We send no claim, no request, no map. We offer our presence.”

Anya listened with her eyes closed.

Elara continued. “I am Elara Voss. My name means—”

“No meanings,” Marcus said gently. “Not unless they matter to someone who knows the language.”

“They matter to me.”

“Then keep them for the spoken record.”

She deleted the sentence.

They took turns offering what could remain.

Anya gave them her brother's name, then withdrew it, afraid that naming him would make the message too much about absence. Elara told her that absence was not selfish. Marcus said names were not anchors; they were doors. Anya entered the name after all.

Marcus offered “witness.” Anya added “presence.” Elara added “answer.”

They spoke of joy: food shared in a kitchen, sunlight through a window, the ridiculous triumph of a repaired machine. They spoke of grief without describing its cause, and of greetings and farewells, of words people used when they wanted another person to stay and when staying was impossible.

At last Elara typed:

“We exist.
We have loved.
We are here.
We are not afraid to answer.
We were here.”

The final line remained on the screen.

Anya touched it. “Past tense.”

“It is honest,” Elara said.

“It sounds like surrender.”

“It sounds like testimony.”

Marcus tightened the strap over his bruised shoulder. “Both can be true.”

For a while, none of them spoke. The silence held oxygen's soft hiss, cooling metal, and Anya's uneven breathing. Marcus's hand descended onto the deck between them, palm upward. Elara placed hers in it. Anya added hers last.

Marcus confessed that he had once believed command meant carrying everyone's fear so they could remain unburdened. “I thought that was courage,” he said. “Maybe courage is letting someone know you are afraid and staying at the controls anyway.”

Anya said she was sorry for the messages she had sent into the dark. Not because she had loved her family too fiercely, but because she had mistaken longing for evidence. Elara told her that longing was evidence of something: evidence that the living had been changed by love.

“Do you think that is enough?” Anya asked.

“No,” Elara said. “But it is real.”

Marcus began the final reroute. He pulled the reserve bus away from heating, navigation, and every system that no longer served the message. The transmitter's capacitors charged in reluctant increments. Lights vanished along the corridor. The Meridian surrendered its conveniences, then its protections.

The external pulse continued.

Short. Short. Long. Short.

Elara watched while Anya loaded the code and Marcus armed the sequence. Then the pattern changed.

The pulses stretched. The intervals opened. The last short note lengthened into a single sustained tone, low enough to feel in the teeth rather than hear with the ears.

Anya froze. “It changed.”

Marcus's hand hovered above the control. “Natural variation?”

“Unknown,” Anya whispered.

Elara closed her eyes. The tone passed through the hull, through their bones, through every word they had failed to translate. It did not sound like a voice. It did not promise rescue. It did not answer any question.

Still, the universe seemed suddenly attentive.

As if it were holding its breath.

Marcus looked to Elara. “Ready?”

She looked at Anya, at the archive icon glowing behind the transmission window, at the message waiting in its three forms.

“Yes,” she said. “We are here.”

## Chapter 9: Transmission

Marcus pressed the control with his thumb.

There was no answering vibration, no bright surge through the room. A small square on the console turned white. Beside it, a counter began moving.

For a moment Elara thought something had failed. She had expected the ship to object—to shudder, to make some final mechanical complaint. Instead the Meridian continued its exhausted breathing. The transmitter fan stirred a loose screw on the deck until it rolled against Marcus's boot.

“Carrier established,” Anya said.

Her voice was barely louder than the fan.

The counter advanced. Their names were leaving. The greetings followed, then the words Elara had chosen from the archive: sounds once made in kitchens, beside graves, across rivers, through walls. Here they had become electrical distinctions, brief departures from silence. Nothing in the machinery knew what it carried.

Elara mouthed a farewell with the timing she remembered from its recording. The woman who had spoken it had laughed afterward, embarrassed by the microphone. Elara had kept the laugh in CONTINUANCE, though she had not put it in the message.

There was so much they had not put in.

“First copy complete.”

Marcus lowered himself beside the console. His knee struck the deck harder than he intended. Elara reached for him, but he shook his head and rested his hand against her ankle, steadying himself there.

The second copy went out.

A light above the inspection panel faded from amber to brown. The fan slowed, recovered, slowed again. Anya held two fingers against the console casing as though taking a pulse.

“Don't stop,” she whispered.

Elara could not tell whether she meant the transmitter or herself.

The third copy reached its final line.

We were here.

The white square went dark.

“Complete,” Anya said. She looked at the counter once more. “All three.”

Marcus reached up and isolated the spent transmitter. The fan stopped. Without it, the room seemed larger and much colder.

Elara had imagined relief. Instead she felt an ordinary, humiliating weakness: her legs would no longer hold her. She sat down beside Marcus, catching her sleeve on the edge of the open panel. He freed the cloth carefully. Across from them, Anya folded forward until her forehead rested against her good arm.

They had done it. Somewhere beyond the hull, their message was moving away from them. It needed nothing further from their bodies.

For a little while the sustained external tone remained audible through the receiver.

Then it ceased.

Anya lifted her head.

The receiver's indicator still glowed. Elara watched it, waiting for the sound to resume before she allowed herself to think about its absence.

A pump clicked. Somewhere behind the bulkhead, metal contracted with a faint, clean snap.

“Did we lose reception?” Marcus asked.

Anya checked the small diagnostic strip. “It's powered. That's all I can tell you without running a sweep.”

“Don't run one.”

“I know.”

Elara hated how quickly she wanted to bargain. One note, she thought. Not a rescue, not an explanation. Just one note to make this room bearable. She had offered their words without conditions, and already some hidden part of her was presenting a bill.

Marcus's hand found hers on the deck. His fingers were cold.

“We sent it,” he said.

She nodded, but kept watching the indicator.

The silence lasted long enough for Anya to wipe her face with her sleeve, long enough for Elara to hear the slight catch in Marcus's breathing.

Then a note sounded.

A pause. Another note.

The pulse had returned, no longer sustained. Its intervals seemed wider now, the edges less abrupt. Elara felt each arrival without the old flinch. It sounded almost gentle.

“Is it slower?” she asked.

Anya studied the narrow trace. “Compared with the tone, yes. Compared with the early recordings—I would need the clocks aligned.” She let her hand fall before it reached the controls.

“We don't have to,” Marcus said.

“No.” Anya listened through another interval. “We don't.”

Her face changed, not into happiness, but into something less strained.

“I kept thinking it had to become something else,” she said. “A voice. A place. Someone coming back.” She looked at Elara. “But it was here before we knew what to call it.”

“It has changed,” Elara said softly.

“The pattern has. We measured that.” Anya drew her knees closer. “I mean what we actually know about it. That hasn't changed. We still don't know who's there. Or if anyone is.”

Another pulse sounded. Anya did not turn toward the display.

“I can hear it now without hearing my brother.”

Elara reached across the narrow space. Anya took her free hand.

Nothing had been returned to them. Yet the room no longer felt like a place where each of them waited alone.

Marcus rubbed his thumb along Elara's knuckles.

“I thought being alive was a problem I was supposed to solve,” he whispered. “Keep enough air moving. Find the right course. Bring everyone home.”

“You kept us here,” she said.

“Not for long enough.” He swallowed. “But perhaps it was a voice to be offered. Not a problem. Being here.”

Elara leaned against his uninjured shoulder. She had no better words to give him.

On the console, the composition window closed as its circuit lost power. CONTINUANCE remained in protected storage; she had checked it before they armed the transmitter. She did not check again.

Beyond the doorway, the last corridor light went out. The three of them stayed together on the deck while the Meridian drifted onward, carrying less warmth, less light, and nothing left to send.

In the dark, Anya adjusted her grip so that it no longer hurt her shoulder. Marcus made room. Elara listened to their breathing between the pulses.

## Chapter 10: Beyond the Void

Elara woke because the air had changed.

It was colder, and each breath seemed to arrive from farther away. The vents had gone quiet. Somewhere in the ship, a fan tried once to turn and gave up.

She opened her eyes.

The transmitter room was almost dark. Marcus sat with his back against the bulkhead, his strapped shoulder tilted carefully away from the wall. Anya lay curled beside the dead console, one hand still resting on Elara’s sleeve. Their faces appeared and disappeared in the weak blue wash from the receiver display.

Outside, the pulse continued.

Short. Short. Long. Short.

It crossed the receiver’s speaker with no urgency and no promise. Elara could imagine it continuing forever, indifferent as starlight, beautiful only because someone had lived long enough to notice it.

She let her eyes close again, not to sleep but to remember.

We are here.

Their names had gone out with that declaration. Three points of presence in a dark without coordinates.

We have loved.

That had been harder. Love was not a measurement, and the archive offered too many ways to say it. A word that folded affection into duty. Another that named the ache of wanting someone safe. She remembered listening to recordings, unable to choose what would stand for all the others. Even now she could hear the breath before each speaker began, that small preparation to enter another person’s life.

We are not afraid to answer.

That had not been entirely true. Fear had sat with them at the console, had watched Marcus reroute power and Anya check the code. But a statement did not need to describe a finished condition. Sometimes saying a thing was the first movement toward it.

We were here.

She moved her lips around the words. Already they belonged to a moment she could not return to: the fan still turning, the counter advancing, the three of them with something left to do.

The message might never be received. It might scatter, pass through empty systems, arrive where no mind could recognize its structure. The unknown source might be a machine, a natural process, or something for which they had no category.

Still, they had spoken.

She felt Anya stir. “Are you awake?”

“Yes.”

“Do you hear it?”

“Yes.”

Anya’s fingers tightened around her sleeve. “I used to think I’d recognize him. Even if it was only one sound.”

Elara waited.

“I don’t think that now.”

“I know.”

Across from them, Marcus opened his eyes. For a moment he seemed about to stand, some old obligation gathering in his face. Then he looked at the dark console and settled back.

“Anything?” he asked.

“We’re here,” Anya said.

He nodded. “All right.”

Elara wanted to ask him to check the storage indicator once more. CONTINUANCE remained there, protected, requiring no power to retain its sleeping voices. She had checked it herself. Still she wanted the reassurance of his hand on a panel, his patient attention to one more thing that mattered to her.

Instead she said, “Stay.”

“I am.”

She shifted closer to his uninjured side. He helped her with a care that made her want to weep.

In the dimness she could no longer make out the commander’s insignia, or the dried mark on Anya’s cuff. They were simply the two people who knew what had happened here. Witnesses, she thought. Not proof against extinction. People who had heard one another speak.

The receiver light weakened. Anya drew her knees toward her chest, making room for Elara’s feet. Marcus tucked a loose fold of insulation around them. None of it was sufficient. Each small adjustment relieved something.

Elara imagined a language she had never studied, one without tense or possession, without the need to divide the speaker from the person listening. She could not imagine its words. Only its gestures: Marcus freeing her caught sleeve; Anya reaching across the deck; a greeting offered where there might be no one to hear.

She would once have demanded more of a language. Distinctions. Structure. A way to say precisely what had been lost.

Now she tried to lift her hand and managed only to turn it palm upward. Marcus took it. Anya found the other.

“There,” Elara whispered.

“What?” Marcus asked.

She could not explain. She squeezed his fingers instead, weakly, and felt his answering pressure.

The ship dimmed again.

For a while there was only their breathing between the pulses. Elara lost track of which breath belonged to whom. Once she thought the speaker had stopped, but the next note came before she could ask.

She thought of the languages in CONTINUANCE: greetings and curses, lullabies, testimony, the names people had given rain. Of a woman laughing after a farewell into a microphone. Elara had kept that laugh. Whatever happened to the message, the laugh remained aboard with them, beyond her reach but not erased.

“Thank you,” she said.

Neither asked for what.

A faint flicker crossed the receiver’s small sensor display.

Anya lifted her head. Elara felt the effort travel through their joined hands.

The familiar pulse traced itself across the screen. After it came another brief sequence, lights separated by a pause that almost resembled the opening of their transmission.

Marcus looked toward Anya.

“I don’t know,” she whispered.

She did not release Elara to reach for the controls.

It could have been a failing circuit. Something delayed within the instruments. Their own words returning by a path none of them understood—or something that had never belonged to them.

Elara watched the flicker fade, unable to tell whether it would come again. She had no translation to give it. Beside her were Marcus and Anya, still holding her hands. She let that be the last thing she knew with certainty.

To answer the dark is to become a light, even if no one sees it.
