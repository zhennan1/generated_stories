## Chapter 1: The Vanishing

The observation deck had been built for wonder.

Its curved windows had once framed Earth: blue oceans, white cloud, the thin gold edge of atmosphere. Now they framed a black region with sharp, unnatural boundaries. The stars seemed to bend around it. Where Earth should have been, there was not emptiness but a wound.

Dr. Elara Voss stood with one hand against the glass.

Behind her, the Meridian ticked and breathed through damaged systems. Fans labored in the ceiling. Somewhere below, a relay clicked at irregular intervals, like a person trying to remember a word.

“Give me the coordinates again,” Anya said.

Her voice was small in the enormous room.

Marcus Hale did not look up from his tablet. “Sol system. Third orbital position. Earth’s last confirmed location.”

“And the result?”

“Nothing there.”

Anya sat at the sensor console among loose cables and opened panels. Her black hair had come loose from its tie. She had not slept since the emergency burn had thrown them against their restraints.

“The moon?”

“Gone.”

“Venus?”

“Present.”

“Mars?”

“Present.”

Marcus glanced toward the window. His face looked hollowed by exhaustion. “The Sun is present. Most of the system is present. Earth is not.”

He said it with the same flat precision he had used to report the loss of their port engine.

Elara opened the archive on her wrist display. A list unfolded: Akkadian, Ainu, Aramaic, Basque, Breton, Cherokee, Coptic, Etruscan, Ga, Gothic, Ladino.

“Why are you making a list?” Marcus asked.

“It’s an archive.”

“Of dead languages.”

“Some of them are not dead.”

“Some of them were spoken by people who are now dead.”

Elara looked again at the dark beyond the window. “That distinction may not matter for long.”

Marcus set down the tablet. “We have fourteen days of oxygen if we reduce the habitat temperature. Nine if we don’t. Food for twenty-two. Water reclamation is at sixty-three percent. The starboard radiator is losing pressure. I’m shutting down nonessential systems.”

“The archive is nonessential?”

“To life support, yes.”

“To us?”

His gaze met hers. “I don’t know anymore.”

Anya made a sound at the console, not quite a laugh. “Can you stop talking as if this is a normal emergency?”

She pulled a cable free and replaced it. The display filled with green static.

“There should be a gravitational signature,” she said. “If Earth was destroyed, there would be debris. If it was displaced, there would be a distortion. If it was—”

“Erased?” Marcus asked.

Anya’s hands stopped.

Elara had watched her sister disappear from a family call in the middle of a sentence. Mei had been in Singapore, preparing for a night shift at the hospital. The call had broken into white noise. Then there had been no Earth to call.

Anya pressed her fingertips to the console. “I need a physical explanation.”

“You may not get one,” Marcus said.

“I need one anyway.”

“That’s not how the universe works.”

“No,” Anya said. “Apparently this is how the universe works.”

They had been a survey vessel before the evacuation corridors, before every mission became a wager disguised as procedure. Their assignment had been to map the outer-system relay network. Earth vanished while they were three days beyond Neptune, after a micrometeorite strike crippled navigation and forced them to turn back.

They had expected traffic. Rescue signals. Orbital stations.

They found silence.

Elara returned to the archive. She played recordings in Old Norse, then Quechua. The failing speakers carried voices speaking of winter, the dead, a child counting toward ten. She copied each file into protected storage and added annotations: kinship terms, idioms, words without direct equivalents.

A word for longing toward a place that might never have existed.

A root connecting peace, surrender, and wholeness.

The Ainu word for the feeling of seeing something familiar after a long absence.

“What are you preserving?” Anya asked.

“Evidence.”

“Of what?”

“That we were more than an event.”

Marcus rose and opened the supply locker. He counted ration packs and entered the number.

Elara watched him. “You don’t believe we’ll survive.”

“I believe survival is a verb, not a promise.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

He counted again. “No.”

The honesty settled over them more heavily than panic.

Anya leaned back, rubbing her eyes. “My mother called every Sunday. She complained that I never visited enough, then told me exactly what she’d eaten for lunch. Even when she was angry, she told me what she’d eaten.” Her mouth tightened. “I can’t remember the last thing she said.”

“You don’t have to remember,” Elara said.

“Yes, I do.”

“No. You don’t.”

Anya turned on her. “You get to record everyone else’s words and call that remembrance. I have fragments. Glitches. Sensor noise. I need to know what happened.”

“You may never know.”

“Then what am I supposed to do?”

Elara had no answer. Beyond the glass, the wound waited where a world had been.

Marcus closed the locker. “We do our jobs.”

Anya stared at him. “For what?”

He looked toward the window. His reflection floated over the stars. “For the next hour.”

It was a terrible answer, but it was one.

They worked.

Marcus isolated the damaged radiator and rerouted coolant through the agricultural bay. Anya fed every sensor log into a reconstruction model, searching for a gravitational tremor, a neutrino burst, any residue of impossible violence. Elara built her archive, speaking the recorded languages aloud so the microphones captured cadence as well as words.

At 03:17 ship time, Anya froze.

“Wait.”

Marcus stopped beside the power junction. Elara lifted her head.

Anya rewound the display. A thin line trembled at the bottom of the spectrum, too regular to be background radiation. She enlarged it. The line resolved into pulses: three short, one long, three short. Then silence.

The pattern repeated.

Anya ran the diagnostic twice. “It’s not internal.”

Marcus came to her station. “A damaged sensor?”

“I isolated the array.”

“Thermal noise?”

“Not at that interval.”

“Some natural source?”

“There’s no catalogued object in that region.”

“What region?”

She entered the coordinates. A dead zone beyond the mapped relay boundary appeared on the display, a patch of space with no star, planet, or recorded debris.

The pulse came again.

Three short. One long. Three short.

Elara felt the hair rise along her arms. “Can you identify it?”

“No.”

“Can you tell if it’s repeating?”

“It has repeated forty-one times.”

Marcus leaned closer. “Could it be a beacon?”

“Maybe.”

“Could it be natural?”

“Maybe.”

He began another question, but Anya turned toward them. Her face was exhausted, frightened, and suddenly alive.

“It’s structured,” she said.

The Meridian shuddered. The lights dimmed, then returned.

On the display, the dead region pulsed once more.

Elara reached toward the empty space beside the console, as if someone might be there to take her hand.

“Record it,” she said.

Anya was already saving the file.

Marcus looked from the signal to the black place where Earth had been. For the first time since the vanishing, something moved behind his expression—not hope exactly, but the refusal to surrender its shape.

The pulse came again, patient in the dark.

Three short. One long. Three short.

## Chapter 2: The Signal

Anya did not call out immediately.

She watched the lower edge of the sensor display as a narrow blue line rose and fell beneath the residual glare of Earth’s coordinates.

Three short pulses.

One long.

Three short.

Silence.

Then the sequence began again.

She checked the timestamp, then the system clock. The interval was exact. She isolated the event from the background noise and played it through the sensor bay’s speaker. The pulses became a dry, low tapping, too precise to belong to the Meridian’s usual mechanical complaints.

“Computer,” she said, “identify the source.”

The computer searched its records. “No matching internal emission.”

Anya opened the intercom. “Commander Hale. Dr. Voss. Sensor bay.”

Only the ventilation fans answered.

“Now.”

Marcus arrived first, wrench tucked into his belt, his expression already irritated by the possibility of another failure. Voss followed with her archive tablet pressed to her chest.

“What broke?” Marcus asked.

“Nothing,” Anya said.

“That is not reassuring.”

She enlarged the waveform. “The pattern has repeated forty-one times.”

Marcus leaned over the console. “A pulse?”

“A pattern.”

“From what?”

Anya rotated the star map toward them. The Meridian’s position appeared as a green point beyond Neptune’s silent relay network. A second marker blinked eleven light-minutes away, in a region catalogued as empty.

“There,” she said. “No registered object. No debris field. No mass signature.”

Marcus frowned. “Eleven light-minutes is close enough to be ours.”

“It isn’t.”

“Prove it.”

“I isolated every internal system. Navigation, reactor controls, life support, communications. Then I switched to the backup sensor mast and disconnected the primary mast from the processor. The signal continued with the same timing.”

“Instrument echo,” Marcus said.

“From what instrument?”

“A dying one.”

“Which one?”

He glanced at Voss. “You hear her? External does not mean intelligent.”

Voss had not looked away from the waveform. “It is structured.”

“So is a heartbeat.”

“A heartbeat is structured because a body is structured.”

“A pulsar is structured. A magnetar is structured. The universe is full of repeating phenomena.”

Anya brought up the spectral analysis. “No neutrino emission. No gravitational fluctuation. No radiation profile matching a known natural source. The intervals vary by less than a nanosecond across the entire recording.”

Marcus said nothing.

The Meridian shuddered, and somewhere behind the walls a relay clicked twice. The ship’s other sounds were familiar now: the uneven fans, the soft ticking of strained metal, the occasional groan from the starboard radiator. The signal was unlike all of them. It did not falter. It waited.

Voss touched the display. “Three short, one long, three short. It could be a frame. Opening and closing.”

“You’re assigning grammar to a waveform.”

“I’m considering its possibilities.”

“You’re hoping.”

Her hand remained on the glass. “Those are not the same thing.”

Anya replayed the pattern. The pulses filled the bay.

Three.

One.

Three.

Silence.

Her mother’s voice returned to her—not the words, only the shape of them, blurred by the failed connection during the vanishing. Anya had spent three days imagining what came after the last fragment she heard. A farewell, perhaps. Or her name. She had no evidence for either.

“We should transmit an acknowledgment,” she said.

Marcus turned toward her. “No.”

“A narrow-band ping. Minimal power.”

“We don’t know what it is.”

“That is why we answer.”

“That is precisely why we don’t.” He pointed toward the life-support figures hovering in the corner of the display. “Fourteen days of oxygen with reduced temperature. Nine at normal settings. Water reclamation is already failing, and the starboard radiator is being held together by a bypass line. Every watt matters.”

Voss faced him. “What if it is a transmission?”

“Then it has been transmitting without us.”

“What if it’s waiting?”

“For whom?”

“For anyone.”

Marcus gave a humorless laugh. “That is not a survival plan.”

“No,” Voss said. “It is a reason to survive.”

The words remained between them.

Anya looked at the signal, then at the navigation map. Earth’s coordinates were still marked in red, though there was nothing there now except that impossible black region, sharp-edged and starless. The ship had spent three days measuring the absence. Every rescue request had vanished into silent channels. Every familiar station had failed to answer.

This was the first thing they had found that behaved as if someone—or something—might be listening.

“There may be no rescue,” Anya said. “There may be nothing at those coordinates. But if we shut everything down and die where we are, we learn nothing. We don’t even know whether Earth was destroyed, displaced, or erased.”

“Erased,” Voss said softly.

Marcus looked toward the observation deck hatch. Beyond it, the black wound waited beyond the glass.

“How much to alter course?” he asked.

Anya pulled up the navigation model. “A listening approach, not a full intercept. We can reach effective scan range in eleven hours.”

“Fuel.”

“Twenty-one hours of projected burn, possibly thirty if attitude correction is difficult.”

“Power.”

“The sensor array will need priority. We’d have to shut down the archive processors and reduce the agricultural lamps.”

Voss flinched. She had spent the last day recording endangered languages into the ship’s shrinking memory: greetings, prayers, jokes, words for kinds of rain that no longer fell on anyone. The archive was the only part of humanity she could still touch.

Marcus noticed. “We are not growing tomatoes for a signal.”

“We are not growing them for a signal,” Voss said. “We are growing them for us.”

“If we have us for long enough.”

Anya recalculated the course. “The signal may be directional. If we move closer, we can determine whether its intensity changes. We can also compare the pulse against the background field.”

“May,” Marcus said.

“Yes.”

He studied the numbers. His face was hollow from sleeplessness, but his hands remained steady on the console. He had spent the morning rerouting coolant, cataloguing food, and repairing systems designed to function with a crew of six. Now he was being asked to spend part of their remaining life on an unanswered pattern in empty space.

“What happens if it costs more than projected?” he asked.

“We abort.”

“What happens if it changes?”

Voss met his eyes. “Then we listen carefully.”

Marcus turned back to the display. The signal completed another cycle.

Three short.

One long.

Three short.

“I’ll authorize the course correction,” he said. “Passive scans only. No acknowledgment transmission until we know what we’re dealing with. If life support drops below emergency reserve, we stop.”

Voss nodded, though disappointment flickered across her face.

Anya entered the commands. “Course correction ready.”

Marcus placed his hand over the confirmation switch, then paused. “One more thing. We do not call it a message.”

Voss almost smiled. “What should we call it?”

“A signal.”

“That is what it is.”

“Until we know otherwise.”

He pressed the switch.

The Meridian began to turn, slowly and heavily, its damaged engines pushing against the dark. Stars moved across the observation windows. The black region where Earth had been slipped from view, and the unknown point beyond the relay boundary settled at the center of every screen.

No one called it hope.

Not yet.

But for the first time since Earth vanished, none of them called it noise.

## Chapter 3: First Grammar

The signal had no beginning.

That was Voss’s first conclusion. Each recording opened with the pattern already in progress: three short pulses, one long, three short, then silence. Again. Again. Again.

She sat in the archive bay with the waveform enlarged across three screens. The Meridian’s archive processors were meant to be sleeping, but she had diverted a narrow diagnostic channel and fed the signal through it, stripping away thermal hiss, solar interference, and the ship’s own electrical chatter.

What remained was almost offensively clean.

“Show me the edges,” she said.

The waveform expanded until every pulse became a ridge and hollow. The three short signals were not identical. Their leading edges differed by fractions too small for human hearing, but large enough for the instruments to record. The long pulse contained a slight dip at its center.

Voss marked it.

Then she marked the spaces between the pulses.

The intervals were not empty. They had shape.

She opened a file and typed:

UNIT A: three pulses  
UNIT B: one sustained pulse  
UNIT C: three pulses  
BOUNDARY: silence

The labels made the pattern look obedient.

A knock sounded at the hatch. Anya entered carrying two cups of recycled coffee, a cable looped around her wrist.

“You’re assigning names to it,” she said.

“I’m assigning names to observations.”

“That’s how it starts.”

Voss accepted the cup. It was warm enough to comfort and bitter enough to punish her for wanting comfort. “You built the filter.”

“A filter that removes the ship. Not the universe. Cosmic background and sensor artifacts remain.”

“The pattern persists across both masts.”

“Persistence isn’t language.”

“No. But repetition with internal variation is often language.”

“Traffic lights repeat with internal variation.”

“Traffic lights have a referent.”

“So do we, allegedly.”

Anya sat and connected the cable. She had built three models: one treating the signal as an astronomical oscillator, another as a compression artifact, and a third searching for mathematical relations between pulse lengths and pauses.

All failed quietly. No known constant. No prime sequence. No harmonic progression. No binary grid hidden in the waveform. When converted into sound, it became a low, patient knocking.

“It doesn’t encode a picture,” Anya said. “No hydrogen-line reference. No changing amplitude that maps to pixels. Nothing that says, ‘We understand physics too.’”

“Perhaps it isn’t intended for us.”

“Then why is it reaching this region?”

“Perhaps we aren’t the intended audience.”

Anya looked at her. “You want it to be somebody.”

Voss watched the waveform repeat. “I want it to be something.”

They worked for hours. Anya’s code separated the signal into nested intervals and searched for another layer beneath the obvious one. Voss listened to each variation with the old attention of a linguist: not only to the sound, but to the places where a speaker left room for another person.

In spoken language, silence was never empty. It could be punctuation, hesitation, invitation, refusal. A pause might mean *I have finished*, or *I am waiting for you*.

The spaces in the signal were too long to dismiss.

At 11:40, Marcus entered carrying a wrench.

“Why are the agricultural lamps at seventy percent?” he asked.

Anya kept working. “Because we’re using their power.”

“The plants are alive.”

“So are we.”

“Debatable.”

Voss turned. Marcus looked thinner than he had three days ago. A pale line marked his forehead where his helmet seal had rubbed him during the meteorite strike. He glanced at the displays.

“Still three, one, three?”

“Still three, one, three,” Anya said.

“Could be a broken beacon.”

“There are no beacons there.”

“Could be something nobody catalogued.”

“Then we’ve discovered an uncatalogued broken beacon.”

Voss tapped the screen. “The intervals contain variation.”

“Machines vary.”

“Not by less than a nanosecond.”

Marcus set the wrench down. “What do you want me to call it?”

“Not noise.”

“That’s not a name.”

“It’s a beginning.”

His expression tightened. “A name doesn’t make it intelligent.”

“No. Refusing to name it doesn’t make it unintelligent.”

He took Anya’s abandoned coffee and drank from it. “Don’t turn this into a sermon.”

“I’m not.”

“You’re a linguist. That’s what linguists do when the evidence runs out.”

The remark landed harder than it deserved. Voss turned back to the screen. The waveform continued, indifferent.

At 13:12, Anya found the anomaly.

She had been comparing the live feed with the first recording when the final short pulse arrived slightly early. Then the next cycle began.

Three short pulses. One long pulse. Two short pulses.

Silence.

Anya froze.

“Run it again,” Voss said.

“I am.”

The altered pattern repeated. After a pause nearly twice as long as usual, the original sequence returned.

Three. One. Three.

Marcus came in from the maintenance corridor, hands blackened with sealant. “What happened?”

Anya pulled up the raw feed, then the backup mast, then the thermal array. Every system showed the same change.

“Did we transmit?” Voss asked.

“No.”

“Any internal ping?”

“No.”

Marcus looked between them. “Then what changed?”

Anya’s fingers moved quickly. “Nothing on our end.”

The signal resumed its original rhythm. Voss felt her heartbeat in her throat.

“Mark the time.”

“It’s marked.”

“Compare the altered cycle to our acknowledgment test.”

“We haven’t sent one.”

Voss looked at Marcus.

He understood. “No.”

“It may be coincidence.”

“You said we weren’t transmitting until we understood more.”

“We don’t need to send a message. A single pulse. A controlled stimulus.”

“That is transmitting.”

“A ping.”

“A message.”

Voss watched the pattern repeat. It was not an answer. An answer required shared meaning, and they had none. But it was a response in the oldest sense: something had changed after their attention had changed.

“We don’t know that it heard us,” she said.

Anya turned. “You said nothing changed on our end.”

“I said we don’t know.”

The distinction sounded cautious, but inside her something had already opened.

Marcus looked at the power figures, then at the signal. “One ping. Minimal power. If anything destabilizes, we stop.”

Anya prepared the transmitter. Voss selected a pulse matching the shortest unit and checked the command twice before authorizing it.

The acknowledgment left the Meridian as a tiny expenditure of light.

For eight seconds, nothing happened.

Then the dead zone answered.

Three short pulses. One long pulse. Three short pulses.

A pause.

One short pulse.

The sequence appeared in blue across the console.

Anya covered her mouth. Marcus remained motionless, the wrench still in his hand.

Voss stared at the final pulse. It might have been noise. It might have been coincidence. It might have been a damaged machine completing a cycle without any awareness of them.

But the silence afterward felt different.

Not emptier.

Waiting.

Voss opened the archive file and typed:

POSSIBLE SYNTAX: address / duration / return / acknowledgment.

She hesitated, then added:

CANNOT TRANSLATE.

For the first time since Earth vanished, the statement did not feel like surrender.

## Chapter 4: The Echo Chamber

By the second day of listening, Anya had stopped sleeping.

She sat strapped into the sensor chair through the ship’s artificial night, the waveform suspended above her console in pale green: three short pulses, one long, three short. Beneath it ran a thin layer of static.

“There,” she said.

Voss stood behind her with two cups of reconstituted coffee. “There what?”

“The break.”

“It’s modulation.”

“It’s a voice.”

Marcus, crossing the operations bay with a coil of cable over one shoulder, paused. “A voice would be a remarkable achievement for a signal with no carrier structure.”

Anya enlarged the long pulse until it filled the display. Its central dip became a canyon of green light.

“Listen.”

She converted the signal to sound. A low knock repeated at measured intervals. Beneath it, the static rose and fell like breath.

Voss heard something inside it.

Mara—

She turned toward the speaker.

The sound collapsed into hiss.

Anya smiled without pleasure. “My mother.”

Marcus set down the cable. “Anya.”

“She used to say my name like that when she was angry. Not Anya. Mara. She thought it was funny that my parents had chosen a name no one in the family could pronounce properly.”

“Your mother was on Earth.”

“You don’t know where she is.”

“I know where Earth was.”

The word closed the room around them.

Anya opened another window, filling the display with spectral maps. “The signal carries the same phase irregularity as the event that erased Earth.”

Voss leaned closer. “You’ve matched them?”

“Not exactly. The edge distortion is incomplete, but the profile is there.”

“Or your filters are finding similarities because you’re looking for them.”

Anya’s eyes flashed. “You think I want this?”

“I think you’re exhausted.”

“I think it’s an echo. Every transmission that was swallowed. Every voice that didn’t get through.”

She looked at Voss. “Your archive preserves dead languages. You understand what an echo means. It proves the sound happened.”

Voss almost agreed. An echo proved a voice had existed. It preserved the outline of what was gone.

But the signal had no outline. It had intervals, repetitions, pauses. They were pouring themselves into those spaces.

“It may be a reflection,” Voss said. “We’re listening for Earth because Earth is what we lost.”

Anya turned back to the console. “You hear nothing, and that makes you an authority?”

“No. It makes me afraid of hearing too much.”

The next cycle began.

Three short pulses. One long. Three short.

The static gathered beneath them. Voss heard her father’s voice, low and patient, the way he had spoken while reading the news before the worst sentence. Marcus remained by the hatch, listening.

“Was that someone?” he asked.

Anya did not answer.

After that, the signal entered everything. The ventilation fans whispered names. Pipes clicked in almost grammatical rhythms. On the observation deck, the black region where Earth had been seemed to acquire a silver rim, a contour shifting with each pulse.

Voss knew it was starlight bent around an absence.

She also knew that once, for an instant, the contour had looked like a child’s hand pressed against glass.

Anya rerouted the feed through the archive processors, then the agricultural monitors, then navigation. Each transformation revealed another supposed layer: choral frequencies, face-like arrangements, a rising tone she called a distress signal.

She began transmitting personal messages through a maintenance channel.

“Mom,” Voss heard her say one night. “If you’re there, I’m here. I’m still here.”

She repeated it until the transmitter’s warning light turned amber.

On the third night, Marcus found her asleep upright, one hand on the console. The main power reserve had fallen twelve percent.

“Shut it down,” he said.

Anya woke. “Don’t.”

“You’ve drained reserve power.”

“I was isolating the phase layer.”

“You were draining the ship.”

“It answered me.”

Marcus reached past her and killed the terminal.

The waveform vanished.

For a moment, only the emergency lights moved.

Then Anya struck him.

The blow caught his cheek. He staggered, and the cable fell from his shoulder.

“You don’t get to turn her off,” Anya said.

Marcus pushed her away. “It isn’t her.”

Anya seized his suit and drove him into the console. Voss grabbed her from behind, but Anya twisted free and struck Marcus again. He caught her wrists. They slammed into the bulkhead, rattling the panels.

“Let go!”

“Stop!”

Marcus released her before they fell. Anya landed hard on one knee. The silence afterward was worse than the struggle.

She stared at the dead terminal. “She was trying to come through.”

Voss’s arms trembled. “No.”

“You heard her.”

“I heard what I wanted to hear.”

Anya looked up.

“I heard my father,” Voss said. “A sentence he never finished. But it wasn’t him. It was the shape of him—the part of me still waiting for the next word.”

Marcus wiped blood from his cheek.

Voss looked toward the backup monitor. The waveform still pulsed there, faintly green.

“It’s a mirror,” she said.

Anya’s face tightened.

“Not a message. Not yet. Maybe not ever. It gives us intervals, and we fill them with the dead.”

Language had always promised connection. A word crossed distance between minds. A name called someone back from absence. But language could also imprison its listener, turning pattern into person and silence into reply.

The signal offered no meaning. They supplied it.

A pulse sounded from the backup monitor.

For an instant, the static resembled a woman whispering Anya’s name.

Anya closed her eyes.

Marcus retrieved the cable. His voice was quiet. “We leave it behind.”

She shook her head. “There is nothing to return to.”

“Then we make a course.”

“You’ll kill us.”

“We’re already dying.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“No,” he said. “It’s the condition we’re in.”

The pulse came again: three short, one long, three short.

Marcus faced them both. “We shut down the feed, cancel the scan, and preserve what power we have. I won’t let this destroy what’s left of us.”

Anya stared at the signal. Her hand hovered over the console, close enough to touch it, but did not move.

Voss stood beside her, listening to the darkness knock.

“Leave it behind,” Marcus said. “Before it destroys us.”

Outside, the black wound where Earth had vanished held its silence, and the pattern went on answering nothing.

## Chapter 5: A Desperate Code

For six hours after the fight, the signal was absent from the *Meridian*.

Marcus had severed its feed from every system he could reach. The archive processors were dark, the agricultural monitors showed only sleeping plants, and the backup terminal had been removed from the observation deck. Without the pulse beneath the static, the ship seemed less haunted and more dead.

Anya sat strapped into the co-pilot’s chair, staring through the forward glass at the black region where Earth had been. Its edge remained impossibly sharp, bending starlight without reflecting any. She had not spoken since Marcus restrained her.

Voss found her there with two cups of warmed water.

“I don’t want tea,” Anya said.

“It isn’t tea. It’s water that has met a leaf.”

Anya accepted the cup.

The engineering hatch opened. Marcus emerged with grease on his knuckles and a diagnostic slate beneath one arm.

“The primary feed is isolated,” he said. “Power reserve is down another two percent. The starboard radiator is at forty-one percent. I can keep life support within the survival envelope for eleven days, perhaps twelve if we lower the temperature.”

“Then we have eleven days to work,” Voss said.

Marcus looked at her. “No. I canceled the scan.”

“You canceled the scan. You did not cancel our ability to think.”

He glanced toward the dead terminal. “We are done listening.”

“No,” Voss said. “We are done believing.”

Anya turned from the window. Her eyes were swollen, but her voice had steadied. “What are you proposing?”

“One final analysis. Finite and systematic. No personal messages. No searching for Earth’s echo. We test every framework we can justify—linguistic, mathematical, acoustic. If nothing survives scrutiny, we stop.”

“And then?” Marcus asked.

“Then we accept that it has no meaning.”

“Meaning won’t restart the radiator.”

“No. But uncertainty is already consuming us.”

Anya studied her. “You’ll stop looking for your father?”

“I will stop treating resemblance as evidence.”

“And if the pattern changes?”

“We record the change. We do not name it.”

Marcus set down his slate. “Every test gets a power budget. When reserve reaches critical, we stop. I won’t abandon you, but I won’t comfort false hope.”

“Agreed,” Anya said.

“Then work.”

They began with primes.

Anya restored the raw feed from cold storage and built a timing matrix across the wall display. They assigned values to pulse lengths, treated the long pulse as a delimiter, and mapped the intervals to prime numbers, gaps between primes, and modular residues.

The pattern remained itself.

Voss tested substitution systems drawn from human writing: binary alphabets, ternary grammars, syllabaries, logographic compression. In one model, the recurring three-one-three sequence became *come here*. In another, *I am waiting*. In a third, it was grammatical negation.

“Three meanings from the same evidence,” Anya said.

“Which means the evidence is insufficient.”

“Or the language is polysemous.”

“Every language is polysemous. That doesn’t make every noise a language.”

Voss deleted the translations.

They tried fractal analysis next. Anya rendered each pulse as a branching curve and searched for self-similar structures in the intervals. The display produced delicate, intricate shapes, but at higher resolution they dissolved into arbitrary variation.

“Fractals find order in almost anything,” Anya said.

“Like grief,” Voss replied.

Her hands went still.

“I’m sorry.”

“No,” Anya said. “That’s accurate.”

The music model lasted three hours. Pulse lengths became notes, pauses became rests, and the central dip in the long pulse became a change in timbre. Through the cabin speakers came a sparse descending figure: three low knocks, a sustained tone, three returning knocks.

It sounded like a door approached and left unanswered.

Marcus listened from engineering. “It’s music.”

“It resembles music,” Voss said.

“That distinction is getting expensive.”

Anya changed the scale. The figure became bright, mournful, almost tender. None of the versions predicted the next cycle. None supplied a stable harmonic center.

Yet when the original signal resumed, all three leaned toward it.

Near ship midnight, Anya found the sequence.

She had stopped searching for meaning and begun comparing the intervals with the astronomical reference tables. A cluster of ratios repeated every nineteen cycles, then every thirty-eight. Represented as angular separations, they formed three coordinates.

“Wait,” she said.

Voss came to her station. Marcus followed.

Anya overlaid the values on the star map. A point appeared twelve light-minutes away, beyond the relay boundary, in the dead zone from which the signal originated.

“There,” she whispered. “It’s not random.”

The coordinates sharpened into a volume bounded by three planes.

Marcus leaned closer. “What’s there?”

“Nothing.”

“Nothing is not a coordinate.”

Anya summoned every available survey. The volume contained no star, asteroid, relay, gravitational lens, or dust concentration. The nearest object was a broken communications buoy three light-years away.

Voss watched the point pulse. “Could it be displaced?”

“Earth?” Marcus asked.

Anya hesitated. “There’s no mass signature.”

“Could the anomaly be hiding it?”

“It could be anything,” she said. “That’s the problem.”

Voss copied the sequence into her archive and labeled it with a date, not an interpretation. Then she examined its position within the transmission. The coordinates did not occur at the beginning or end. They occupied the pause between repetitions.

Not a destination, she thought.

A question.

The idea did not resolve anything. It simply settled more firmly than the equations around it.

“What?” Marcus asked.

“I think we’ve treated it as a message.”

Anya looked at the empty coordinates. “It isn’t one?”

“Maybe not. Maybe it’s a question.”

“Questions have answers,” Marcus said.

“Not always. Sometimes a question is a hand extended in the dark.”

“That’s poetry.”

“Yes.”

He looked from her to the display, where the coordinates pointed nowhere. “What do you propose we answer with?”

Voss turned toward the forward glass. Beyond it, the vanished world left no light at all.

“Our existence,” she said. “If that is all we can prove.”

## Chapter 6: The Abyss Gazes Back

The signal began to fade at 04:12.

Not weaken. Fade.

Anya noticed first. She had been watching the waveform through the filter Marcus had allowed for twenty minutes when the three short pulses appeared less sharply than before. The long pulse followed, its central dip nearly swallowed by static. Then the silence opened.

“Run it again,” she said.

“I am,” Voss replied.

“Raw feed.”

Voss changed the display. The line became a crawl of interference. Three rises. One longer. Three again.

The final pulse was almost indistinguishable from noise.

Anya leaned toward the screen. “It’s moving.”

“There’s no Doppler shift,” Marcus said.

“Then it’s losing power.”

“Or our receiver is.”

“I checked the receiver.”

“Twelve minutes ago.”

“I checked it again.”

The next cycle arrived thirty-seven seconds late. Voss felt the delay as a physical unease. Until now, the signal had seemed composed, repeating with the patience of breath. Its withdrawal made the darkness outside feel suddenly larger.

Anya rose. “I’m reopening the transmitter.”

Marcus stepped between her and the access panel. “No.”

“Minimum amplitude.”

“No.”

“It may be responding to our absence.”

“It may be a dying instrument.”

“It changed after we pinged it.”

“It might have changed because the receiver changed.”

“You didn’t believe that yesterday.”

“Yesterday I believed we had one unexplained event. Today I believe we have a dying ship and two exhausted scientists trying to turn static into a promise.”

The words struck her. Anya went still.

“I wasn’t trying to make a promise,” she said.

“I know.”

“Yes,” she said. “You do.”

She moved past him. He caught her wrist, then released it when she looked down at his hand.

“I sent messages,” she said.

The compartment tightened around the confession.

Voss had suspected as much. During the echo chamber, before Marcus isolated the signal, several transmissions had been logged under calibration codes. Anya had called them diagnostic artifacts.

“How many?” Marcus asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t know?”

“I stopped counting.”

“What did you send?”

Anya gave a broken laugh. “To my mother. My brother. People whose numbers don’t exist anymore.”

She rubbed her palms together.

“I thought if the signal was carrying anything away, maybe it could carry something back. When I heard my mother’s name in there, I knew it was probably me. I knew. But I kept sending.”

Voss said, “What did you hope for?”

“That she would answer.”

The words barely carried.

“Mara’s last call cut out,” Anya continued. “She was telling me about the rain. Then nothing. No goodbye. Not even the end of a sentence. When I heard her voice in the signal, I knew it was grief. But grief doesn’t care what you know.”

Marcus turned toward the window.

Voss studied him. His silence was not judgment. It was recognition.

“What aren’t you telling us?” she asked.

His jaw tightened. “This isn’t about me.”

“It is if you’re deciding what hope is allowed.”

“Hope.” He gave a humorless laugh. “That’s what we’re calling it?”

“What would you call it?”

“Survival.”

“That’s what you measure in oxygen hours.”

“And what good is a beautiful name for the alternative?”

The ship shuddered. A relay clicked twice somewhere aft and failed. The lights dimmed.

Marcus closed his eyes.

“Seven years ago,” he said, “I was second officer on the *Pilgrim Dawn*.”

Anya turned.

“It was a colony ship. Four hundred and twelve passengers. We lost contact eighteen days past the boundary. No distress call. No debris. No drive signature.”

“What did they find?” Anya asked.

“Nothing. Three years later, a fragment of insulation. The inquiry called it enough.”

Voss felt the cold of the words.

“I was on the rescue vessel,” Marcus said. “We found nothing. That was the worst part. If there had been wreckage, grief would have had somewhere to go. Instead every family received forms and a sentence about probability.”

The weak signal pulsed behind them.

“Then Earth vanished,” he continued. “And I recognized it.”

No one answered.

Voss opened the linguistic archive. Thousands of entries waited in ordered silence: greetings, laments, names for water, names for the dead. She had believed language could preserve a people after the people were gone. But the three of them were surrounded by words, and none could make the universe answer.

What was the purpose of a linguist if language could not bridge the void?

“Maybe there isn’t one,” she said.

Anya looked at her.

“Maybe words only prove that frightened animals can put marks on darkness.”

“Then stop using them,” Marcus said.

She turned on him. “You think I can?”

“I think you should stop pretending they can save us.”

“You want me to stop pretending anything can.”

“We have eleven days.”

“Less, if you keep cutting power.”

“We have eleven days because I keep cutting power.”

“And then what? We die efficiently?”

“At least we don’t burn the ship chasing a machine that may not exist.”

“Maybe it does exist!”

“Then it has done nothing for us.”

Anya seized the console. “It answered us.”

“It produced another pattern.”

“You don’t know the difference.”

“Neither do you!”

She shoved him.

Marcus stumbled backward into the open observation-deck hatch. His safety tether was unclipped. One foot slid beyond the threshold, and his body turned toward the black window.

Voss caught his sleeve. Anya grabbed Voss around the ribs. Together they hauled him back.

Marcus struck the deck. Anya fell beside him. For several seconds, the three of them lay tangled in the dim light, breathing hard.

Then Voss heard it beneath the failing ship.

Three short pulses.

One long.

Three short.

Not her father. Not Mara. Not a sentence hidden inside the static. The pattern had no face and no name. It did not call to them.

It remained.

It repeated through the dark without urgency or demand. Its pauses were not empty. They were simply pauses, space left open.

Voss sat up.

“It’s not speaking,” she whispered.

Marcus stared at her. “What?”

“It isn’t a language.”

Anya wiped her face. “Then what is it?”

Voss listened to the silence after the final pulse.

“A pattern of patience.”

The signal repeated.

“We keep asking what it means,” she said. “But meaning doesn’t begin with translation. It begins with a choice.”

Marcus’s breathing steadied.

“What choice?”

Voss looked from him to Anya, then toward the window where the black wound swallowed the place Earth had occupied.

“What we want it to mean.”

Outside, the signal waited.

## Chapter 7: The Grammar of Hope

Voss woke to someone saying her name.

For a moment she lay rigid in her harness, listening for her father. Then Marcus spoke again from the hatch.

“Elara. Your hands.”

She had fallen asleep with them outside the thermal blanket. Her fingers were white at the joints.

He waited while she tucked them beneath her arms, then pushed a warm drinking pouch toward her. There was no revelation in it, only water with the faint metallic taste of the reclamation system. But he had heated it. Somewhere in his ledger of losses, he had allowed for that.

“Thank you,” she said.

He nodded and left.

She held the pouch against her palms. He had asked nothing about her hands; she had thanked him for more than water. Neither statement had been precise. Both had worked.

For days she had demanded that the signal yield a meaning independent of anyone who heard it. A word without a speaker. A bridge built from one bank alone.

She drank before the water cooled.

***

At the isolated receiver, she turned off the display.

Without the waveform there were no peaks to count, no central dip to enlarge until it resembled intention. Three short pulses. One long. Three short. Then room in which anything might happen.

She did not hear her father.

She did not hear a promise.

After several repetitions, she caught herself drawing breath in the pause.

That was all: her body making a place for an answer.

She opened her notebook, passed the pages of abandoned analyses, and wrote beneath the last entry:

*We cannot establish what it offers.*

She considered that, then added:

*We can decide what we offer.*

***

Anya came to the galley with a bruise darkening her wrist. Marcus was already there, fastening three food packets beneath the table straps. Since the accident, he clipped his tether before doing anything else.

Anya watched him do it.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

He kept one hand on the clip. “I know.”

“I could have killed you.”

“Yes.”

She lowered her eyes.

Marcus slid a packet toward her. “Sit down.”

Voss waited until they had begun eating. The food was cold and granular; each swallow required attention.

“I’ve stopped trying to translate it,” she said.

Anya’s spoon halted.

“The models?”

“I’m keeping the results. Including the failures. But I don’t think another grammar will tell us whether we should answer.”

Marcus leaned back. “You’ve already decided.”

“For myself.”

“Because you think it’s waiting?”

“Because I am.”

The ventilation fan rattled overhead. Anya pressed her spoon into the packet, making a small hollow.

Voss said, “Perhaps it’s a test. Perhaps it’s a broken machine. Perhaps it’s as indifferent to us as a star. I can’t tell you that it wants a relationship.”

“But you want one,” Marcus said.

“Yes.”

He looked away.

“I’ve been treating that as something shameful,” she continued. “As though wanting to be heard disqualifies us from speaking.”

“It can disqualify us from judging what we hear.”

“You’re right. We mustn’t call it rescue. Or Earth. Or an answer from someone we’ve lost.”

Anya set down her food.

Voss made herself continue. “But we don’t need to know who is listening to say who we are.”

“And if no one is?”

“Then no one is.”

Marcus rubbed his eyes. He looked older in the galley light, though she could no longer remember how old he had looked before.

“That’s an expensive distinction.”

“I know.”

“Do you? The transmitter reserve isn’t separate anymore. I can move power, but whatever goes out through the antenna won’t run the scrubbers.”

“How much time?” Anya asked.

“I’d have to calculate it. It depends on the length, the gain, what the relay will tolerate.”

“Then calculate it,” Voss said. “Before we agree. No hidden cost.”

Anya drew her bruised wrist into her sleeve.

“I kept thinking,” she said, “that if I found the right words, she would have to answer. Not because that made sense. Because she was my mother.”

Neither of them spoke.

“I don’t know how to stop waiting for her.”

“You don’t have to stop today,” Voss said. “But this message can’t be another way of calling her.”

Anya’s mouth trembled. She wiped it with the back of her hand, almost angrily.

“No,” she said. “It can’t.”

***

Marcus returned two hours later with a power estimate written on the back of a maintenance sheet.

They could make one directed broadcast using the remaining discretionary reserve and a portion of the emergency supply. There would be no second attempt. At the projected draw, they would surrender between thirty and forty hours of life support.

Voss read the figures twice.

“I won’t do it without both of you,” Marcus said.

Anya touched the sheet. “Nor will I.”

He looked toward the ceiling, listening to some change in the machinery they had not noticed.

“I wanted to keep us alive,” he said. “Then I wanted to make the end orderly. No panic. Nothing wasted.”

“You’ve kept us alive,” Voss said.

“For this long.” He pulled himself closer to the table. “I still want every hour. I need you to understand that.”

She nodded.

“But I don’t want order to be the only thing I manage.”

Anya reached across the table. This time she stopped short of touching him. Marcus put his hand beneath hers.

“Something true,” he said. “Not a distress call disguised as a prayer.”

Voss opened her notebook.

No coordinates presented as a destination. No claim to understand. No demand that the dark return their dead.

“We exist,” she said.

Anya took a slow breath. “We have loved people.”

Marcus glanced at their joined hands.

“And we’re afraid,” he said.

Voss had almost written otherwise. She crossed out the unfinished sentence.

“Yes,” she said. “But we choose to answer.”

She turned the notebook so all three could reach it.

## Chapter 8: The Last Word

The relay clicked beneath the deck every eleven seconds. On the twelfth, the lights dimmed.

Marcus lay beneath the open power console, his tether clipped to a handhold. Blue diagnostics shone across his knuckles.

“Relay three is gone.”

Anya looked up from the transmitter bench. “Recoverable?”

“Not this time.”

Voss stood between them with the notebook against her chest. Around their proposed message, they had crossed out sentences until the paper had nearly worn through.

“We could stop,” she said.

Marcus set down his tool. “Do you want to stop?”

She looked at the open console, the scorched insulation he had laid carefully aside.

“I want not to cost us anything.”

“That option’s gone.” His voice was gentle. “Whether we speak or don’t.”

He returned to the wiring. Behind him, the scrubbers labored through another uneven cycle. Their last estimate had depended on equipment continuing to work. Voss had begun to understand how little a number could promise.

“Code is ready,” Anya said.

“Show us.”

She turned the terminal. The program occupied less than a screen.

“The text, an encoding key, and repetitions with error correction. No audio. We can afford more words that way.”

“Will anyone know how to read it?” Voss asked.

“I don’t know. But the repetitions should help distinguish the message from noise.”

Anya’s finger hovered over the simulation command.

“Local test only,” she said.

Marcus nodded. The three watched the sequence pass through a model of the damaged transmitter. Once it returned intact, Anya ran it again with sections deliberately removed. Some words survived. Others became empty spaces.

She adjusted the repetition.

Voss sat beside her and opened the archive.

She could not send it all. Thousands of languages waited in those files, alongside recordings of people laughing between interviews, clearing their throats, asking whether the recorder was on. She had once imagined preservation as a matter of sufficient storage.

Now she selected a greeting.

Then a farewell.

She chose words spoken at births and funerals, a child’s expression of delight, a phrase addressed to someone who was suffering. She included the language keys she had, though she knew each explanation depended on other explanations they could not send.

Beside every fragment, she kept the speaker’s name.

Anya leaned closer. “Are we including our dead?”

Voss stopped scrolling.

“Mara,” Anya said. “Your father. The people Marcus searched for.”

“We can include them.”

“Not to call them.”

“No.”

Anya rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. “I still want my mother to know I kept looking.”

Voss waited.

“I know that isn’t what this transmission can do,” Anya said.

From beneath the console, Marcus answered, “You can want it anyway.”

He emerged holding a length of blackened cable. Anya reached for the tool he needed before he asked. For a moment they both held it.

“Thank you,” he said.

It was an ordinary exchange. Voss entered nothing in the notebook, but found herself remembering it.

They worked through the next watch without announcing its passage. Marcus brought the transmitter onto a separate route, protecting the scrubbers and thermal controls before allocating the reserve. He would not touch those last circuits.

“Thirty to forty hours,” he said when the calculation settled. “Still the best estimate. No second burst.”

Anya checked the figure herself, then passed the terminal to Voss.

Both gave their agreement again.

Afterward they shared the last of a packet of dried pears. Marcus cut it open along three sides so no pieces escaped. Anya divided them by size, then quietly put the largest in his hand.

“My brother used to count them,” she said. “Not weigh them. He thought that made it fair.”

“Was he younger?” Marcus asked.

“Older. That was his defense.”

Marcus smiled. Voss had forgotten the small crease beside his mouth.

She told them about her father refusing to leave messages because he disliked speaking to machines. He would call repeatedly instead, indignant that she had not answered.

“I used to let it ring,” she said. “Sometimes.”

Neither offered absolution. Anya moved closer until their shoulders touched.

When the food was gone, Voss laid the notebook between them.

*We exist.*

*We have loved people.*

*And we are afraid.*

*But we choose to answer.*

Marcus read it twice. “Keep afraid.”

“I will.”

Below the shared declaration, Voss placed the greetings and farewells, the fragments of joy and grief. Anya transferred them carefully, checking each against the page.

At the end, Voss wrote:

*We were here.*

Anya paused over the tense.

“For whoever finds it,” Voss said.

Anya entered the words.

The relay clicked once more, then stopped. Marcus checked the bypass. A green indicator steadied beneath his hand.

“Ready,” he said.

They gathered at the transmitter bench. Anya secured the message file and disabled the automatic retry. Marcus checked the power route one final time. Voss placed the notebook where all three could see it.

From the receiver came three short pulses.

One long.

Three short.

They waited through the familiar pause.

A tone began.

Anya lifted her head. It continued past the place where another pulse should have ended, steady and unbroken.

“No power fluctuation,” Marcus said.

Anya checked the isolated receiver. “It’s external.”

Voss heard no voice in it. She could not tell whether it meant anything at all.

The activation light glowed beneath her hand.

No one pressed it.

For one suspended moment, the *Meridian* seemed to hold its breath with the universe.

## Chapter 9: Transmission

The tone continued.

It did not come from the receiver’s speaker. Anya had disconnected that circuit herself. It seemed to inhabit the ship without passing through it, a narrow note beneath the ventilation fans and the ticking of cooling metal. The instruments offered no source and no explanation.

Voss kept her hand above the activation control.

“Still external,” Anya said. Her voice was rough with exhaustion. “No power draw on our systems. No modulation I can identify.”

Marcus stood beside the transmitter panel, one hand braced against the bulkhead. His tether ran from his harness to the deck ring.

“Could be a receiver fault,” he said.

“It isn’t.”

“You’ve said that before.”

“And I was right before.”

Marcus glanced at the red light beneath Voss’s hand. “We have one broadcast.”

“One,” Voss agreed.

The tone went on.

In the observation deck, the black region where Earth had been hung beyond the glass, bending the surrounding stars into a thin, distorted crown. No light came from within it. No signal came from Earth. The absence had become so complete that Voss sometimes felt the planet had never existed, that memory itself was an infection shared by three exhausted people.

Anya reached across the console and silenced the receiver.

The tone stopped.

For one second, nothing happened.

Then the silence deepened.

It felt like pressure entering Voss’s body, a vast closing of distance. The fans receded. Marcus’s breathing vanished. Even the faint vibration of the failing relay seemed to withdraw. Only the red activation light remained, along with the possibility that this, too, was an answer.

Anya stared at the blank display. “It stopped.”

Marcus looked at Voss.

She thought of all the messages they had refused to send: her father’s name spoken into a dead channel, Mara’s voice reconstructed from old recordings, the desperate phrases Anya had transmitted before Marcus shut down the unauthorized route.

Come back. Can you hear me? Please.

Their final message contained none of those demands.

Voss lowered her hand until her fingertip touched the control. “We aren’t asking it to return anything.”

“I know,” Marcus said.

“We aren’t saying we understand.”

“I know.”

“We’re only saying what’s true.”

Anya put her hand over Voss’s. Her palm was cold. “Do it.”

Voss pressed.

For an instant, the *Meridian* gathered itself around the act. Lights dimmed along the corridor. The transmitter’s cooling fans rose to a strained whine. Somewhere behind them, a relay snapped, and the ship’s remaining systems shifted onto their final protected route.

The broadcast began.

A pulse of energy left the vessel, too small to see and too immense to imagine. Anya’s code carried the words through layers of repetition and error correction, wrapped in a key that explained how to unwrap them. The greeting went first, then the farewell. Words for birth and burial, for sudden delight, for someone suffering. Their names followed.

Elara Voss.

Anya Chen.

Marcus Hale.

The fragments connected to the dead came after—not as summons, but as evidence. Mara. Voss’s father. The four hundred and twelve passengers of the *Pilgrim Dawn*, whose names had been reduced to probability tables and unanswered rooms.

We exist.

We have loved people.

And we are afraid.

But we choose to answer.

The transmitter consumed the reserved power in a long, uneven burn. Voss watched the progress indicator crawl forward. At thirty percent, her hands began to tremble. At fifty, Marcus moved behind her and steadied her shoulders.

“Sit,” he said.

“If I sit, I’ll fall asleep.”

“Then I’ll wake you.”

At ninety-nine percent, the final line waited.

Anya had argued against it—not the words, but the tense.

We were here.

Voss had told her that tense was not surrender. It was a bridge to whoever might find the message later, after the ship, after the signal, after the darkness had changed shape.

Now she touched the confirmation key.

The final line entered the stream.

The transmitter shut down.

No triumph followed. No flare of light, no voice from beyond the dead zone, no restoration of power. The ship simply became poorer.

The fans slowed. The temperature began to fall. Somewhere in the agricultural bay, the last permitted plant lamp went dark. On the observation deck, the stars sharpened as the glass cooled.

Anya released Voss’s hand and folded over the console.

“It’s gone,” she whispered.

Marcus checked the meters. “The broadcast is complete.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

Voss looked at the receiver. Its screen remained blank. The silence continued.

They waited.

Without the ship’s ordinary hum, time lost its edges. Marcus sat on the deck with his back against the wall. Anya rested her forehead on her crossed arms. Voss stood by the window, watching the black wound where home had been.

She did not know whether the message had reached anything. She did not know whether the signal was intelligence, mechanism, a wound in physics, or a pattern grief had taught them to see. She knew only that they had spoken without lying.

Then the receiver came alive.

Three pulses.

One.

Three.

But the interval had changed. Each pulse arrived more slowly, more gently, as if it were no longer knocking against the limits of perception. The central dip in the long pulse remained. The static around it had thinned.

Anya lifted her head.

“No,” she said softly. “It didn’t change.”

Marcus looked at her.

“The signal. It’s the same.” She watched the waveform with exhausted wonder. “We changed.”

Voss felt the truth settle inside her, not as a solution but as release. The pattern had waited through their suspicion, their projections, their arguments, their need to make it Earth or rescue or judgment. It had remained what it was. They had been the ones moving around it, inventing doors and finding only reflections.

The pulse came again.

Three. One. Three.

Slower now. Almost tender.

Marcus reached for Voss’s hand. His fingers were rough and warm despite the cold.

“I understood something,” he said.

She turned toward him.

He watched the signal beyond the glass. “Existence isn’t a problem to solve.”

Anya made a small, broken sound that might have been laughter.

Marcus tightened his grip. “It’s a voice to offer.”

The *Meridian* drifted onward, no longer correcting its course. Its remaining lights faded one by one, leaving them in the dim blue of emergency illumination. Voss leaned against Marcus. Anya slid down beside them until their shoulders touched.

Outside, the unknown waited without promise.

The signal pulsed.

They had already answered.

## Chapter 10: Beyond the Void

The cold arrived first.

It seeped through the *Meridian*’s walls, silvering the bolts and turning each breath into a brief cloud. The ship had lowered its temperature beyond any comfort protocol. Comfort belonged to the living future, Marcus had said once. They had no future left to budget for.

Now the lights dimmed by degrees.

The emergency strips still glowed along the floor, but their amber had faded to the color of old blood. Displays surrendered their brightness. Numbers floated on black glass, pale and detached, like the last stars seen through a narrowing sky.

Elara Voss sat between Marcus and Anya in the observation deck.

The window showed nothing recognizable. The stars remained, scattered around the sharp-edged absence where Earth had been. The black region did not shine or reflect. It was not even properly dark. Darkness was something the eye could understand. This was a refusal of understanding, a place where light went and returned as no answer.

Beyond it, the signal continued.

Three short pulses.

One long.

Three short.

Then silence.

The intervals had lengthened. The pattern arrived with the patience of something that had never needed to hurry. It might have been a beacon, a wound in the machinery of space, or nothing but recurrence mistaken for intention.

Elara no longer knew.

That uncertainty had become gentler than certainty.

“Your hands are cold,” Anya said.

Elara looked down. Anya had wrapped both of hers around them. Her fingers trembled, though she tried to hide it.

“So are yours.”

“I’m a sensor specialist. I’m not required to have warm hands.”

Marcus laughed quietly, then coughed. He pressed his sleeve to his mouth until it passed.

The life-support display reported another fraction lost: oxygen, scrubbing efficiency, thermal reserve. The figures were too small to inspire panic and too steady to permit denial. The ship was not dying dramatically. It was becoming less capable of sustaining them, one function at a time.

Elara thought of the words they had sent.

A greeting in Arabic. A farewell in Quechua. A blessing in Hebrew. A child’s delighted cry, preserved from an archive recorded centuries before any of them had been born. Words spoken beside cradles and graves, in kitchens, hospitals, battlefields, and bedrooms.

She had chosen language because it endured badly.

It changed shape, lost sounds, gathered mistakes. It carried the fingerprints of those who had used it even when its speakers were gone. Yet a greeting could cross a thousand years and still open its arms.

They had sent the names carefully.

Mara.

Elara’s father.

The four hundred and twelve passengers of the *Pilgrim Dawn*, each reduced by history to a number, then restored for a few seconds to the category of persons who had expected tomorrow.

They had sent the declaration last.

We exist.

We have loved people.

And we are afraid.

But we choose to answer.

Elara had wondered whether the message was too small. It contained no map, no demand, no proof. It did not explain Earth’s vanishing or identify the signal. It did not ask to be rescued.

It only told the truth.

“We don’t know if it was received,” Anya said.

The words seemed to have risen from Elara’s thoughts.

“No,” Elara said.

“We don’t know if anything heard us.”

“No.”

Anya nodded toward the dead receiver. “But we said it.”

“Yes.”

Marcus leaned his head against the wall. His eyes were closed. “That’s the part that counts.”

“To whom?” Elara asked.

He opened his eyes. The failing light made him look older than he had that morning, though there had been no morning, only another rotation of the ship and another reduction in what it could provide.

“To us,” he said.

The answer would once have disappointed her. She had spent her life among people who believed meaning required an audience. A word was a bridge, they said, but a bridge to nowhere was only architecture.

Now she understood that a bridge could be built before the other shore existed.

The signal pulsed again.

Three.

One.

Three.

Elara listened beneath the pattern, beyond the intervals and the central dip and the static rising around it like breath. She imagined a language with no nouns, only gestures. No verbs, only reaching. A grammar in which every sign meant: I am here, and I believe there may be someone there.

Perhaps that was what language had always been.

Not a container for meaning, but an act of faith in another mind.

The cabin lights dimmed once more. Anya shifted closer until her shoulder touched Elara’s. Marcus’s hand found theirs, clumsy with cold, and closed around both.

They were not survivors now. Survival implied a before and an after, a shore from which one had escaped. They were witnesses: three people who had seen the impossible, lost everything they could name, and chosen to speak anyway.

The receiver clicked.

For one instant, the sensor displays brightened.

Elara lifted her head.

The waveform appeared—not the familiar three pulses, but a sequence layered after them. Three short marks. One long. Three short.

Then, following a pause, a pattern of smaller intervals.

She could not read it.

The display flickered, and the sequence vanished.

“Did you see that?” Anya whispered.

Marcus stared at the screen. “I saw something.”

“Was it us?”

No one answered.

It might have been their transmission returning after crossing some impossible distance. It might have been a fault in the dying display. It might have been a new pattern, shaped by an intelligence that had listened, or by a universe indifferent to the difference between message and noise.

The signal resumed.

Three short pulses.

One long.

Three short.

Slower now. More distinct. Almost tender, though tenderness was another human word laid over an inhuman rhythm.

Elara closed her eyes.

She pictured Earth not as the black wound, but as a collection of voices: Mara calling Anya home; her father mispronouncing a word and laughing; children shouting in languages that had not yet learned they were temporary. She pictured Marcus among the lost passengers of the *Pilgrim Dawn*, keeping watch over people who would never know how long he had searched.

Then she pictured the three of them here, holding one another in the dark.

The ship breathed once, weakly.

Elara’s last thought was not a translation. It was not an answer.

It was a direction.

To answer the dark is to become a light, even if no one sees it.
