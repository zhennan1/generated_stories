Her brother was laughing when the recording stopped.

“So the inspector asks whose goat it is, and Aunt Sela says—”

Neri waited. The receiver’s progress bar had reached the end of the available data, but she kept her thumb off the controls, as though another second might arrive if she left room for it.

Around her, the cabin continued its small mechanical noises: circulation fans, a pump ticking behind the wall, the faint rattle of something loose in a storage net.

“Playback failure?” Imani asked.

Neri checked the buffer. “Transmission ended.”

So had the surface weather service. So had the port bulletin scrolling beneath it. Three separate streams had stopped within two seconds of one another. Their carrier indicators faded, leaving the receiver listening to its own noise.

Neri requested retransmission before remembering that the request would take four hours and twelve minutes to reach home.

An answer, if anyone sent one immediately, would require as long again.

She wrote the reception time beside her brother’s last packet, then its embedded transmission time: 03:14:08. Two times for the same unfinished sentence. The distinction had always been routine. Now the space between them seemed large enough to fall through.

Imani unlatched herself from the command couch.

“Bring up the optical record.”

The telescope had been taking navigation exposures throughout their survey run. At source time 03:11, the homeworld was a pale crescent, six pixels wide, exactly where the corrected ephemeris placed it.

At 03:16, it was not.

Neri enlarged the image until the pixels became squares. She reduced it again. The star shone in the guide-camera window; in the science image, with its glare masked, the prediction circle enclosed blackness.

“Check the pointing,” Imani said.

Lev matched the background stars. Neri checked the telescope clock against the receiver clock, then recalculated the light-travel correction. They widened the field and searched along the projected orbit. The crescent in the earlier image remained ordinary, almost offensively familiar.

Further exposures arrived. None showed the planet.

There was no flare in the stored frames, no expanding glow. Nothing resembling fragments rose above the telescope’s detection limit.

“It isn’t there,” Neri said.

Imani kept one hand closed around the overhead rail. “It wasn’t at those coordinates at the source times of these observations.”

“That’s what I said.”

“No. You said *isn’t*.”

Neri turned toward her.

Imani’s voice softened without becoming less exact. “We’re seeing light that left more than four hours ago. We don’t have observations of its present condition.”

“So it could be there now.”

“We don’t know. That uncertainty isn’t evidence that it is.”

Neri looked back at the empty circle. The correction felt necessary and unbearable.

Imani opened the incident register. Under classification, she selected COMMUNICATIONS EMERGENCY.

Neri watched her attach the interrupted transmissions, the telescope frames, their clock checks. In the description field, Imani entered: *Loss of all monitored surface channels. Planetary target absent from predicted position in subsequent optical observations. Cause undetermined.*

Communications emergency sounded like a damaged aerial, a storm over a relay station. It belonged to a world that still had maintenance crews.

“What else would you call it?” Imani asked, apparently seeing her expression.

Neri had no word she was willing to put in the register.

They did the inventory next.

Lev brought up the consumables ledger, then checked it against tank pressures and cartridge serial numbers. Neri read the physical labels while he entered them. Twice she lost her place and had to start a row again.

Oxygen was not the first limit. Carbon-dioxide removal was.

“Nineteen days,” Lev said. “For three people, at normal restricted-cabin activity. That includes the usable reserve, not the number printed on the cartridge cases.”

The fan stirred the short hairs against Neri’s forehead. She had never been so aware of air entering her nose, warming inside her, leaving changed.

“Less activity?”

“Some improvement. There’s uncertainty in the bed capacities, too. We should plan on nineteen.”

He did not round it upward.

The resupply carrier had still been in its surface cradle in the final port image, service lines attached. No departure beacon had arrived during its scheduled launch window. There was no vessel already en route whose course they could change, no cargo waiting beyond the missing world.

Lev removed the expected delivery from the ledger.

The air estimate remained nineteen days.

Propellant allowed attitude control and modest course corrections. It could not turn their survey cabin into a fast transfer ship. Lev tested the nearest stations anyway, entering their trajectories rather than their present positions. Each solution failed on fuel, time, or both.

“What if we stop the survey burns altogether?” Neri asked.

“We should,” he said. “It preserves options if something changes.”

“But nothing reachable?”

“Nothing we know about.”

He left the failed trajectories visible. Neri appreciated that he did not make her ask again.

They locked out the survey instruments and the nonessential heaters. Imani marked the circulation fans, scrubber controls, pressure monitoring, and thermal regulation as protected loads. None would be traded for more transmitter power without a fresh calculation.

Neri pointed to the receiver’s continuous low-power mode.

“That stays?”

Lev checked its draw against the remaining generation and storage margins. “Yes. Listening is affordable. The high-gain transmission bursts need a schedule, but this can stay on.”

She waited for Imani’s confirmation.

“Keep it running.”

Only then did Neri release the edge of the console. Her fingertips hurt where she had been pressing them into the rim.

Imani restored the duty roster.

“Filter inspection at nine. Thermal loop afterward. Communications checks on the hour.”

Neri almost objected. It seemed indecent to assign a filter inspection beneath that empty circle.

Then the pump behind the wall missed a beat, caught again, and she understood enough to keep quiet.

They ate because eating was on the schedule. Lev clipped his opened pouch to a restraint and forgot it until Imani pointed. Neri swallowed three mouthfuls without tasting them. A bead of sauce escaped her spoon; she caught it with a cloth before it reached the ventilation grille.

The next communications check was eleven minutes away when she returned to her station.

She put on the headphones and opened her brother’s recording. Behind his voice she could hear street traffic, then someone nearby calling his name. There had been wind where he stood. She had missed that on the first playback.

“So the inspector asks whose goat it is, and Aunt Sela says—”

Neri knew how her aunt lifted her chin before answering a foolish question. She could supply the gesture perfectly. Not the words.

She checked that the receiver was still listening, then moved the recording back to its beginning.

At the next scheduled receiver check, Neri closed her brother’s recording and opened the live trace.

The low-power channel showed the usual spread of noise. She checked the gain, the receiver temperature, and the time against the roster. Nothing had changed since the previous check. The cabin’s fans whispered through the ducts; behind the wall, a pump clicked once, paused, and resumed. She tried not to hear the pause as a question.

On the second sweep, a thin mark rose from the noise floor. It faded, then appeared again at nearly the same interval.

Neri leaned toward the display. The feature was faint enough that a change in gain might have made it vanish into the background. Its regularity was more troubling. Instruments could repeat their mistakes as faithfully as they repeated their measurements.

She saved the trace and started another sweep. The mark returned. A fourth appearance followed, nearly identical to the third.

“Lev,” she called. “Can you check something?”

He came over from the stores inventory, carrying a slate. Neri showed him the sequence without explaining what she thought it meant.

“How many samples?” he asked.

“Four in this run. I saw two during the last check.”

“Same settings?”

“Same gain and sweep length. Receiver temperature is within range.”

He set down the slate and examined the timing marks. His expression narrowed, not with excitement but with concentration. “It repeats.”

“That’s what I saw.”

“It could still be the receiver.”

“I know.”

Imani joined them and looked first at the instrument status, then at the display. No alarms showed. She opened the communications log.

“Record it as an unresolved receiver fault,” she said. “Include the settings and time.”

Neri hesitated. “A fault makes it sound as if we know it’s coming from the receiver.”

“It means the receiver has produced a result we can’t explain,” Imani said. “We can change the category when we have evidence.”

Neri entered the description: recurring narrowband feature, weak, origin undetermined. She added the sweep settings and temperature. Then she saved the raw samples separately from the processed trace.

“If we only keep the filtered version, we’ll lose what the receiver actually gave us,” she said.

“Keep both,” Lev replied. “And don’t let the display interpolate between samples.”

She checked the export settings and copied the raw files to the independent storage volume. The checksum matched. The act steadied her more than it should have. The samples were small, but at least they would remain what they were, not what anyone later remembered seeing.

Lev ran the built-in diagnostics. Calibration pulses came through with their familiar clean edges. The timing check produced a short sequence of regular ticks; the noise test laid a rough, uneven band across the display. None resembled the faint feature.

He repeated the tests at a different gain. The mark remained weak, its outline shifting slightly as the noise changed.

“Not one of the known diagnostic patterns,” he said.

“Could it be an unfamiliar one?” Neri asked.

“Could be. The diagnostics only rule out the patterns they contain.”

“Interference?”

“Also possible. We can compare channels, but that won’t tell us where it started.”

Imani checked the power schedule. “The continuous listening mode stays on. Its draw is already accounted for. We can collect samples during the routine windows, but no extra transmitter bursts or unscheduled tests.”

Neri glanced at the faint trace. The receiver was using little power; listening did not threaten the protected loads. But wanting more evidence did not make every test affordable. “Understood.”

Imani returned to the roster. “We’ll check again on the next window.”

“At home,” she said, “we would notify the network and wait for a response.”

The word stayed with Neri. “There isn’t a network to notify.”

“The procedures still use the station’s reference point,” Imani said. “Home is where the emergency chain leads.”

Lev looked from the empty channel list to the receiver trace. “Then what does return mean? A route to a destination, or an instruction written for a destination that isn’t reachable?”

Imani’s hand paused over the roster. “The procedures have to be usable before we know what the emergency is.”

“I understand why the word is there,” Lev said. “I don’t know what it points to now.”

Neri watched the feature rise and disappear again. For Imani, home was a coordinate in a chain of decisions. For Lev, return required somewhere they could actually go. Neri thought of her brother’s voice, cut short by the end of the recording. Both words had once meant a place where she could expect to hear him finish speaking.

“Can we leave room for contact?” she asked. “In the log. We know there’s a repeated feature. We don’t know that nothing can answer.”

“Possible isn’t likely,” Imani said.

“I didn’t say likely.”

“And it isn’t the same as current,” Lev added. “We have no source time. It could have been emitted long before we received it.”

Neri felt the limits of the evidence pressing against the wish she had not meant to reveal. “Then say what we know, and leave the rest undecided.”

Imani considered this, then nodded. “Record the recurrence and the uncertainty. No claim about contact.”

Neri amended the entry: structure and recurrence observed across repeated receiver checks; origin, meaning, and source time unknown. She saved the revision alongside the raw files. The words looked neat on the screen. They did not feel neat inside her.

The next scheduled task was inspection of the cabin filter. Neri put the receiver back into low-power listening mode and left the samples queued for the following check. The signal could be a receiver artifact, interference, or something beyond their tests. Nothing justified changing the schedule, and nothing justified pretending they had identified it.

Later, after Lev had gone to sleep and Imani was in the equipment bay, Neri put on her headphones. She opened her brother’s recording and lowered the volume. Traffic moved behind his voice. Wind brushed the microphone. Someone called his name; he laughed, then continued.

“So the inspector asks whose goat it is, and Aunt Sela says—”

Neri could picture her aunt lifting her chin before answering. She could hear where her brother meant to pause. But the words after that were missing, and memory would not supply them.

She stopped the recording and returned to the receiver data. The last sample showed the same narrow feature at the same faint level. She checked its timestamp and added it to the log.

The structure held. The recurrence held. Its origin, meaning, and source time remained unknown.

Neri began with the least dangerous explanation.

She laid the Aster’s calibration records beside the receiver samples. The carrier was a narrow tone used to measure drift in the communications system. A fraction could leak through damaged shielding and enter the receiver chain, appearing to arrive from outside.

The recurring feature was faint, its edges softened by noise. At this resolution, the resemblance was enough to investigate.

“We should test feedback first,” she said.

Lev looked up from the power ledger. “How?”

“A brief calibration burst. Record what reaches the receiver, then compare it with the feature.”

“And the cost?”

“Point-three units of discretionary power. The ceiling would be point-four.”

He studied the figures. “That allowance covers problems we haven’t predicted.”

“This could be one of them.”

“Or it could be something harmless we spend power trying to name.”

Neri understood the objection. Since the planet’s absence, every expenditure carried the weight of needs they could not yet foresee. But an untested fault would contaminate every later interpretation.

“I’m not proposing contact,” she said. “I’m proposing one bounded test.”

Imani stood beside the equipment rack. In the night lighting, the shadows beneath her eyes looked bruised.

“Set the limits now,” she said. “And log a failure as carefully as a result.”

They agreed on a two-second burst, automatic overload protection enabled, and physical isolation of the transmitter afterward. Lev would ready his independent backup receiver during the recovery period.

Neri entered the authorization.

For a moment, her finger rested above confirmation. She had not called the signal a voice. Still, some part of her had begun imagining someone waiting beyond the dark. An internal fault would end that possibility without requiring her to admit she had entertained it.

She pressed the control.

A low vibration passed through the deck. The transmitter indicator rose within its expected range. Then the receiver display whitened.

“Overload,” Lev said.

The burst cut off automatically. A warning tone sounded, and the primary input closed behind a thermal lockout.

Neri opened the diagnostic panel. The transmitter output had remained within limits, but leakage through the shared front end had exceeded the receiver’s tolerance.

“Recoverable,” she said.

“Did we get a comparison?” Imani asked.

Neri examined the saturated trace. “No.”

The lockout timer began counting down. Behind its red warning, the receiver showed neither signal nor background noise.

Lev recorded the expenditure. “Point-three-eight units.”

“Within the ceiling.”

“Yes.”

His agreement offered no comfort.

They isolated the transmitter at its hardware disconnect. Lev checked the open circuit before moving to the backup receiver. Meanwhile, the primary unit’s temperature fell more slowly than its initial estimate predicted. The timer extended itself.

The next expected recurrence passed while the main receiver remained blind and Lev was still bringing the backup into a stable listening state.

He marked the missed interval in the log.

“We don’t know it would have appeared,” Neri said.

“We know we couldn’t have seen it.”

She looked away. Ventilation whispered through the cabin filter, the next inspection on their schedule. Ordinary work had become strangely difficult to face when a few lines on a screen could consume all her attention.

Lev’s backup was an older instrument on a separate power line, with its own antenna near the emergency navigation array. Its display gathered more noise than the primary receiver, but it shared neither the overloaded front end nor the calibration injection path.

He adjusted the gain, then took his hands off the controls.

Neri stood behind him. “How long until the next interval?”

“Twenty seconds.”

Imani watched the clock. No one spoke.

Noise crawled across the display. Then a narrow feature rose through it: the same short structure, the same pause, the same longer descent.

Lev leaned forward. He checked the timestamp, the antenna connection, and the raw trace before saving the capture.

“It’s there.”

Neri felt relief arrive before she could decide what it meant.

“The transmitter?” she asked.

“Still isolated.”

“Residual emission?”

“Not from that circuit. And this receiver doesn’t share the path we were testing.”

She studied the faint line. It had survived the removal of her explanation. That was all. The relief in her chest embarrassed her.

“An external recording could do this,” she said.

“Or an automatic beacon,” Imani added.

Lev nodded. “Neither needs anyone listening now.”

Nor did either establish when the transmission had begun its journey. It could have left its source before the planet disappeared, carrying an orderly repetition through events it knew nothing about.

Neri opened the incident log.

“Independent recurrence detected during transmitter isolation and primary receiver cooldown,” she dictated. “The proposed onboard-feedback path is ruled out. Origin, meaning, and source time remain unknown. No evidence yet of an active correspondent.”

“Include the blind interval,” Lev said.

She did.

“And the power.”

“I haven’t forgotten.”

“An interpretation has already cost us.”

“The test eliminated something.”

“At a measurable price.”

Imani pulled a restraint strap around the loose diagnostic case. “Then make the next price explicit before we pay it.”

When the primary receiver reached operating temperature, they restored it at low gain and checked its noise floor before returning to observation settings. No damage appeared in the diagnostics.

They agreed to scheduled listening windows, preservation of raw recordings, and no unscheduled transmissions. Any further burst would require all three approvals and a stated deduction from the remaining discretionary allowance.

Neri accepted each condition. Lev entered them without looking at her.

The main display settled into its familiar gray scatter. For now, the feature was absent.

She saved the amended log. They had ruled out one fault, lost one interval, and spent point-three-eight units. None of those facts canceled the others.

Across the cabin, Lev closed the ledger.

They resumed listening, but not together. The Aster continued through the dark, carrying its reserves and their disagreement over what uncertainty was worth.

For two days, they divided time into what the Aster could afford.

Listening windows followed battery checks. Analysis ran while the processor would otherwise have been idle. Meals came in sealed pouches warmed against equipment housings; nobody authorized the galley heater. The cabin smelled faintly of insulation, stale coffee, and the mineral dampness of three people breathing the same reclaimed air.

Neri marked the signal’s pulses in three columns: duration, count, interval.

At first the relationships seemed almost too clean. One cluster contained twice as many pulses as the preceding cluster. Two short intervals together approached the length of a third. But the faintest pulses vanished beneath the noise, and a missing mark could turn a simple ratio into an invention.

She left those entries blank.

Lev noticed. He said nothing, but stopped checking every number she entered.

They authorized two brief transmissions, separately, with a silent observation window between them. Before each, all three confirmed the deduction from the discretionary allowance. Lev kept the transmitter below the level that had caused the thermal lockout.

The first outgoing pattern was deliberately simple: three equal pulses, a pause, three more.

Eleven minutes later, the received sequence changed.

Neri felt herself lean toward the display.

“Association,” she said, before anyone could speak. “Not response.”

Imani recorded the distinction.

The second test produced no comparable change. During the following silent window, the earlier variation returned without any transmission from the Aster.

Lev brought the traces together.

“If it’s answering,” he said, “we don’t know which question.”

“Or how long it takes the question to get there,” Imani said.

“Or whether there’s a question at all,” Neri added.

Saying it cost her something. She disliked that it did.

By the second evening, the cabin lights had dimmed to their sleep-cycle amber, though none of them had gone to their bunks. Beyond the forward glass, stars remained fixed against a darkness that offered no scale. Home had once occupied a place among those coordinates. Neri could still look toward it without meaning to.

The new sequence arrived near the end of a listening window.

Three parts: a compressed cluster, a longer separation, then two descending groups distinguished by their pulse spacing.

She knew it before she knew why.

Her hands went cold.

“Wait,” she said.

Lev froze the display.

Neri tapped the three sections. In memory she heard the old household receiver interrupting an evening broadcast: a bright chime, a pause, then the relay tone that preceded civil notices. Storm routes. Coastal evacuations. Instructions delivered in voices trained never to hurry.

“That’s the emergency delimiter.”

Imani turned toward her.

“Which system?”

“The old continental relay. Before they consolidated the bands.” Neri enlarged the trace. “They used it to bracket status traffic.”

The remembered sound carried a kitchen with it: warm tiles, her father opening a window, rain ticking against the sill. For an instant the cabin seemed less real.

“It may be an all-clear,” she said. “From survivors.”

Neither of the others answered.

Then Imani released a breath so slowly that Neri realized she had been holding it. Lev’s hand lifted from the ledger.

That small movement should have warned her. Instead, it made the possibility feel shared, and therefore stronger.

“Show me the reference,” Lev said.

Neri opened the archive.

The search took nine minutes. Later, she would remember how long those minutes had allowed them to believe.

Lev found the first match in a routine transport advisory recorded seventeen months before the disappearance. He found another in a scheduled relay test, then a third preceding agricultural weather data.

He played them without commentary.

Through the cabin speaker came a dry succession of tones. Familiar. Ordinary.

Neri stared at the dates.

“It framed emergency traffic too,” she said.

“Yes,” Lev replied. “But it didn’t mean all-clear.”

“No.”

“And we haven’t established that this is the same encoding.”

“No.”

Imani rested her fingertips against the edge of the console.

“Even if it is from home,” she said, “that doesn’t tell us when it left. It could have been traveling for hours before we received it. We can’t place it after the disappearance.”

The cabin’s ventilation clicked into a higher cycle. Neri heard every faint rattle in the grille.

She had not lied. That defense occurred to her, complete and useless. She had said *may*. She had left herself a grammatical exit while offering the others something much larger.

“I recognized a resemblance,” she said. “I made it into evidence of survival.”

Lev looked down.

“I wanted it to be that,” she added.

She opened the log before she could soften the correction.

“Three-part structure retained as an observation. Similarity to archived relay delimiters noted, but nonexclusive. No demonstrated status meaning, homeworld origin, or post-disappearance transmission time. Survival interpretation withdrawn.”

Her voice remained steady until the final word.

Imani saved the archive references beside the entry. Lev returned his hand to the ledger. Neither action restored what had existed nine minutes earlier.

“We cannot keep spending discretionary power on hope,” Lev said.

“No transmission was made because of this.”

“This time.”

Neri turned toward him. “Then say what you want. Silence unless I can guarantee who’s listening?”

“I want the cost stated before wanting something makes it look cheap.”

“I stated the costs.”

“You stated an all-clear.”

The words struck harder because he did not raise his voice.

Neri gripped the console edge. “If every uncertain thing is a reason not to speak, we’ll preserve enough power to hear nothing until we die.”

Lev’s expression changed.

For the first time, she saw exhaustion rather than resistance.

“I count it,” he said quietly, “because it’s something we still have.”

Imani pushed away from the console.

“Current evidence remains insufficient to determine—”

She stopped.

Neri watched her swallow.

“I don’t know,” Imani said. “I don’t know what happened after the last photons we saw. I keep writing it properly, and it still means that.”

No one offered her a better sentence.

The next listening window began in six minutes. Lev checked the receiver temperature. Neri cleared the analysis workspace, leaving the uncertain marks visible. Imani opened a fresh recording file.

When the window arrived, they took their stations.

Nothing between them had been repaired. Still, Lev read out the settings, and Neri checked them. Imani started the clock.

They listened without calling the silence an answer.

By the sixth day, Neri could recognize the ship’s cooling cycle through the soles of her feet. The vibration climbed from the deck, held for eleven seconds, then faded. In the pause that followed, she heard Lev turning pages in the emergency ledger.

He had begun using paper when the display dimmed between entries. Neri had mistaken the choice for an accusation. Now she watched him hold a page flat with two fingers and wondered how much concentration that small task required.

The listening window opened.

Lev read out the receiver temperature and gain. Neri checked both against her console. Imani started the recording and spoke the shipboard timestamp. Beyond the forward glass, stars occupied positions so familiar that Neri sometimes expected one to move out of pity.

The signal arrived nineteen minutes later.

Its narrow pulses crossed the display, carrying the same measured separations they had examined until even their disagreements felt rehearsed. Neri kept her hands still until the final interval had passed.

“Raw capture saved,” she said.

“And the processed trace,” Lev said.

She saved that too.

He waited until Imani marked the signal’s end.

“We can ask it something we haven’t asked before.”

Neri looked at him. “You mean transmit.”

“Yes.”

Yesterday, she would have heard surrender in that word. Today she was wary of how much she wanted to.

“What would distinguish an answer?” Imani asked.

“Fresh information,” Lev said. “Something generated after this arrival. If a later transmission contains it, in a separate reply structure, a fixed recording cannot explain that.”

“An echo could.”

“Which is why we test our own paths first.”

He pulled a maintenance diagram onto the dim display. His fingertip followed the transmitter branch, then stopped at the receiver junction.

“We disconnect reception during transmission. Physically. No shared signal path through the console. The receiver records again only after the transmitter is off and its antenna branch is open.”

“That excludes direct leakage,” Neri said. “It doesn’t exclude a buffer playing back what we sent.”

“We clear the buffers, disable automatic retransmission, and check with an unconnected input. We keep the backup receiver independent.”

Imani drew a line across her page.

“And an external relay could return the pattern without understanding it.”

“Yes,” Lev said. “We’d have evidence that something outside this ship received and returned fresh information. That’s the question we can afford to ask.”

He opened the power ledger.

The figures had acquired a physical ugliness for Neri: heater cycles, circulation pumps, the stubborn demand of warm bodies. Lev laid the updated tank readings beside the battery estimate and checked the cartridge serial numbers against yesterday’s inventory.

“The remaining discretionary margin is six point four percent of full battery capacity,” he said. “After life-support minima, propulsion contingency, and the protected reserve.”

He showed them the calculation twice, once by subsystem and once by duration.

“The complete exchange comes to approximately one point eight percent of capacity. That includes transmitter operation, switching losses, and the additional processing, with a small allowance for variation. It covers the challenge and the sustained follow-up we’ve discussed.”

“Listening?” Imani asked.

“Routine receiver draw stays in its existing budget. I haven’t counted it twice. Essential life support is untouched.”

Neri subtracted before he spoke again.

“Four point six remains discretionary,” she said.

“If actual draw stays within estimate. There’s a hard cutoff.”

Imani studied the numbers. A loose thread trembled at her cuff in the ventilation stream.

“This gives us no promise of rescue.”

Lev nodded. “I accept that.”

“Neri.”

She thought of the withdrawn entry, of how briefly she had allowed an uncertain sequence to become news from home. Her cheeks warmed despite the cabin chill.

“I accept it. Even a correct answer wouldn’t tell us who sent it, where they are, or whether they can help.”

“I accept the same,” Imani said. She entered the authorization beneath the calculation. “Proceed within the limit.”

Lev spent the next hour making the sequence difficult to perform incorrectly.

He labeled the switches with strips torn from a packaging sleeve. Reception isolated. Transmission enabled. Transmission disabled. Reception restored. Each transition required another person to read back the state. With the transmitter connected to a dummy load and the receiver input disconnected, they looked for leakage at several gain settings.

The raw trace stayed at its ordinary noise floor.

Only then did Neri generate the challenge.

She took a new stretch of live receiver noise, preserved the untouched samples, and extracted a balanced pulse sequence using the rule they had agreed before opening the file. She checked it for bias and saved the extraction steps beside it. The sequence had not existed when the latest signal arrived.

It looked disappointingly plain.

She framed it with the repeated boundaries and intervals that had survived their earlier tests. Those conventions remained tentative; she resisted adjusting them to make the packet resemble speech.

Imani recorded the generation time.

“Observation,” she said. “New challenge derived from live noise after incoming signal ended.”

Her fingers moved to a separate field.

“Hypothesis: an external responsive process may return recognizable information from this challenge.”

Neri watched the distinction settle onto the screen. It hurt less when the words were accurate from the beginning.

At the transmit station, Lev read the first switch state.

Neri confirmed the physical break in the receive path. Imani confirmed the timestamp.

The transmitter came alive with a low vibration through the console. On the power display, the draw rose to the predicted level. Neri followed the outgoing pattern without counting the pulses aloud. Each one carried away a quantity Lev had made visible and all three had agreed to spend.

When the packet ended, Lev disabled transmission. They verified the open branch, cleared the local buffers, and restored reception in order.

The independent receiver showed only background noise.

Imani recorded that too.

For several minutes, nobody left their station. The earliest comparison worth making lay beyond the delay they had allowed for, yet Neri kept glancing at the trace.

Lev closed the ledger without putting it away.

In the quiet between cooling cycles, Imani set the wait timer. Neri rested her hands beside the controls, and this time managed to leave them there.

The timer carried them through the last hours of day six and into day seven. Their sleep came in short shifts, interrupted by the receiver’s faint hiss and the regular clicks of the ventilation relays. Neri woke once with her hand already reaching for the console. She stopped before touching it. The trace on the screen was only noise; the room around her held its stale warmth, the air dry against her lips.

Before the signal could arrive, they needed to agree on what would count.

“If we decide after we see it, we’ll find whatever we’re hoping for,” Neri said. She spoke carefully, as if each word had to pass an inspection. The earlier mistake had left a thin reserve of caution between them. Not distrust exactly, but the awareness that certainty could be made from longing.

They set the criterion together. A reply had to preserve the newly generated pulse intervals, not merely resemble their number or rhythm. The pattern had to sit inside the boundaries and pauses they had tentatively identified as a reply structure. It had to arrive after the challenge had gone out, not within the period in which a local echo could plausibly return. Anything less would be suggestive, not an answer.

Imani entered the rule in the log with the time. She separated it from the later observation by opening a new field and locking the first one. “Criterion recorded before reception,” she said. “No revisions after the result.”

Neri kept the original challenge file open beside the raw receiver samples. She checked its extraction steps and matched its checksum to the copy preserved before transmission. The outgoing trace was still there, narrow and clean on the display. She had generated it from live receiver noise after the previous signal ended; no earlier source could have supplied that particular arrangement. She did not say what that meant. Not yet.

The first incoming rise came while Lev was checking the transmitter’s disabled state. He turned at the receiver’s alert, and all three went still. The signal unfolded in the familiar, spaced groups: boundary, interval, boundary. For a moment Neri saw only shape. She waited for the recording to finish before asking the system to extract anything.

Then she compared the returned sequence against the preserved challenge. The pulses matched in order and spacing. She ran the comparison again from the raw samples, with the same result. A second pass, using a wider tolerance for timing drift, changed nothing.

“It’s there,” she said. Her voice had gone quiet. “Inside the reply structure.”

Lev did not take the result on trust. He checked the switching log, then the transmitter status and the receiver’s buffer history. The transmit path had been isolated during the challenge, as planned. The local buffers had been cleared before reception began. The transmitter had remained disabled. He checked the physical branch again, though nothing in the records suggested it had changed.

“I can’t find a shipboard route for the pattern to come back through,” he said. “The sequence wasn’t present in the equipment before we sent it.”

Imani wrote that down as an equipment finding, not a conclusion. She recorded the matching intervals, the timestamps, and Lev’s checks. Then she added what the result did not establish: no proof of a living correspondent; no location; no intentions; no evidence that the source understood anything about them beyond the challenge it had received and returned.

Neri read the list without looking away from the screen. “Last time I called a resemblance an answer,” she said. “I made the rest of you argue me back from it.”

“You made a claim,” Imani said. “We let the claim travel too far before we stopped it.” She paused, then entered that sentence in the notes too, in the section for their own errors.

Lev leaned against the console, shoulders drawn tight from the night’s checks. “This is different. It’s evidence of an external process receiving and answering a challenge. That matters. It doesn’t tell us what the process is.”

Neri nodded. The relief in her came mixed with a smaller, sharper feeling: the answer had not opened a door so much as marked a boundary they could now see. Whatever was out there had returned their unpredictable pattern. It might be a machine, a living thing, a system they had no language to describe. It might not know where they were, or that they were afraid. They had no grounds to call it rescue.

The power display showed the exchange still within the amount they had authorized. The receiver’s routine draw had not changed; life support had not been touched. Lev reviewed the remaining margin, then closed the ledger. No one proposed another test. The answer they had risked power to obtain was enough to make the next choice theirs.

“What could we send that is actually ours?” Imani asked.

They spoke first of facts: their names, their position, the condition of the ship. Each seemed useful, and each depended on assumptions about what the other process could parse. Then Lev remembered a meal from home, one he had described once during a watch: a crowded table after a long journey, dishes passed hand to hand, too many people talking at once. The memory brought a faint smile to his face before he could restrain it.

Not that meal, Neri said. She remembered the one her family shared when a neighbor came by with fresh bread, and everyone stayed long after the plates were empty. Imani remembered the sound of people arriving before the food was ready. The details differed, but the shape of the experience was familiar to them all: people gathered, food prepared and passed around, the meal lasting because nobody was in a hurry to leave.

They would send that shape, not claim that it could carry taste or warmth intact. They could mark a beginning, a gathering, the shared meal, and its continuation beyond the food itself. Their boundaries and intervals were still tentative conventions; they had no reason to believe those signs meant what they wanted them to mean. But the experience was particular enough to choose, and true enough to offer.

Imani opened a new entry and asked each of them to say what they agreed to communicate. Neri watched the words appear beneath the untouched receiver trace.

“A meal shared with others at home,” she said.

Lev and Imani agreed. They had not decided how it would be understood. For now, they had decided only what they wished to send.

The words about the meal remained on Imani’s screen while Lev reopened the receiver logs. The ship’s circulation fan scraped at the same point in every rotation. Neri had begun to anticipate it, tightening her jaw just before the sound, as though anticipation could make anything aboard less likely to fail.

Lev aligned the independent recordings, correcting for the clocks’ measured offset. Then he checked the interval between the fresh challenge and the returning pattern.

“Well?” Neri asked.

“It fits a delayed exchange outside the ship.” He kept his hand on the controls. “The answer wasn’t in the earlier buffer. Both receivers recorded the arrival. That doesn’t tell us who—or what—answered.”

“But something answered.”

“Something responded to a pattern we had just made. We can claim that now.”

The qualification should have frustrated her. Instead, its precision steadied her. They had gained something that did not require her enthusiasm to exist.

“I owe you an apology,” she said. “The resemblance to an emergency relay wasn’t evidence. I wanted it to be familiar.”

Lev looked at her then.

“I know.”

“You were right to challenge it.”

“And the fresh-pattern test changed the claim.” He closed the comparison window, leaving the raw records intact. “Not all my objections. That one.”

Imani drew a line beneath the meal entry. “Then let’s keep the claims separate.”

They worked within the power allowance already entered in the ledger. No additional burst could come out of the reserve needed to maintain circulation. Between transmission windows, they built examples without sending them, listening to the fan and watching the receiver temperature settle.

Their first distinction required no memory: an identical pattern repeated, followed by a pair in which one group contained an extra pulse.

Lev insisted on changing only one feature at a time.

“If we change the spacing and the count, we won’t know which difference matters.”

“And if it repeats our marker?” Neri asked.

“We record repetition. We don’t write ‘understands.’”

They began a small table. On one side, Imani entered what they had transmitted and what had returned. On the other, she placed their proposed meanings: same, changed, one more. The separation left an uncomfortable amount of empty space around their hopes.

Across the next exchanges, the returned patterns preserved some distinctions. Identical groups received one recurring treatment; altered groups received another. Lev reversed the examples and varied their lengths. The treatment remained consistent often enough to justify another trial, not enough to erase the question marks.

For correction, they resent an earlier example with one substitution, retaining its other features. They associated a new marker with the replacement through repeated demonstrations. Whether the correspondent interpreted this as correction, selection, or merely another operation remained unresolved.

Order proved harder.

Imani placed two distinguishable groups on the screen, then exchanged their positions.

“First and second,” she said.

“Or left and right,” Lev replied.

“Not on a wire.”

“Earlier and later in the signal, then. That still isn’t earlier and later in an event.”

Neri rubbed her eyes. The distinction was maddening, and necessary. A message arrived in an order whether or not it described one.

The meal they had chosen remained in the ledger: a gathering, food passed among people, company continuing after the plates were empty. Neri still wanted to send it. But they could not yet reliably distinguish a sequence from a collection.

“There’s something smaller we could work with first,” she said.

She remembered rain outside her childhood home after a long dry spell. The first drops had struck the metal roof separately, with such force that she had looked up from the table. Dust lay pale along the window frame. Through the open doorway came the sudden smell of wetted earth.

Her younger sister had begun telling her about a bird she thought she had heard on the walk home. She had followed its little whistle halfway down the lane before discovering that the sound came from her own sandal.

“She demonstrated it,” Neri said. “Twisted her foot on the floor. It wouldn’t make the noise indoors.”

Imani smiled.

“And that was what made you laugh?”

“No. She said the bird had become shy.”

For an instant Neri could hear the change in her sister’s voice, the solemn delivery that had finally defeated them both. Then the fan scraped, and she was back beneath the ship’s low ceiling.

Imani recorded only the sequence Neri could attest to: drops struck the roof; her sister began speaking; the account ended; they laughed.

“Did the rain stop before the laughter?” Lev asked.

“No. I don’t remember when it stopped.”

Imani left that interval open.

Lev made paired versions of the sequence, exchanging only the positions of two events. These were exercises in relation, not competing accounts. Their event tokens were arbitrary; nothing yet connected one to rain, another to speech.

Neri studied the versions.

“Send the numerical examples first,” she said. “Then the unchanged pair, then the reversed pair. Not the whole memory.”

“And the uncertain parts?”

“We keep them out of the asserted sequence. Use the provisional marker beside the mapping we haven’t established.”

She added a note in the ledger: marker intended to distinguish an untested interpretation, not demonstrated to mean uncertainty.

The final packet contained none of the roof’s sharp music, none of her sister’s stubborn little performance. Neri felt the loss keenly, but would not disguise it.

She authorized the burst.

A brief draw registered against their allowance. Then the transmitter fell quiet, and they waited beside a memory they had begun, very carefully, not yet to tell.

The transmitter cooled with small metallic clicks. Neri kept her hand beside the switch, feeling foolishly that moving away would mean abandoning whoever—or whatever—might be listening.

Across the compartment, the scrubber fan scraped through another revolution. The response had changed something in the room, but not the cartridge inventory or the pressure in the tanks.

Lev opened the independent receiver log again.

“You checked that,” Imani said.

“I checked the previous interval.”

He aligned the new timestamps with the switching record, then compared the raw traces from both receivers. Neri watched his finger stop at the transmitted burst and travel to the returning pattern. The interval remained consistent with a delayed external exchange. No path through the ship’s equipment had been found that could account for it.

“That doesn’t make it a person,” he said.

“No,” Neri answered.

“An automated station could compare patterns. So could something designed to answer challenges.”

“But it answered this one.”

Lev looked at her, then back at the trace.

“Yes. The fresh pattern changes what we can reasonably claim.”

She had wanted a warmer admission. Hearing his exact words, she realized she preferred them to reassurance he did not believe.

“I owe you an apology,” she said. “The resemblance to an emergency relay—I presented that as evidence.”

“You presented a resemblance.”

“As if it established a purpose.”

He nodded once, accepting the correction without making her repeat it.

Imani drew the ledger between them. “Then let’s keep separating those.”

The returned packet preserved several numerical groups and altered the placement of one comparison marker. It was not a simple copy of what they had sent. That was encouraging; what the alteration meant remained unresolved.

They began again with the smallest distinctions.

Two identical groups. A group repeated with one count changed. The same two groups presented in opposite order.

Imani kept a column for what they had transmitted and another for what came back. Lev proposed competing interpretations beneath each response. A marker they tentatively called same might indicate repetition, agreement, or merely the instruction to reproduce an adjacent block. They needed examples that would force those possibilities apart.

Neri crossed out a sentence in her notes: They understand difference.

She replaced it with: Responses distinguish these cases.

The smaller claim felt less satisfying and more useful.

While Lev prepared the next comparisons, she returned to the rain.

Not to the abbreviated sequence in the ledger, but to the afternoon itself: the pale yard outside her childhood home, the ground hardened by a dry spell, the first dark circles appearing beneath the eaves. Water had struck the metal roof in separate, startling taps before the sound thickened.

She remembered the smell rising from the earth. She could not remember whether she had noticed it before her sister began speaking.

That uncertainty irritated her disproportionately. The afternoon had always seemed whole. Asked to account for its order, she found gaps everywhere.

“The sandal story,” Imani said gently. “Stay with what you remember happening.”

Neri nodded.

Her sister had followed a whistle down the lane, believing there was a bird nearby. Only halfway home had she discovered the sound came from her own sandal. Indoors, she had twisted her foot against the floor to demonstrate. The whistle would not return.

Then that solemn explanation: the bird had become shy.

Neri smiled before she could prevent it.

Imani waited until she looked down at the ledger again.

“First, you heard drops striking the roof. Then your sister began the account. She finished it. You both laughed.”

“Yes.”

“Did you laugh only after she finished?”

“After that last sentence. There may have been smiles before.”

“Then we won’t claim an absence of laughter earlier.”

Lev turned his display toward them. Four arbitrary event tokens occupied a row. Beneath it lay a second version, with the first two exchanged.

“Everything else stays fixed,” he said. “Only the relation between these two changes.”

“In one, the drops come before she speaks,” Neri said.

“In our intended interpretation. They don’t know that either token refers to drops or speaking.”

“And putting a token beside a recording of rain wouldn’t necessarily fix that,” Imani added. “It could refer to the roof. Or the sound. Or the recording itself.”

Neri studied the four marks. They contained no yard, no sister, no reason for laughter. Even their apparent simplicity concealed claims the protocol could not yet support: that these were events, that they belonged together, that their arrangement represented time.

“Not the whole sequence yet,” she said. “Send the two-event versions beside the numerical order examples. Then repeat the correction with different counts.”

Lev checked that the paired versions differed in only the intended relation.

“And the memory mappings?” he asked.

“Keep them provisional.”

She placed their untested marker beside those mappings in the packet, then wrote its intended function in the ledger. She added the qualification herself: no evidence yet that the correspondent understood it as uncertainty.

Before authorizing the burst, she read everything once more. She could defend the sequence she remembered. She could not defend the meanings they hoped to attach to its tokens.

Her sister’s voice remained unmistakable in her mind, yet nothing on the screen distinguished her from any other speaker.

Neri pressed the switch. The power draw registered, brief and measurable.

Then the compartment was quiet except for the fan, and Lev began the new timing interval.

On the tenth day, the homeworld channel remained a blank line across Lev’s display. No new signal, no revised estimate, nothing to suggest that anyone there knew the crew was still alive. The air reserve showed nine days. In the compartment, the scrubbers breathed with their steady mechanical hush, and every few minutes the ventilation fan gave a small metallic tick.

The reply to their previous challenge arrived while Lev was checking the receiver’s stored sequence. It had taken long enough for him to measure the delay twice and distrust the first result. Now he laid the returned patterns beside their transmission, aligning each mark with the count that had prompted it.

They had sent pairs of numbers in two arrangements: two before five, then five before two; three before eight, then eight before three. The counts changed, but the relation stayed simple. In both replies, the correspondent had returned the lower number before the higher one and placed the familiar correction mark beside the transposed arrangement.

Lev ran the comparison again. “Same feature. Different counts.”

“An ordering convention,” Imani said. “That’s all we can claim.”

“It doesn’t tell us what the other tokens mean,” Neri said.

“No,” Lev replied. “Or whether this is time, rank, direction. Just that the order matters to them in the same way across these examples.”

Neri looked at the row of marks on the screen. Each had been assigned a place in her memory by the crew, not by the correspondent. Their ledger called the mappings provisional, and she was grateful for the word. It let them proceed without pretending the unknown had become familiar.

She brought up the account she had built from the memory: the dry interval, long enough to notice the silence; rain arriving from above; the roof’s answer to it; then her sister laughing after the anecdote broke off unfinished. Neri had once thought the story itself was what she wanted to preserve. Now she knew it was the moment her sister laughed, as if the missing ending had been a private joke between them.

But she could not send that knowledge as certainty. Not to the correspondent. Not even to herself, some days.

They used the event tokens they had tested before. Neri placed them in the intended order, keeping the remembered details in their separate fields. Beside it, she made a second version that exchanged only the rain and the roof’s response. The dry interval stayed first; the laughter stayed last. Nothing else changed.

Imani checked the comparison criterion in the ledger, the one they had agreed to before seeing this reply: the response had to preserve the unchanged parts, distinguish the two arrangements, and return the intended relation with the correction mark. No revising the test after the fact. No promoting a guess into a translation.

Neri attached the correction sign to the paired sequence. It was the same limited convention they had used in earlier exchanges. Whether the correspondent understood it as “wrong,” “changed,” or something else was still unknown. She noted that in the ledger, though seeing the qualification beside her sister’s memory made it feel almost indecent.

Lev checked the packet once more, then checked it again. “Ready.”

Imani gave the authorization. The transmission took less power than the scrubbers used in a minute, but the draw appeared on the board all the same. When the signal ended, Lev started measuring the next reply interval.

They waited through the usual receiver noise: thin static, a low pulse that repeated at irregular intervals, then silence. Neri tried not to read intention into the silences. She made tea she did not finish. Imani inspected the air figures and entered them in the log without rounding upward.

Near the end of the watch, the receiver registered a return.

Lev brought the pattern onto the central display. The correspondent had not reproduced both arrangements. It returned the sequence in the intended order: dry interval, rain, roof response, laughter after the unfinished story. The rain token came before the roof token. The correction mark stood beside the rejected relation, where their criterion said it should.

For several breaths, no one spoke. Then Lev read the sequence aloud, not as a translation but as a check against the symbols. “It chose the order we marked.”

“It used the mark,” Imani said. She rested a hand on the back of his chair. “That’s evidence of a distinction. It isn’t evidence of what the memory means to them.”

Neri watched the unfamiliar marks hold their places. The answer did not contain her sister’s voice, or the smell of rain, or the dark wooden beams of the old roof. It did not prove anyone had felt what she felt. It was smaller than that: the other mind, whoever or whatever it was, had received a difference and used it.

Imani entered the result in the log. She wrote that the ordering test had met its criterion, then added that the meaning of the tokens remained unresolved. She made no prediction about rescue or survival.

Lev opened a personal recording. He did not address it to whoever might someday find them. He spoke to Imani and Neri, naming the test they had run and the fact that, for now, they were still together. His voice was careful at first, then steadier.

Neri played the saved fragment of her sister’s unfinished anecdote. The laughter came at the same place as always, bright and abruptly cut short. Neri let it run without asking it to answer anything.

When the recording ended, the watch changed hands. The receiver remained open.

On the tenth day, the homeworld had sent them nothing.

Nine days of breathable air remained in the reserve estimate. The number appeared in the corner of the habitat display, modest and gray, as though it described a supply unrelated to the three people living beneath it. Imani had checked the figure twice during her watch. Each time she had entered it in the log without adding a forecast.

The receiver had been quiet long enough for its silence to become part of the room: a thin electrical hiss beneath the fans, under the soft clicks of the cooling system. Neri sat with her hands folded to keep from touching the playback controls. Lev watched the signal trace. Imani stood behind them, reading the transmitted sequence again on the side display.

They had sent the memory in parts, each one bounded and marked: a stretch of dry time; rain descending from above; the old roof answering with its uneven tapping; then her sister’s laughter, after the story had stopped before its ending. They had given context where they could, and corrections where they had learned the other side might use them. Nothing in the exchange had earned the name translation.

Neri had wanted to send only the true order. Lev had asked what a response could prove if it had no alternative against which to be tested. His qualification had stung her. It sounded, at first, like he was reducing her sister to a puzzle. She had asked him for his assessment anyway, and he had made the limits clear: send the sequence once in its intended order, then send a paired version with one relation reversed. Mark the error using the correction convention they had already established. If the reply preserved the intended relation and carried the mark in the expected place, they could say the distinction had been received and used.

They had agreed on a two-second transmission burst, then physically isolated the transmitter. No repetition, no convenient extra clue. They had also removed an earlier claim from their working record: repetition alone did not show understanding. The test was narrower, and the narrowness was the point.

Now Lev leaned nearer the display. The trace thickened, broke into short pulses, and settled. He waited through the full return before touching anything.

“Recorded,” he said.

Imani checked the clock against the receiver log. “Same delay window as the previous exchange.”

It was not certainty. It was a fact they could keep.

Lev brought the result onto the central display. The correspondent had not reproduced both arrangements. It returned the intended order: dry interval, rain, roof response, laughter after the unfinished story. The rain token came before the roof token. Beside the rejected relation stood the small correction mark they had used in earlier exchanges.

Lev compared it first with the two versions they had sent, then with the recorded pattern of corrections. He followed each mark with one finger, careful not to cover the symbols. The habitat fans filled the pause. Neri noticed that she had been holding her breath and let it go slowly.

“It chose the order we marked,” Lev said. “And put the correction marker with the rejected relation.”

Imani rested her hand on the back of his chair. “That meets the criterion.”

The phrase sounded almost too clean for what it meant. Neri looked at the sequence until the marks blurred slightly. The return did not tell her whether the correspondent knew rain as water falling from a sky, whether it recognized the roof as protection, or whether the laughter meant joy to anyone beyond her. It did not contain her sister. It did not restore the warm, damp smell of the room where the story had been told, or explain why the laugh always came just before the part Neri had wanted to hear.

But the distinction had crossed the distance between them. They had marked a difference; the reply had used it. That was not the whole memory. It was not nothing.

Imani entered the result in the log. She recorded the paired sequence, the return, and the placement of the correction marker. She wrote that the ordering test had met its criterion. Then she added that the meaning of the tokens remained unresolved. She made no prediction about the homeworld and promised no rescue.

Lev opened a personal recording. He did not address whoever might find it, or some imagined audience waiting beyond the dark. He spoke to Imani and Neri. He named the test, the measured result, and the fact that the three of them were still together in the room. At first, his voice had the flat precision he used for instrument readings. Then he paused, and when he continued it was steadier.

Neri played the saved fragment of her sister’s anecdote. The voice crackled softly through the speaker. The story stopped where it had stopped before; the laughter followed, bright and abruptly cut short. Neri let the fragment run to its end. She did not ask the silence after it to explain anything.

When the recording ended, they returned the primary receiver to its observation settings. The watch changed hands. Beyond the narrow hull, the receiver continued listening into the dark, and the channel remained open.
