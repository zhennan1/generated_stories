Incorporate woke to the sound of someone speaking his name.

“—corporate. Incorporate, wake up.”

The voice came through the helmet speakers, thin with static. He opened his eyes to the blue emergency lights of the survey vessel *Asterion* and the slow tumble of loose objects beyond his restraint webbing. A pen drifted past his face. Behind it, a bead of water shivered in the air, holding the shape of a tiny moon.

“Eyes open,” said Vale. “Good. Can you hear me?”

Incorporate tried to answer, but his tongue felt too large for his mouth. He pulled the microphone toward his lips. “I hear you.”

Across the cabin, Vale gripped the edge of the navigation console with one hand. Her dark hair had escaped its tie and floated around her head. At the engineering station, Sato was braced against the wall, one knee locked beneath a handhold while he worked through a panel with a screwdriver. The three of them had trained for engine failure, collision, radiation storms, and even mutiny. None of the manuals had included the disappearance of a planet.

The forward display remained black except for the pale grid of the observation window. Beyond it lay the system where their home world had been.

Incorporate turned his head toward the glass. “Bring up the external feed.”

“It’s already up,” Vale said.

“No. The feed is dark.”

“The feed is accurate.”

The words settled between them more heavily than the alarm tones. Incorporate released his restraint and pushed himself toward the console. Every movement sent him drifting, and he caught the nearest rail before his shoulder struck the ceiling. On the display, stars shone in their appointed places. The red giant at the edge of the system burned steadily. The inner moons crossed their orbital paths.

There was no planet.

Not a shattered ring. Not a cloud of debris. Not a dim sphere hidden behind glare. The orbital instruments showed an empty volume where the world had been, while the moon that had circled it continued along a curve that no longer made sense. Its path bent around absence.

Incorporate called up the archived sensor sweep. “Run the mass scan again.”

Sato gave a humorless laugh. “I’ve run it six times.”

“Run it while I watch.”

“I am running it while you watch.”

“Then say the commands aloud.”

Sato turned from the panel. His face was gray in the emergency light. “Mass scan. Local volume. Full spectrum. Confirmed negligible planetary mass.”

The phrase sounded absurdly polite. Negligible planetary mass. As if their world had misplaced itself beneath a couch.

Vale reached over and activated the communications board. “We’ve sent every emergency band. Nothing from the orbital stations, the colonies, or the surface.”

“The surface isn’t answering because the surface isn’t there,” Sato said.

Incorporate stared at him. Sato’s screwdriver spun free and drifted between them. “Don’t make the sentence smaller than it is.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means the words matter.” Incorporate caught the screwdriver. “If we call it a failure, we’ll search for a failed system. If we call it an evacuation, we’ll search for survivors. If we call it destruction, we’ll search for debris. We need the correct word.”

Vale watched him carefully. “And what word do you have?”

He looked again at the empty orbit. “Unknown.”

The communications board clicked.

All three astronauts turned toward it.

A narrow green line had appeared beneath the silent emergency channels. It pulsed at regular intervals, too slow to be a carrier tone and too deliberate to be background noise. Incorporate pulled himself into the communications seat and isolated the transmission. The signal contained no recognizable language, only a sequence of tones: three low notes, a pause, two high notes, then a long descending chord.

Sato floated closer. “That wasn’t there before.”

“Record it,” Incorporate said.

“I’m recording.”

“Route it through the linguistic analyzer.”

Vale shook her head. “It isn’t speech.”

“Everything sent from one mind to another is speech eventually.”

He filtered out the static and watched the waveform sharpen. The sequence repeated. Three low notes. Two high. The descending chord. On the fourth repetition, a secondary pattern emerged beneath it, faint enough to vanish whenever the ship’s coolant pumps engaged.

Incorporate briefly shut off the pumps.

The hidden pattern became clear.

It was not a voice, but it had intervals. The spaces were measured. The signal was dividing itself into units.

He entered the pattern into the analyzer and assigned provisional marks: A, B, C. The machine compared the intervals, searching for recurrence. A translation window opened, then stalled.

“Try binary,” Vale said.

“Already did.”

“Mathematical notation?”

“Nothing.”

Sato pointed to the display. “There. It changed.”

The signal had paused.

Then, instead of the three low notes, it produced a burst of static shaped like a breath. The analyzer translated the nearest pattern into text—not because it understood the message, but because Incorporate had given the unknown units names.

A single line appeared:

**WE HEAR YOU**

No one moved.

Incorporate felt the cabin contract around him, every surface suddenly near and tangible. The message might have been a coincidence. A corrupted echo. A trick of the analyzer, assembling familiar words from meaningless intervals. Yet the transmission had arrived on their emergency band, after the planet vanished, and it had answered the only thing they had sent into the dark: a repeated distress call bearing their vessel’s identification.

He opened a new channel and keyed the microphone.

“This is the *Asterion*,” he said. “Identify yourself.”

The signal repeated its tones.

Three low notes. A pause. Two high notes. The descending chord.

The analyzer updated.

**WE HEAR YOU, INCORPORATE**

His hand tightened around the microphone. The unknown source knew his name. Then the text flickered, and a second line appeared beneath it.

**DO NOT USE THE OLD WORD FOR HOME**


Incorporate took his thumb off the transmitter.

The warning remained beneath his name. He read it twice, resisting the impulse to repeat it aloud. Beyond the communications board, the coolant indicator had climbed into amber.

“Pumps,” Sato said.

Incorporate restored them. Vibration returned through the seat frame, and the faint intervals vanished beneath mechanical interference. For a moment he welcomed the noise. It belonged to a machine they understood.

“What old word?” Vale asked.

“There are hundreds,” Incorporate said. “Depending on the language. A birthplace. A household. The people who let you in.”

“Then it’s a useful warning,” Sato said. “Don’t say an unspecified word in an unspecified language.”

His bitterness offered Incorporate something solid to hold. Suspicion was work they could do. He saved the incoming signal and the analyzer’s settings separately, then disabled automatic transmission. Nothing they said in the cabin would leave through the microphone now.

“It may not be a warning,” he said. “We supplied the analyzer with provisional divisions. It might be forcing noise into sentences.”

“And your name?”

“Vale said it over the helmet channel. If something intercepted that, it could copy it.”

Vale reached past him and checked the transmission log. “So it doesn’t necessarily know you.”

“No.”

He wanted that distinction to comfort him. Instead, it stripped something away. For several breaths he had imagined being recognized by someone who might know where the world had gone. Now his name looked like bait.

He unfastened his restraint and pushed away from the communications seat. Catching the overhead rail, he pulled himself across the cabin toward the equipment locker behind Sato’s station. His boot struck a floating packet of wipes, sending it slowly toward the observation window.

“Where are you going?” Vale asked.

“Independent recorder. I want to separate what arrived from what we made of it.”

Inside the locker, beneath spare headset cables, he found the survey receiver used for checking surface beacons. He clipped its case to his belt and carried it back along the rail. At the communications station, he secured it beside the main board and connected it directly to the antenna feed, upstream of the linguistic analyzer.

Sato leaned over his shoulder. “That won’t tell you what it means.”

“No. It can tell us whether something changes when we ask it to.”

The receiver’s little screen filled with narrow peaks. Incorporate adjusted its filter until the hidden intervals emerged despite the pumps. They had been there throughout; he had simply needed equipment that did not average them away.

He turned the analyzer’s text display facedown.

Vale’s fingers stayed on the console edge. “Thank you,” she said quietly.

He understood. The words had become another window they could not stop looking through.

“We send a pattern,” he said. “Not language. Then request a transformation.”

“How?”

“Demonstrate it.”

He built two pairs of tone sequences: a short series followed by the same series in reverse. Between each pair he inserted a longer pause. Then he entered a third sequence without its reversed counterpart.

Sato checked the routing before Incorporate could ask. “Only those tones. No voice. No ship identification.”

Incorporate transmitted.

They waited through the receiver’s slow sweep. Somewhere behind the wall, a valve knocked three times. His mouth had gone dry. He swallowed and heard the small sound inside his own head.

Nothing answered.

“Give it another cycle,” Vale said.

“We don’t know what a cycle is,” Sato replied.

Incorporate watched the blank interval stretch. He had spent his working life treating silence as a technical condition: distance, obstruction, equipment failure. Now it contained streets, kitchens, people who might never have had time to understand what was happening.

He reached to end the test.

A cluster of peaks crossed the receiver.

“Don’t touch it,” Sato said.

Incorporate froze. The incoming sequence reproduced their third series backward. Then it repeated, with the same spacing.

Vale brought up the outgoing record beside it. She compared them point by point, moving her finger between screens.

“That’s the reversal.”

“It could still be aboard,” Sato said. “A diagnostic loop.”

“Yes,” Incorporate said, and found that he could breathe more easily even while agreeing. “So we test that next.”

They disconnected the antenna feed and transmitted a different unfinished pair into the isolated recorder. No answer came. They reconnected the antenna, sent a fresh sequence, and received its reversal.

Incorporate saved both tests.

“We have a responsive signal on the antenna path,” he said for the log. “Source and meaning unverified.”

He uncovered the translation screen. The old warning still waited there.

“Can we ask whether they’re alive?” Vale said.

He looked at her hand, pressed flat against the console as though holding something down.

“We can ask. We can’t yet trust the answer.”

“Ask anyway.”

Incorporate opened a fresh working file. Above the provisional vocabulary, he typed a sentence for the three of them, not the transmitter:

**A reply is not yet a promise.**

Then he made room beneath it for their first careful question.


Incorporate left the question on the working file and reached for the service intercom. “We need to inspect the antenna junction.”

Vale looked up from the receiver. “Now?”

“If the response is being altered somewhere between the feed and the board, we’re building a conversation with our own equipment.”

Sato’s voice came over the cabin channel, faint beneath the coolant noise. “The junction is in the communications spine. I can meet you there.”

Incorporate hesitated. The receiver’s screen still showed the last reversal: a clean descending pattern answering the sequence he had sent. It was evidence of responsiveness, not intelligence. The distinction felt increasingly like a wall built from words, and increasingly like a wall he needed.

“Bring the branch tester,” he said. “Keep it isolated from the antenna.”

He clipped the receiver to his suit belt and pushed away from the communications station. The cabin’s handholds led him toward the narrow hatch beside the emergency locker. As he pulled the hatch open, the warning on the translation display remained behind him, facedown now but not forgotten. Vale followed, carrying the isolated recorder against her chest. Sato appeared at the opposite hatch a moment later, his boots catching the floor grid as the ship’s artificial gravity cycled unevenly.

They entered the communications spine in single file. The passage was colder than the cabin, and every few meters a strip of maintenance lighting flickered from white to red. Incorporate hooked one arm through the rail and moved himself along, careful not to bump the cable trays. Behind the panels, the signal traveled through copper, fiber, and old sections of coaxial line installed before the *Asterion* had been refitted. Any one of them could be carrying a diagnostic echo. Any one of them could be carrying something else.

At the junction cabinet, Sato removed the cover and exposed three bundles of marked cable. “Antenna feed enters here. Analyzer branch leaves there. Receiver branch is the temporary line.”

“Disconnect the analyzer branch,” Incorporate said.

Vale steadied herself beside the cabinet. “Then we won’t see a translation.”

“That’s the point.”

Sato pulled the connector free. The analyzer’s status light went dark in the cabin, visible through the open hatchway. Incorporate checked the receiver. The previous waveform remained stored, but the live trace had flattened into ordinary background noise.

He routed a short sequence through the isolated internal branch with the tester—not toward the antenna, but into the branch itself. Three low tones, two high, one descending chord. He waited.

The receiver remained blank.

Sato glanced at him. “That rules out the analyzer.”

“It rules out this branch producing the response,” Incorporate said. “Reconnect the antenna. Leave the analyzer disconnected.”

When Sato reseated the connector, Incorporate sent the same sequence through the proper feed. The receiver caught a faint pulse almost immediately. It returned the tones in reverse order, then repeated them with a pause longer than the original.

Vale brought the recorder close to her visor. “It answered the test.”

“Or something beyond the hull did.”

“Can we ask the same question?”

Incorporate looked at the cable junction. A question required a shared distinction. They had only a pattern and its transformation. Still, refusing to ask would be another kind of answer, one he could not defend in the log.

He opened the receiver’s pattern editor and constructed a paired sequence: one signal for “alive,” if the unknown system could understand that concept, and another for “not alive.” The terms were arbitrary. He assigned them different rhythms, then wrote the assignments in the log with a warning beneath them: **These marks represent our test, not discovered meaning.**

Sato read over his shoulder. “You’re asking a machine to choose between two words it may never have heard.”

“I’m asking for a repeatable distinction.”

“And if it chooses one?”

“Then we ask whether it can choose the other.”

He transmitted the first pattern. The ship seemed to hold its breath, though Incorporate knew that was only the cessation of their own movement. The receiver showed static, then a narrow rising line. It formed the same pattern they had sent, but not in reverse. The repetition arrived with an additional pulse at the end.

Vale touched the edge of the recorder. “That’s new.”

Incorporate recorded the interval before speaking. “It may be an acknowledgment. Or an instruction. Or interference.”

“Can we send the second pattern?”

He wanted to. The impulse was almost physical, a hand pushing between his shoulder blades. But the extra pulse had changed the exchange. If they transmitted immediately, they might mistake persistence for understanding.

“Not yet,” he said. “We need to know whether the added pulse is stable.”

He sent the first pattern again. This time the response came later, after the coolant pumps cycled, and the extra pulse was absent. The return still matched the original rhythm closely enough to make his skin prickle.

Incorporate saved both recordings under separate labels: **response A, delayed** and **response B, altered**. Then he disconnected the temporary receiver and secured the cabinet.

“We’re moving the experiment,” he told Vale and Sato. “No more interpreting at the console. We’ll build a sealed test at the antenna junction and compare every transmission against the raw feed.”

Vale looked toward the cabin, where the dark analyzer waited. “And the warning?”

Incorporate pulled himself back through the hatch. “We leave it untouched until we know whether the sender is using language or merely reflecting ours.”

The three of them returned to the communications station. He reconnected the receiver upstream of the analyzer, but left the translation display dark. On the working file, beneath **A reply is not yet a promise**, he added a second line:

**A transformation is not yet an answer.**

Then he placed the two life-patterns beside the transmitter, ready for the next test and unwilling, for the moment, to call either one hope.


Incorporate kept both life-pattern files open while the communications station settled around them. The temporary receiver monitored the antenna feed, the analyzer branch remained disconnected, and the translation display stayed dark. Nothing in the equipment had changed, yet the cabin felt altered by the decision not to send the second pattern. A choice could occupy space. It could sit between three people like another instrument, waiting to be handled.

Vale secured the recorder to the console with a strip of fastening tape. “You said we’re moving the experiment.”

“To the junction,” Incorporate said. “Not the whole station. We’ll transmit from the sealed service panel and record there before the signal reaches any of our interpretation equipment.”

Sato turned from the open cabinet. “The junction is already exposed.”

“Exposed isn’t sealed. We need to eliminate every route by which the ship can answer itself.”

He brought up the station schematic and enlarged the communications spine. The antenna feed entered through a coupler, passed the junction, and continued toward the outer dish. The analyzer branch was attached at a second port. Their temporary receiver sat on a line they had added by hand. If any of the ports carried a stored diagnostic pattern, a response could appear without crossing the hull. The possibility was ordinary. That was what made it dangerous. Ordinary explanations did not become less possible merely because the alternative made them feel less alone.

He gathered the receiver, two passive splitters, the branch tester, and a spool of shielded cable. Then he pushed away from the communications seat and pulled himself along the overhead rail toward the service hatch. Vale followed with the recorder. Sato came last after checking that the analyzer remained electrically isolated. Incorporate watched the familiar console recede behind him, including the dark translation display and the warning he had chosen not to disturb.

At the hatch, he stopped and looked back. “No one speaks a question during the test.”

Vale’s eyebrows lifted. “Because the microphone is disconnected?”

“Because we need one kind of input at a time.”

Sato gave a short nod. “Patterns only.”

Incorporate opened the hatch. The narrow passage beyond was still cold from the earlier inspection, and the maintenance lights pulsed unevenly along the cable trays. He entered first, bracing his shoulder against the wall as the ship’s gravity shifted. The move from the communications station into the spine was only a few meters, but the distance gave the experiment a boundary. Behind them was the place where they had begun assigning names to sounds. Ahead was copper, shielding, and the unadorned fact of transmission.

At the junction, Sato held the cabinet door while Incorporate connected the receiver directly across the antenna coupler. Vale ran the shielded cable back to the recorder and checked each seal with her gloved fingers. No line touched the analyzer branch. No audio channel remained live. Incorporate placed a strip of opaque tape over the analyzer indicator on the remote console, then wrote the setup into the log by hand.

**Test four. Antenna coupler monitored at junction. Analyzer disconnected. Voice input disabled. Pattern two withheld.**

He read the last sentence twice. “Begin with a null.”

Sato activated the tester without transmitting anything. The receiver registered the ship’s background: coolant vibration, electrical grain, a faint irregular tick from somewhere beyond the panel. Incorporate marked the trace. They waited through another pump cycle. The tick remained, but it did not arrange itself into the intervals of either life-pattern.

“Null stable enough,” Vale said.

“Stable for this sample,” he corrected.

She did not argue. That, more than agreement, made him trust her.

He selected the first pattern, the one with the shorter rising interval, and sent it through the sealed line. The outgoing pattern appeared cleanly on the receiver’s trace at the coupler. Then static gathered at the edge of the trace and separated into a return.

The answer reproduced the pattern almost exactly. A pause followed. Then came the additional pulse.

Incorporate held his breath until the recorder captured the final interval. He replayed the raw signal at reduced speed. The pulse was there: narrow, distinct, neither part of the original pattern nor a repetition of any ordinary background tick.

“Record the gap,” he said.

Vale marked it. “The same gap as before?”

“Not the same duration. Similar position.”

Sato leaned close to the receiver. “Send it again.”

Incorporate’s hand rested above the control. The second life-pattern remained untouched in its file. “We repeat the first. Nothing else.”

He transmitted again. This time the return arrived after a longer silence. The pattern came back slightly distorted, and the added pulse appeared at the end once more. Its amplitude was lower, but its position relative to the return remained within the margin they had established.

Vale exhaled. “That makes two.”

“It makes two observations,” Incorporate said. “Not a stable code.”

“But not random background.”

“Not proven.”

He sent the first pattern a third time, then waited through two full pump cycles before allowing himself to look away from the receiver. The return came back without the added pulse. The original rhythm was still recognizable, but the extra interval had vanished completely.

Sato’s hand tightened on the cabinet rail. “There.”

“Yes.”

“Does that rule out an external sender?”

“No. It rules out consistency so far.”

Incorporate saved all three recordings with their conditions: **junction response one, pulse present; junction response two, pulse present; junction response three, pulse absent**. He added a note that the antenna remained connected throughout and that the analyzer had not received power.

The experiment had not given them an answer. It had given them a narrower question, which was the only kind he trusted. He opened the sealed-test file and typed a revised procedure: repeat the first pattern under controlled pump states, then compare the added pulse against the ship’s electrical cycles. Only after that would they consider the second pattern.

Vale read the entry over his shoulder. “You still think we should ask whether anyone is alive?”

“I think we should learn whether the system can distinguish one pattern from another before we give it a choice.”

“And if it can?”

Incorporate looked down the dark passage toward the communications station. “Then we’ll decide what a choice means.”

They disconnected the junction receiver but left the antenna path untouched, securing the cabinet before returning through the hatch. At the station, Incorporate placed the withheld second pattern in a locked test queue. He did not transmit it. Beneath the earlier lines, he added one more sentence to the working file:

**A repeated event is evidence of behavior, not yet evidence of a speaker.**

The warning remained unchanged beneath it, waiting in a language none of them could claim to understand.


Back at the communications station, Incorporate kept the junction recordings open beside the ship’s maintenance telemetry. The three of them had returned through the service hatch with the antenna path intact and the cabinet secured, carrying the raw traces instead of an answer. He reseated the portable receiver upstream of the dark analyzer branch, checked that the translation display remained disabled, and wrote the transition into the log: **Test location restored to communications station; junction isolated; antenna connected; interpretation equipment offline.** The sentence looked bureaucratic, but it gave the experiment a boundary. They were no longer pretending that the station itself was a neutral room.

Sato floated the shielded cable into its storage cradle. “We have three responses and one irregularity.”

“One irregularity that appears twice,” Vale said.

“In similar positions,” Incorporate replied. He enlarged the traces until the added pulse became a thin vertical mark against the grid. “The difference matters. If we call it the same pulse before measuring its relation to the ship, we’ve already made the conclusion.”

Vale fastened the recorder beside the console. “Then measure the ship first.”

He looked at her. She had said it without impatience and did not press him to qualify the conclusion further. Trust, Incorporate had learned, was not agreement. It was allowing another person to preserve the uncertainty you wanted to erase. He opened a fresh test file and named it **response-pulse correlation**, leaving the second life-pattern locked in the queue.

They began with the pumps at their ordinary cycle. Sato brought up the engineering monitor while Vale armed the recorder to capture the antenna feed, the junction return, and the electrical bus on separate channels. Incorporate sent the first pattern once. The outgoing tones crossed the trace cleanly. After the expected delay, the return formed, and the added pulse appeared at its familiar relative position.

He marked the pump phase, then waited. “Again after the next cycle.”

The ship’s coolant system engaged with a muted shudder. Pipes trembled behind the bulkhead, and the console lights dipped by a fraction. Incorporate watched the telemetry rather than the waveform until Sato called, “Cycle complete.”

He transmitted the same pattern. The response came back distorted at its edges. The added pulse appeared again, but its amplitude rose with the electrical draw. Incorporate replayed the raw recording twice, then aligned it against the bus fluctuation. The peaks did not match exactly. They were close enough to invite a theory and far enough to punish one.

“Correlation?” Vale asked.

“Suggestive,” he said. “Not causal.”

Sato adjusted the pump controller to its low setting. “We can force the difference.”

“That changes more than the pump state,” Incorporate said. “Voltage, vibration, timing. Record all of it.”

Sato nodded and initiated the controlled shift. The station grew quieter as the coolant machinery slowed. For a moment Incorporate heard only the relay clicks and the soft rasp of his own breath inside the suit collar. The silence made the distant planetless dark feel near—not visible through the hull, but present as a pressure around it. He pushed the thought away and selected the first pattern again.

The transmission returned with no added pulse.

Vale’s hand stopped above the recorder. “Absent.”

“Under low pump state,” Incorporate said. “One observation.”

Sato increased the setting to high. The ship answered with vibration that traveled through the seat frame and into Incorporate’s wrists. He sent the pattern a fourth time. The response appeared later than before, then reproduced the tones. A narrow extra mark emerged at the end, weaker than in the ordinary cycle but unmistakable on the expanded trace.

No one spoke while the recorder finished. Incorporate saved the files under their operating conditions, then compared the intervals manually. The pulse appeared during ordinary and high pump states, vanished during the low state, and did not line up precisely with the electrical spikes. That was progress, though it felt less like opening a door than removing one layer of darkness from a corridor.

“We can’t call it external yet,” Vale said.

“No,” Incorporate answered. “But we can say the ship’s operating state affects what we receive.”

Sato looked toward the locked second pattern. “Does that earn us the other test?”

Incorporate closed the file and secured it with a checksum. “No. It earns us another first-pattern run with the pumps held steady and the transmitter power varied independently.”

Vale gave him a tired smile. “You’ve found a way to make caution sound like a destination.”

“It’s the only destination we can verify.”

He entered the revised procedure beneath the previous note: **Repeat under fixed pump state. Compare transmitter power, bus noise, and pulse position. Do not transmit pattern two.** Then he added the event to the shared log, preserving the earlier life-pattern tests and the untouched warning rather than replacing them with the new result. The old conclusions remained accepted: the first pattern had produced recognizable returns; the second had not been tested; repeated returns still did not establish a speaker.

When he finally leaned back, the receiver emitted a single irregular tick from the antenna line. It was not arranged like either pattern. Incorporate recorded it anyway. The sound might belong to the ship, the junction, or something beyond the hull. For now, language could do only one honest thing with it.

He labeled the file **unclassified interval** and left the channel listening.


The unclassified interval remained a thin scratch on the receiver display. Incorporate let the recording finish before touching anything. Beside it, the proposed experiment waited in plain text: fixed pump state, variable transmitter power. The tick had changed his desire to conduct that experiment, not its justification. He wanted to follow the stray sound wherever it led. Instead, he saved it separately, preserving the conditions under which it had appeared.

“We need a power measurement at the junction,” he said. “The transmitter’s own readout isn’t an independent measurement.”

Sato unfastened a portable meter from the equipment rack. Incorporate took it, pushed out of the communications seat, and caught the overhead rail. He passed through the service hatch into the communications spine, leaving Vale at the recorder and Sato beside the pump controls. Their voices followed through the open hatch, ordinary sounds traveling a distance he could still cross.

At the junction, he braced his feet beneath a cable tray and inspected the seals they had placed during the earlier test. None had shifted. He connected the meter to the existing monitor port without opening the antenna path or reconnecting the analyzer branch. Its display settled on the background level. For several breaths he watched the digits change, listening for another tick. There was only the ventilation and the faint knock of equipment against his sleeve.

“Monitor attached,” he called. “Seals intact.”

Vale repeated his words from the station. Hearing them returned without alteration gave him an unexpected measure of relief. Once, a report like that would have disappeared into a planetary network: archived, overlooked, available to people who had never met him. Now its entire audience could fit inside this passage.

He secured the meter, then pulled himself back through the hatch. At the communications station, he checked that its feed appeared on the recorder beside the bus-noise channel. Sato held the pump at the ordinary setting. They waited for the transition fluctuations to settle before Incorporate armed the first-pattern file. The second remained locked; the translation display remained dark. Beneath the working windows, the warning survived untouched.

“Low power first,” Incorporate said.

The transmitted pattern registered at both monitoring points. He entered the measured power, marked the bus noise, and watched the empty stretch that followed. A recognizable return emerged, faint enough that he enlarged the trace without changing the saved recording. At its trailing edge lay a small rise.

“Added pulse?” Sato asked.

“Candidate,” Incorporate said. “Too close to the noise floor to count confidently.”

Vale entered that wording. He was grateful she did not shorten it to *present*.

They waited through an equal recording window without transmitting. Incorporate marked it as a null, then increased the transmitter setting while Sato kept the pump unchanged. This time the return stood clearly above the background, and an added pulse followed it. Incorporate measured its position relative to the returned pattern, recorded the bus fluctuation, and checked the junction meter against the station readout.

“There,” Sato said, touching the screen’s edge. “More power, clearer pulse.”

“For these two samples.”

“I know.” Sato withdrew his hand. “I’m trying to say what I can see.”

Incorporate looked up. Sato’s mouth was tight, his fingers hooked under the console rail.

“So am I,” Incorporate said, more gently.

Vale shifted the recorder so all three of them could see it. “Then let’s keep both sentences. The pulse is clearer. We don’t know why.”

He nodded. She had left room for the observation without making it carry their survival. He entered her wording in the log.

For the third transmission, he returned to the lower power setting. The original rhythm came back faintly, but no trailing rise could be distinguished from the noise. He saved the entire window rather than trimming it around the expected position. The three trials established a workable range for further measurements; they did not establish that the pulse switched on and off with power. A weaker signal could conceal what a stronger one revealed. The transmitter could also disturb something aboard the ship. He wrote both possibilities down.

His hand cramped around the stylus. He loosened it slowly.

“Before,” he said, “I could have sent these recordings home.”

Neither of the others spoke.

“Someone would have found a mistake. Or suggested a better instrument. We would have complained about the delay.”

Vale rested her palm against the recorder. “We can still check each other.”

He looked at the three columns of measurements. It was not enough. Three exhausted astronauts were not a world, and careful labels could not explain how a planet vanished. Yet Vale had offered something they could actually do.

He read the entries aloud. Sato caught a transposed power value; Vale asked him to separate *pulse absent* from *pulse not distinguishable*. Incorporate corrected both, leaving the original entries visible beneath the amendments.

Then he secured the new recordings alongside the earlier accepted results. The stray tick retained its own file and its unresolved origin. Pattern two remained untested.

“Another low-power run?” Sato asked.

Incorporate checked the bus trace, then the steady pump reading.

“After a null window,” he said.

He armed the recorder. This time, while the empty channel accumulated its small variations, he left his hand open on the console beside theirs.


The null window ended without a tick. Incorporate preserved it at the same scale as the transmission windows, resisting the urge to stretch its small fluctuations into something worth seeing. Beside his open hand, Vale’s fingers rested against the recorder housing. Sato still held the pump at its ordinary setting. Nothing had answered because nothing had been asked, though even that sentence claimed more than they knew.

“Low power,” Incorporate said.

He checked the setting with Sato before transmitting. The first pattern passed through the monitor traces, followed by the familiar interval of waiting. Its return emerged faintly, recognizable by spacing rather than height. At the trailing edge, the line trembled and settled.

“Not distinguishable,” Vale said.

Incorporate entered the phrase. There was comfort in its precision, and something intolerable too. Their world had disappeared; stations and colonies remained unresponsive; somewhere inside the unknown signal lay his own name. Against those enormities they had placed a sentence about a tremor too small to classify. He wanted to strike the console, not from anger at the machine but to make an event whose cause he understood.

Instead, he released the stylus.

“We’re judging the faint returns after we know the power,” he said. “And after we know where to look.”

Vale lifted her hand from the recorder. “You want the windows mixed.”

“Copies. Originals untouched. Include the nulls.”

Sato glanced toward the service hatch. “The meter still has its local record. We should preserve that before we build another comparison.”

“Yes,” Incorporate said. “Thank you. Leave the pump where it is.”

He unfastened his seat restraint and pushed toward the hatch. The communications station narrowed behind him as he pulled himself into the spine, one hand following the rail. He took the route slowly. Fatigue had made his limbs unreliable in small ways: a boot caught beneath a strap, an elbow struck against a rounded corner. Each contact sounded louder here than it had beside the others.

At the junction he braced beneath the cable tray. The seals remained intact, the analyzer branch disconnected. He checked the meter’s available storage and copied its local record to the portable memory clipped inside his sleeve. The transfer indicator advanced while the antenna cable lay motionless beside his wrist.

He had once used *alone* carelessly. Alone in a cabin, alone on watch, alone with a difficult calculation. There had always been people beyond the closed door, and beyond them streets, kitchens, arguments. Here, only a short passage separated him from the other two astronauts. He could still hear the ventilation at the station, but not their voices.

“Copy nearly complete,” he called.

“Receiving you,” Vale answered.

He closed his eyes for one breath. She had not promised anything. She had only confirmed that his words had arrived.

When the transfer finished, he verified the file size, left the meter connected, and pulled himself back along the rail. At the station he secured himself beside the console and opened the copy against the recorder’s matching interval. Sato checked the alignment using the outgoing transmission, then pointed to a small offset.

“That much,” he said. “Not the value from the previous run.”

Incorporate corrected it. “Check me again after I save.”

Together they prepared the review set. Vale stripped the operating labels from duplicate windows and assigned neutral letters. Sato verified that each showed the same duration and scale. Incorporate kept the key closed. Pattern two remained locked, the warning untouched beneath their working files; the dark translation display offered no assistance.

They inspected the windows separately, passing the screen between them and writing judgments before speaking. Incorporate found the exercise unexpectedly difficult. Without the power labels, one rise he had wanted to call a pulse looked ordinary. Another seemed persuasive until he noticed that he had shifted his own threshold for what counted.

Vale opened their judgments side by side.

They agreed on the clearest return. They disagreed on two faint trailing rises. One null window had received a tentative mark from Incorporate.

He stared at his mark. His face warmed.

“Keep it,” he said when Vale reached for the annotation controls. “Don’t clean that away.”

“I was adding the comparison,” she said.

“Right. Sorry.”

Sato leaned closer, his shoulder touching Incorporate’s. “We still see the strong one.”

“We do.” Incorporate made himself look at all the judgments, not only his mistake. “And we don’t have a reliable rule for the weak ones.”

Vale typed that beneath the table, then turned the screen for his approval.

He read it twice. They had not disproved the added pulse, found its source, or learned whether anything beyond the hull meant to reach them. But they had exposed one way their longing could enter a measurement. He saved the comparison beside the unaltered recordings and added a requirement: independent judgments before operating labels were revealed.

“Next null?” Sato asked.

Incorporate checked the storage and the steady pump reading. “Yes. Then one low-power repeat. Same procedure.”

Vale armed the recorder. This time he turned the results window away until all three of them were ready to look.


Vale armed the recorder and turned the results window away from them. Incorporate watched the status bar begin its slow accumulation. The communications station seemed to contract around that blank display: three restraints, three pairs of hands, the sealed access to the service spine. The pump held its ordinary state. The transmitter remained disarmed. Nothing in the apparatus had changed except their decision to keep themselves ignorant for a little longer.

Sato checked the cable seating, then read the meter on the auxiliary panel. “Background is stable.”

“Say the value.”

Sato did. Incorporate repeated it and entered the number into the open procedure log. He added the bus-noise range, the recorder scale, and the fact that the antenna-line monitor had shown no unclassified tick during setup. The words made the experiment feel solid without making it safe. A null was not an absence of whatever had answered before. It was only an interval in which their instruments had recorded no answer they could distinguish.

The window completed. Vale tapped the control that locked its label before displaying the trace. “Null saved. No one looked.”

Incorporate checked the seals listed in the procedure. “Low-power repeat next. Same pump setting. Same duration. Same monitor points.”

Sato’s hand moved to the transmitter control, then stopped. “You want the junction meter checked first?”

“Yes. We know the station readout can drift against the local record. Let’s not give ourselves another avoidable argument.”

He released his restraint and pushed toward the service hatch. The movement carried him out of the communications station and into the narrow spine, with the hatch frame sliding past his shoulder and the station’s layered electronic hum thinning behind him. He caught the rail at each interval, carrying the portable memory and a calibration lead clipped to his belt. The route to the antenna junction was familiar now, but familiarity did not make the dark corners less oppressive. His suit brushed a cable tray. The sound traveled ahead of him and returned altered by the passage.

At the junction he planted both boots beneath the tray and examined the monitor port. The seal remained unbroken. He connected the calibration lead without opening the antenna path, then compared the meter’s idle reading with the value Sato had spoken. The difference was small but real, within the range they had already marked. He recorded it instead of correcting for it. A correction could be applied later; a vanished discrepancy could not be recovered.

“Junction is ready,” he called.

Vale’s voice arrived through the spine speaker. “The null remains locked. Sato is confirming the transmitter setting.”

“Have him confirm it aloud before arming.”

A pause followed, then Sato’s voice: “Pump ordinary. Transmitter low. No analyzer branch. Recording all channels.”

Incorporate looked at the disconnected branch. Its connector sat beneath a transparent cover, where the last unexplained antenna-line tick had appeared in the earlier record. He had wanted to touch it, to find a loose contact or a residue of charge that would turn the event into a mechanical fault. Instead, he photographed the connector in place and added the image to the trial directory. The unknown signal deserved neither reverence nor convenient destruction.

“Ready,” he said.

Through the speaker he heard Vale count the start. The transmitter fired. At the junction, the meter rose by a fraction, settled, and dropped. The event was too clean to resemble the unclassified tick. Incorporate watched the local trace while the signal crossed whatever distance separated their ship from its source. The pattern itself had no voice, yet it carried the shape of intention: spacing, repetition, a pause where a reply might be expected. Language could survive even after meaning had been stripped from it. He wondered whether that was what they had become—syntax without an audience.

The return appeared after the expected interval. On the small junction display it was barely more than a change in the line, but the spacing was unmistakable. Incorporate did not name it. He measured the delay, marked the background, and waited through the trailing edge. A slight rise followed, then another fluctuation that merged with the noise floor.

“Candidate trailing event,” he said. “Not distinguishable from local interference at this point.”

“Recorded,” Vale answered.

“Don’t enlarge it yet.”

He let the full window finish. The discipline felt almost cruel. Every part of him wanted to magnify the trace until the doubtful rise became a message, perhaps even the beginning of his name. He kept his hand away from the controls and watched the raw line remain what it was: a record of something they had not yet learned to describe.

When the recording ended, he saved the junction file and verified that its size matched the station copy. Only then did he disconnect the calibration lead and secure it against the tray. He pulled himself back through the spine, passing the hatch into the communications station where Vale and Sato waited beside the still-hidden results window.

“Independent judgments first,” Incorporate said, settling into his restraint. “Then we compare the junction and station traces.”

Vale turned the screen toward them but kept the operating labels covered. Sato placed his written assessment face down beside hers. Incorporate wrote his own before looking at either. The three papers formed a small bridge between their separate certainties.

He glanced at the sealed pattern-two file, then back to the new recordings. “We have another return,” he said. “We still don’t know whether the trailing rise belongs to the signal, the ship, or our hunger to connect.”

No one altered the sentence. Vale opened the first neutral trace.


The first neutral trace occupied the screen at the agreed scale. Incorporate held his assessment beneath his palm while Vale brought up the remaining windows, one at a time. They had written their judgments; now each had to finish without borrowing confidence from the others. He checked his marks against the intervals he had recorded, resisted changing a doubtful entry, and signed the bottom of his sheet. Across the console, Sato folded his paper once. Vale locked her annotations.

“Ready,” Incorporate said.

They placed the assessments together. All three had marked the expected return. At the trailing edge, Incorporate had written *possible*, Sato *not distinguishable*, and Vale *possible; no separate boundary*. Incorporate read her qualification twice. On his own sheet the unqualified word seemed suddenly extravagant.

“Keep the original wording,” he said. “Mine too.”

Vale nodded and opened the operating key.

The two tentative judgments belonged to the low-power repeat. The null had received no marks. Incorporate felt a lift so immediate that he gripped the console edge.

“One quiet null,” Sato said.

“Yes.” Incorporate let go. “Not enough to establish that the difference is real.”

Vale entered both statements in the comparison log. They looked less contradictory written beside each other. That had become one of language’s remaining uses: allowing things to coexist without forcing them into agreement. He could want the rise to mean someone was listening and still decline to call it an answer. Neither sentence had to erase the other.

They opened the junction recording beside the station copy. Using the outgoing transmission as their reference, Sato aligned the time axes while Incorporate checked the saved offset against his notes. The small meter discrepancy remained in the record, unapplied. At the trailing interval, the junction trace showed an uneven shoulder; the station trace showed a shallow swelling. At their fixed scale, neither offered a clean boundary.

“Different instruments, different shapes,” Vale said.

“Same interval within the alignment uncertainty,” Sato added.

Incorporate entered that rather than *same event*. He remembered the unexplained tick, the disconnected branch beneath its cover. Whatever they were hearing had already used his name. That fact made every neutral label feel temporary, a thin partition behind which something waited. But a name in a signal did not make every fluctuation a word.

He reached toward the sealed pattern-two file, stopped short, and moved his hand to the procedure log.

“Not yet,” he said.

Neither of the others asked him to explain.

Before authorizing another transmission, he wanted the physical setup checked against the photograph. He unclipped the portable viewer, released his restraint, and pushed from the communications station toward the service hatch. Sato stayed at the instrumentation panel to read the station values; Vale preserved the comparison and left the transmitter disarmed. Incorporate pulled himself through the hatch into the spine, carrying their agreed uncertainties with him.

At the antenna junction, he hooked his boots beneath the tray and opened the earlier photograph on the viewer. Cover, connector, retaining clip: he compared each against the hardware without touching it. The disconnected analyzer branch remained disconnected. The seal had not shifted. He photographed it again from the same angle, then read the local meter aloud.

Sato repeated the station value through the speaker.

The difference remained small. Incorporate recorded both readings and their separation, leaving the earlier discrepancy untouched.

“No adjustment,” he said.

“Confirmed,” Sato answered.

For a moment Incorporate stayed braced there. Beyond the tray, the passage lights stopped at a bend. Their ship carried three living people through a darkness that had swallowed the place where billions should have been. No call from a colony had interrupted their work. No station had returned a routine acknowledgment. He had begun to resent the word *routine*, its careless promise that something would happen again.

“Still with us?” Vale asked through the speaker.

“Yes. Checking the image.”

He looked once more, though the comparison was finished. Then he saved it and unclipped his boots.

He returned along the rail, pulling past the bend and through the hatch until the communications console came into view. Vale moved a loose restraint out of his path. He caught it, settled beside her, and attached the viewer to the station port.

“Setup unchanged,” he said. “Discrepancy preserved. We haven’t explained the tick.”

“Or the rise,” Sato said.

“Or the rise.”

Incorporate added the junction check to the completed review, then opened a fresh procedure beneath it: same pump state, transmitter power varied independently, another null before emission, independent judgments before labels. He turned the proposed settings toward Sato and the review sequence toward Vale.

“We need a stopping point,” Vale said. “Before we’re too tired to notice what we’re doing.”

“One more paired trial,” Incorporate said. “Then we stop and leave it intact.”

Sato checked the settings. Vale read back the sequence. Incorporate listened to both, correcting one omitted monitor channel before he signed. Outside, nothing had answered their questions about home. Inside, three voices established what they would do next, and he armed only the recorder.


Incorporate left the communications station only after the recorder displayed its armed status. He pushed through the hatch into the service spine, carrying the portable viewer and the calibration lead clipped to his belt. The station’s layered hum thinned behind him, replaced by the faint ticking of thermal contraction in the hull. At the antenna junction he caught the rail, swung himself beneath the cable tray, and checked the local monitor. The disconnected analyzer branch remained sealed beneath its transparent cover. He photographed the junction before touching any control, then routed the viewer’s cable to the independent monitor port.

“Junction ready,” he said.

Vale’s voice came through the speaker. “Station ready. Null window begins when you confirm.”

Sato read the baseline values aloud. Incorporate repeated each one and entered it into the trial record. He included the station-to-junction difference, the recorder scale, the pump state, and the fact that the analyzer branch was still disconnected. The numbers did not make the apparatus trustworthy. They made its limits visible.

“Begin null,” he said.

The transmitter stayed disarmed. Incorporate watched the local trace crawl across the viewer while the station recorder accumulated its corresponding line. He kept his hands folded against the tray, resisting the urge to adjust the gain. A fluctuation appeared near the lower edge of the display, then sank back into the noise. He marked it without enlarging it.

“Minor movement at the junction,” he reported. “No separate tick. Not distinguishable from background.”

Sato answered from the station. “Same interval here. No classified event.”

“Don’t classify it,” Incorporate said. “Record the wording exactly.”

Vale repeated the sentence to confirm it, then waited for the null window to complete before locking the file without showing its trace. The restraint of the action steadied him. They were no longer asking the instruments to prove that something existed. They were asking them to preserve what had happened, including the parts that refused to become meaningful.

With the null window complete, Incorporate checked the elapsed interval against the procedure and gave the authorization for the first emission. Sato armed the transmitter at the lower of the two independent settings. Vale counted down through the speaker. At zero, the junction meter lifted by a narrow, clean increment. The outgoing pulse crossed the local display, disappeared, and left the expected delay open before the return emerged.

Incorporate did not look at the station trace. He watched only the junction display until the return’s spacing could be measured. A faint shoulder followed it, uneven and shallow. He wrote the interval, the amplitude range, and the uncertainty qualifier.

“Expected return present,” he wrote. “Trailing rise possible; no separate boundary.”

The words looked familiar, but familiarity did not make them safer. The same phrase could be a careful description or a shelter built around fear. He saved the file and kept his thumb away from the compare function.

“Independent judgment,” Vale said.

“Yes. No labels until all three are written.”

He closed the viewer’s live display and wrote on the review slate. The lower-power return was clear enough to locate and too weak to interpret. The trailing rise remained possible, not distinguishable from ship-generated interference. He signed beneath the assessment and placed the slate face down in the junction’s storage clip.

Sato changed the transmitter setting only after Incorporate confirmed that the first file had been sealed. The second power level was higher, but the pump state and emission duration remained unchanged. Incorporate verified the cable path again, then pulled himself along the rail to the junction’s auxiliary panel. The movement took him past the sealed branch and the photograph pinned to the viewer’s edge. Nothing in the hardware had shifted.

“Second setting ready,” Sato said.

“Run the same null interval first.”

They waited. The ship’s background filled the monitor: pumps, relays, the nearly imperceptible vibration of the spine. Once, the local line trembled near the level of the prior unexplained tick. Incorporate saw no corresponding sharp feature. He recorded the tremor and its duration, refusing to make absence into evidence of purity.

“Null remains negative for a distinct event,” he said.

Vale locked the second null. Then Sato fired the higher-power pulse.

The outgoing mark was stronger, but the return did not become a voice. It arrived at the same broadly expected spacing, clearer at the center and surrounded by a ragged edge. Afterward came the rise: more visible now, yet still without a boundary that could separate it from the ship’s own disturbance. Incorporate felt hope press against his discipline, not as certainty but as pressure to stop qualifying.

He measured the interval twice. “Return is present. Trailing rise is more visible at increased power. It remains not distinguishable from local interference,” he wrote.

“Written,” Vale said.

“Written,” Sato echoed.

Only then did Incorporate open the paired traces. The higher setting had increased the return’s visibility, but the change did not answer whether the trailing feature belonged to the signal. The nulls remained quiet. The junction and station recordings still differed by the small preserved meter offset. Nothing had become conclusive.

He saved both files under neutral names, attached the independent judgments, and disabled the transmitter. At the junction he removed the viewer cable, capped the port, and photographed the unchanged connector and analyzer branch. He did not reconnect anything. He pulled himself back through the spine, passing the hatch frame into the communications station where Vale and Sato waited beside the locked records.

“One paired trial completed,” he said. “Two nulls, two power levels, independent judgments. Setup stays exactly as it is.”

Vale copied the statement into the procedure log. Sato secured the transmitter cover.

Incorporate watched the trial files settle into redundant storage. Their ship still crossed the darkness where their planet had been, carrying no acknowledgment from home. Yet the records held three voices refusing to let uncertainty become either despair or proof. He left the junction photographs attached, the analyzer branch disconnected, and the final traces unaltered.
