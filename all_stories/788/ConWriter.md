The chair in the endocrinology waiting room had arms that pressed against my hips. I sat forward, keeping my coat on my lap, and tried not to take that personally. Across from me, a screen advertised a wellness app: a woman running uphill beneath the words TAKE CONTROL. My phone vibrated with a notification from my own fitness app. I turned it facedown before it could congratulate me or tell me what I had failed to do.

In my tote bag was a folder thick enough to feel embarrassing. Appointment summaries, lab results, a list of medications, and the timeline I had rewritten so it would fit on one page. Thirty-one pounds gained without an explanation. Walks that had grown shorter despite my efforts to make them longer. Cold hands. Constipation. Exhaustion that sleep barely touched. I had put the symptoms before the weight this time.

At the gym, my trainer had scrolled through my food log while I stood beside the cable machine, holding a towel.

“People forget the little things,” he’d said. “Oil. Coffee creamer. A handful of nuts.”

“I measure the oil.”

He had smiled with a patience that made me feel worse than irritation would have. “Then we need to talk about honesty.”

I still heard that word while cooking, while buying groceries, while waking tired after nine hours in bed. Honesty had become something I apparently owed everybody except myself.

The first doctor had suggested an exercise program. The second had suggested that my exercise program wasn’t rigorous enough. Neither had asked why climbing the stairs to my apartment now left my thighs trembling. At the last appointment, I had started crying and watched the conversation turn toward stress. Stress was real; I had plenty. But I had arrived with more than one symptom, and I wanted more than one possibility considered.

When the nurse called my name, I stood too quickly and caught the chair arm.

“Take your time,” she said.

I followed her down a hallway smelling faintly of disinfectant and coffee. At the scale, I asked, “Could you not say the number out loud?”

“Of course.”

Such a small accommodation. I nearly thanked her twice.

Dr. Rao entered the exam room carrying a tablet, but she set it on the desk before sitting down. I noticed that immediately. Most people who had questioned me about my body had kept their eyes on a screen.

“What would make this appointment useful to you?” she asked.

I had prepared for *What brings you in?* I had not prepared for useful.

“I want to know why I’m gaining weight,” I said. “But I also want to be able to get through a workday without feeling like somebody unplugged me.”

She waited.

“And I need you to believe I’m trying.”

My voice cracked on the last word. I looked at the folder instead of her face.

“Trying isn’t something you have to prove before you deserve an evaluation,” she said. “May I see what you brought?”

I handed it over. While she read, I watched her turn each page rather than merely lift the corners. She asked when the fatigue had begun, whether my periods had changed, whether I took supplements, whether I snored. Some questions surprised me. None sounded like a trap. When I couldn’t remember something, she gave me time to check my notes.

She stopped at an older lab report.

“Were these results discussed with you?”

“The message said there was nothing urgent.”

She moved her chair so we could look together. One thyroid result sat just beyond the laboratory’s reference range. I had seen the tiny flag beside it before, but the reassuring portal message had made me set it aside.

“This alone doesn’t establish a diagnosis,” she said. “A mild elevation can be temporary. But with the symptoms you’re describing, it deserves follow-up. I’d like to repeat your thyroid tests, including free T4, and check a few other possible contributors to the fatigue.”

“So there’s something to investigate?”

“Yes,” she said. “There is.”

I pressed my thumb into the folder’s bent corner. I wasn’t relieved exactly. I wanted normal results and an answer, two wishes that might not fit together. Still, for the first time, I could picture a next appointment that wasn’t simply another opportunity to disappoint someone.

“What should I do about training?”

“Tell me what happens when you exercise.”

I described the shaking, the prolonged soreness, the trainer urging me through another circuit.

“For now, don’t push through dizziness or unusual weakness,” she said. “We can work out an activity plan while we investigate. Movement doesn’t need to be punishment.”

I thought of the woman at my gym who always apologized before taking the lighter dumbbells. I had nodded along instead of telling her there was nothing to apologize for. Sitting there, I wondered what it would take to become the sort of trainer who asked questions before giving instructions.

Before I left, I asked when to expect the results and who would contact me. Dr. Rao wrote down the plan, then handed me the page to check.

I read it carefully.

“Can we add the leg weakness?” I asked.

She uncapped her pen. “Absolutely.”


I read the additions twice before giving the page back. Dr. Rao had written *leg weakness with exertion* beneath the list of blood tests, then added a note about contacting the clinic if the weakness worsened or came with fainting, chest pain, or trouble breathing. The warning did not frighten me as much as the absence of one would have. It made the appointment feel like a real plan instead of a polite dismissal.

“Will the activity plan come from you?” I asked.

“Partly,” she said. “Once we know more, I may refer you to physical therapy or a dietitian who understands medical weight changes. For now, I’d like you to track what you can tolerate, not what an app says you should tolerate.”

I opened my phone and showed her the exercise summary from the previous week. The colored rings were mostly gray. A red banner announced that I had missed my movement goal three days in a row.

“This makes me feel as if I’m failing,” I admitted.

“Then it isn’t useful data in its current form.” She pointed to the notes section. “Record the activity, how long you did it, and what happened afterward. If ten minutes of walking leaves you shaky for hours, that matters. If stretching helps, that matters too. The purpose is to observe your body, not grade it.”

I typed the words into my notes while she spoke. *Observe, don’t grade.* They looked almost rebellious beside the app’s demand to close my rings.

A knock sounded, and the nurse appeared with a small paper cup and a label sheet. “The lab can see you now,” she said. “Would you like someone to walk with you, or are you comfortable going down the hall?”

“I’m comfortable walking,” I said, then glanced at Dr. Rao. “As long as I can sit if I need to.”

“Absolutely,” the nurse replied. “There are chairs outside the draw room.”

She did not say it as if I were making a dramatic request. She said it as if chairs existed for exactly that reason. I tucked the plan into my folder, stood carefully, and waited for the brief gray shimmer at the edge of my vision to pass. It did. Dr. Rao watched without rushing me.

“Tell the lab technician about the weakness,” she said. “And don’t drive if you feel unsafe afterward.”

“I will.”

The nurse led me out of the endocrinology exam room and into the hallway, making the transition as ordinary as possible. The clinic’s doors opened and closed around us; phones rang at the front desk; someone’s printer clicked through a stack of insurance forms. At the lab window, I handed over my paperwork and confirmed that the results would appear in the patient portal. The staff member explained that some tests might return before others and that the clinic would contact me when Dr. Rao had reviewed them.

I almost laughed at the phrase *when Dr. Rao had reviewed them*. My previous results had arrived as an automated notification with a green checkmark and no explanation. This time I wrote down the clinic number and asked what to do if I had questions before the follow-up.

“You can send a portal message,” the nurse said. “Use the symptom details rather than just saying you feel worse. It helps the team triage it.”

That advice landed harder than she could have known. For months, I had edited myself into acceptable sentences: *I’m tired. I’m gaining weight. I’m trying.* Now I opened a blank note and began listing the details—stairs, trembling thighs, soreness that lasted two days, the point at which walking stopped feeling restorative and started feeling dangerous.

When the phlebotomist called me in, I sat in the chair with my folder on my lap. She checked my name and birth date, explained each tube, and asked which arm I preferred. I chose the left. As the needle went in, I watched the dark blood fill the first vial and thought about how much effort had gone into proving I was not secretly careless. Food logs. Weigh-ins. Workout screenshots. Apologies to trainers. Silence in examination rooms.

I knew the blood tests would not tell the whole story. Even the thyroid result, if it came back abnormal again, might be one piece rather than the answer. But investigation was different from accusation. I could feel that difference in my shoulders, which had lowered for the first time all morning.

Afterward, I remained seated until the nurse said I looked steady. She offered crackers and water. I accepted both, then sent the portal message while the instructions were fresh: *I completed the labs today. I’m also tracking the leg weakness as discussed. Please let me know if you need anything else before follow-up.*

Outside the clinic, I stopped beneath the awning instead of heading straight to the parking lot. My phone buzzed with a message from the gym reminding me about the evening circuit. I opened it, stared at the list of timed exercises, and canceled.

For once, canceling was not surrender. It was an action based on information I had gathered. I put the phone away and walked slowly toward my car, carrying Dr. Rao’s plan in my folder and a different kind of plan beginning in my head: if I ever trained people, I would ask what happened after the workout, not just whether they completed it.


At my car, I put the folder on the passenger seat and waited before turning the key. My legs felt tired, but the trembling had settled. I drank the rest of the water the nurse had given me, checked that I felt clearheaded, and drove home without stopping at the gym to explain myself.

That omission felt enormous. Usually I would have gone inside, shown my face, offered some proof that canceling had cost me something. Instead, I parked outside my apartment and carried only my tote upstairs, leaving a bag of laundry in the trunk. Halfway up, I paused on the landing. Someone had propped a delivery box against the railing; I stood beside it until my thighs stopped quivering. Upstairs, I recorded the stairs, the pause, and how long recovery seemed to take. I did not add a verdict.

My phone rang while I was taking bread out of the freezer. The gym’s number filled the screen. For a moment I considered letting it go to voicemail, but I wanted to know whether canceling had used the last session in my package.

“Hey,” my trainer said when I answered. Music thudded behind his voice. “Saw you dropped tonight. Everything okay?”

“I had a medical appointment. I’m changing my activity while we investigate the weakness.”

“Sure. We can modify. Getting here is the main thing.”

I pressed the frozen slices apart with my thumb. That had once sounded encouraging. Now I heard how little room it left for the possibility that getting there wasn’t the right goal.

“I’m not coming tonight.”

A brief silence followed.

“You’ve missed a few lately. Consistency matters more than intensity. We don’t want to lose momentum.”

I set the bread down. “I understand consistency. I’m having symptoms that need evaluating.”

“Have you been eating enough protein?”

The question might have been reasonable somewhere else, from someone else. Coming after months of doubt about what I ate, it made my face grow hot.

“My doctor and I have a plan,” I said. “I need to pause our sessions. Can the unused ones stay on my account?”

“I can ask the manager. But if you wait until you feel motivated—”

“This isn’t about motivation.”

My voice came out louder than I intended. I looked toward the open kitchen window, embarrassed even with nobody there.

“I’m not asking you to diagnose anything,” I continued. “I’m asking you to respect that I’m not doing circuits while ordinary stairs leave my legs shaking.”

The music on his end dipped, then swelled again.

“Okay,” he said. “Send the pause request through the app. I’ll flag it.”

I thanked him automatically. After we hung up, I disliked the thank-you, then disliked myself for policing even that. A conversation did not have to be perfectly brave to count. I had said what I needed and stayed with it.

In the app, the pause form required a reason. The choices were travel, injury, financial, and other. I chose other and wrote, *Medical evaluation in progress. Requesting a temporary pause in training.* I nearly attached the lab order before realizing the gym did not need my medical record. I submitted the request without it, saved the confirmation, and switched off workout reminders.

The kitchen became strangely quiet. I toasted the bread, made eggs, and ate sitting down instead of standing over the counter. My folder lay beside the plate. Under it was an older appointment summary with the same recommendation I remembered: increase physical activity. There was no mention of the stairs. I turned the page over rather than read it again.

After lunch, I opened my laptop and searched for personal-training qualifications. The results came crowded with promises: transform lives, build your brand, become your own boss. One advertisement showed a trainer shouting beside a sweating client. I scrolled past it and opened a course outline.

Anatomy. Exercise programming. Screening. Scope of practice. CPR and first aid.

Until then, becoming a trainer had been a thought I visited when I wanted to imagine a kinder gym. The list made it work: study, money, assessments, skills I did not yet have. I checked the price and winced. Then I opened a blank document titled *Questions before enrolling*.

Could I study part-time? How did practical assessments work? What training addressed chronic illness and referral to qualified clinicians? I added another question: how did a trainer learn to distinguish ordinary effort from a reason to stop, without pretending to be a doctor?

I pictured the woman who apologized for choosing lighter dumbbells. I had no idea what she needed beyond permission to choose them. That was the point. I would have to ask.

A portal notification interrupted me. My stomach tightened before I opened it. It was an acknowledgment from the clinic: my message had been received; results were still pending review.

No answer yet. I put the phone beside Dr. Rao’s instructions and added the afternoon’s fatigue to my log. Then I returned to the course website, found the admissions contact form, and typed my first question.


The admissions form remained open while the apartment darkened around me. I read my question again: *How does your program accommodate students who need to study part-time or who have a medical condition affecting exercise tolerance?* My finger hovered over the Submit button. The course website had made everything sound simple—watch the modules, pass the assessment, start helping people—but I no longer trusted simple promises.

I added a second question before sending it: *What is the boundary between a trainer’s responsibility and a clinician’s?* Then I attached no medical documents, described no diagnosis, and wrote only that I was currently undergoing evaluation for fatigue and exertional weakness. The wording felt careful rather than secretive. I was allowed to ask about a course without turning my body into an application essay.

I pressed Submit. A confirmation appeared immediately, promising that someone would respond through email. I saved a screenshot beside the clinic portal acknowledgment. Two messages, both saying essentially the same thing: received, not yet reviewed. The parallel made my chest tighten. I wanted an answer from Dr. Rao, an answer from the school, an answer from my own body. Instead, I had to tolerate the space between asking and knowing.

The next morning, I woke with the heavy sensation that had become familiar but never normal. My calves felt packed with wet sand. When I sat up, I waited for the room to steady before standing. I walked to the bathroom, brushed my teeth, and recorded the effort afterward: *short walk inside apartment; legs weak but no dizziness; rested four minutes.* The old version of me would have called that an embarrassing amount of information. Dr. Rao had called it useful data, and I was trying to believe her.

By breakfast, the gym app had sent another automated reminder. *Your body is capable of more than you think.* I stared at the sentence until its encouragement turned accusatory. Then I opened the pause request and checked its status. *Under review.* No promise about preserving the unused sessions appeared, but the request existed outside my head now. I forwarded the confirmation to myself and created a folder titled *Health and Training Records*. Into it went the clinic message, my symptom log, the gym request, and the course questions.

The organization did not cure anything. My weight had not dropped. My legs did not suddenly become reliable. But arranging the documents gave the uncertainty edges. I could see what I had reported, what had been ordered, and what remained unanswered. I was not waiting passively for a verdict; I was preparing to participate in the next conversation.

When my phone rang, I expected the gym. It was the clinic’s number. I answered carefully.

“Hello?”

“This is the clinic calling to confirm that Dr. Rao has received your message,” a staff member said. “Your laboratory results are still pending review. Some values have posted, but she wants to look at the complete panel before commenting.”

“Thank you for letting me know.” I glanced at the folder on the table. “Should I continue tracking the weakness?”

“Yes. Please include what activity you did and how you felt afterward. If you develop severe weakness, fainting, chest pain, or trouble breathing, seek urgent care.”

“I understand.”

The call ended without a diagnosis, but not without meaning. The clinic had not lost my message in a digital queue. Dr. Rao was waiting for the complete information, just as I was. I wrote down the call, including the phrase *complete panel*, and resisted the temptation to open the portal every few minutes.

Instead, I followed the activity instructions she had given me. I filled a glass of water, moved through the gentle stretches, and stopped after the second one when my thighs began to quiver. I sat on the edge of the couch and watched the timer. The shaking eased after several minutes, but the tiredness stayed. I recorded both facts. Stopping had not been failure. Continuing would not have proved courage.

An email arrived from the training program while I was writing. The subject line read, *Your admissions question has been received.* It offered no answer yet, only a reference number and a note that an advisor would respond. I laughed once, softly, at the absurdity of collecting confirmations instead of solutions. Then I added the email to the folder.

My local plan changed as I typed beneath it: *Do not enroll until I understand the schedule, practical requirements, accessibility, and scope-of-practice education.* That was not abandoning the idea of becoming a trainer. It was the first responsible step toward it.

I opened a new page and wrote the questions I would ask if an advisor called. Would students practice screening without treating it as diagnosis? Were they taught how to refer someone back to a doctor? Could a client modify an exercise without being labeled uncommitted? What did the program say about weight-neutral coaching, fatigue, and conditions that were not visible?

My hand paused over the last question. I remembered the trainers who had told me to push through, the doctors who had glanced at my weight and assumed the explanation was obvious, and the moments when I had started believing them. If I became a trainer, I could not promise every client an answer. I could promise to listen before prescribing effort.

The portal still showed *results pending review*. I left it unopened after that. I put on loose clothes, carried my water to the chair by the window, and continued the symptom log while the afternoon passed. My body remained unexplained, but my next action was clear: wait for Dr. Rao’s review, keep gathering accurate information, and learn how to make room for someone else’s truth before asking them to move.


The apartment stayed quiet after the clinic call, but quiet no longer felt restful. It gave every notification room to become important. I placed my phone face down on the table and opened the symptom log instead. The last entry read: *Gentle stretches, stopped when thighs quivered; shaking eased after several minutes; fatigue continued.* Beneath it, I added the time I had spent sitting afterward and the heaviness that remained in my legs. I was learning to record the whole effect, not just the moment that made me stop.

The scale waited beside the bathroom cabinet. I had moved it there weeks ago after avoiding it for several days, hoping proximity would make the number less frightening. It had not. My weight had climbed despite the meals I logged, the sessions I attended, and the effort I kept making when effort was supposed to explain everything. I stepped on, wrote down the result without changing the entry, and stepped off. The number was information, not a confession. I repeated the sentence silently until it sounded less like something borrowed from a wellness post.

My phone buzzed before I had returned to the couch. The gym had replied to my pause request. *We can place your membership sessions on temporary hold. Please provide a medical note and an estimated return date.* I read it twice. The message was more polite than my trainer’s comments had been, but the demand still made my shoulders tighten. I did not have a diagnosis to submit. I did not know when I would return. The entire reason for pausing was that I could not responsibly promise either.

I opened the reply box and typed, *I am currently undergoing medical evaluation, and my clinician has not provided a return date. I can provide confirmation that an evaluation is in progress, but I am not authorizing release of laboratory results or other medical records.* My finger hovered over Send. The old fear rose quickly: they will think I am difficult; they will say I am making excuses; they will cancel the sessions because I cannot prove I deserve rest.

I sent it anyway. Then I added a second sentence asking them to identify the policy requiring a return date and to confirm whether unused sessions would remain available while the request was reviewed. The wording was plain, almost formal. It did not accuse anyone. It also did not apologize. I saved a copy in the records folder and wrote, *Pause request now includes boundary about private medical information.* That was a small advance, but it was an advance I could point to.

The reply came while I was still staring at the folder. The manager had not approved the request yet, but the sessions would not be erased during review. I let out a breath I had not noticed holding. Nothing about my body had changed. The laboratory panel was still pending. Dr. Rao still had to review it. Yet the gym could no longer turn an unanswered request into a lost package while I waited.

I made tea and carried it to the desk, moving slowly because my legs felt less steady than they had before the stretches. Halfway there, a tremor began in my right thigh. I set the mug down, braced one hand against the desk, and sat before the shaking intensified. I started the timer on my phone. The weakness was not dramatic enough for a movie, only a quivering muscle and the stubborn need to stop doing something ordinary. After five minutes, I stood carefully and finished the short walk to the chair by the window. I recorded the activity, the tremor, and the recovery time.

I reopened the admissions website on my laptop. Beneath the cheerful photograph of a trainer demonstrating a squat, I reread the course description: practical coaching, client assessment, exercise modification, professional conduct. I copied the contact information into my notes and drafted a message instead of enrolling. *Before I apply, I need to understand how students are taught to respond when a client reports unusual fatigue or weakness. What is the process for referring a client back to a clinician? How are modifications handled when attendance or performance is affected by health? Are instructors prepared to teach weight-neutral coaching rather than treating weight loss as the automatic outcome?*

I read the draft aloud. The questions sounded demanding, but not unreasonable. I added one more: *Can practical assessments be adapted or rescheduled when a student is following a clinician’s activity restrictions?* Then I saved the message without sending it. My plan was no longer “sign up and hope.” It was “ask, verify, and decide.” That difference steadied me.

A second clinic notification appeared, and my stomach clenched. I opened it carefully. It only confirmed that the laboratory panel remained in process and that Dr. Rao would review the complete results when available. No diagnosis, no explanation, no sudden answer to the weight gain or the fatigue. I closed the portal and placed the phone beside the log. Waiting was still part of the plan, not evidence that I had been forgotten.

Before turning off the laptop, I wrote a local replan beneath the unanswered questions: *Pause training. Continue gentle activity only as instructed. Track exertional weakness and post-activity effects. Await laboratory results and Dr. Rao’s review. Do not enroll until the program explains its medical boundaries.* The list did not make me well, but it kept other people’s assumptions from becoming my instructions. I leaned back, tired from the short walk and the decisions, and let the apartment remain quiet without mistaking quiet for surrender.


The next morning, I opened the saved message to the training program before checking the clinic portal. My questions filled most of the screen. I removed the sentence explaining how hard I had tried to keep exercising. It had nothing to do with whether a school taught safe practice. Then I pressed Send, put the laptop aside, and walked from the chair by the window to the kitchen table, carrying only my phone. The notebook could wait until I had eaten.

Bread caught in the toaster. I eased it loose with the appliance unplugged, annoyed by how even breakfast had acquired extra steps. On the counter, my old shaker bottle stood behind the kettle. My trainer had once tapped its lid and asked whether I was counting “the little extras” when I mentioned the weight gain. At the time, I had emptied my bag to show him the label on the protein powder. Remembering that now, I left the bottle where it was and spread butter on my toast.

The clinic number appeared while I was pulling out the kitchen chair.

“Is this a good time?” Dr. Rao asked. “I’m calling from my office. Your complete panel isn’t available yet, but I’ve read your update about the weakness.”

I sat down. “It eased after I rested. I wasn’t sure whether that made it less important.”

“It matters that it improved. It also matters that it happened.”

My hand tightened around the phone. She asked whether I was weak now, whether I could walk as usual, whether there had been numbness, pain, or any other change. I answered slowly, resisting the urge to round everything down. The right-thigh shaking had stopped. The heaviness remained. I could move around the apartment, but ordinary tasks took more effort than they used to.

“Thank you for being specific,” she said. “Please don’t repeat an activity to prove the symptom happens. Your description is enough for us to act on.”

I looked at the stretch of floor between the table and the counter. Some part of me had been considering exactly that: another walk, timed more accurately, perhaps evidence no one could dismiss.

“So I don’t need a video?”

“Not if obtaining one means provoking weakness or risking a fall. If the weakness returns or worsens, contact us promptly so we can decide where you need to be assessed. Sudden or persistent one-sided weakness needs emergency assessment, especially with speech or facial changes. Don’t wait for a portal reply.”

I repeated the instructions back. She corrected one detail, and I wrote them on the clean edge of an envelope. Then I asked the question I had avoided at every appointment before hers.

“If the thyroid results don’t explain this, what happens?”

“Then we keep evaluating,” she said. “We review what they do tell us, examine you again as needed, and consider other causes. A test that doesn’t explain a symptom doesn’t make the symptom disappear.”

The refrigerator hummed behind me. I watched a drop of water slide down the kettle and tried to answer without crying.

“The other doctor said I probably wasn’t aware of how much I was eating.”

“Food and activity can be relevant to health,” she said. “But they aren’t substitutes for investigating a change in weight accompanied by fatigue and weakness. You deserve that investigation.”

Nothing in the kitchen changed. My toast had gone cold. My legs still felt heavy beneath the table. Yet I no longer had to spend the whole conversation establishing that there was a problem. We could talk about what to do next.

Before we ended the call, I asked whether the clinic could provide a brief letter confirming that I was undergoing evaluation and following activity restrictions, without including results or a diagnosis. She said I could request it through the office. She could not give the gym a reliable return date yet.

“That’s what I told them,” I said.

“That was a reasonable answer.”

Afterward, I stayed at the table and ate. I did not refresh the portal. Dr. Rao was still reviewing an incomplete picture, and I was still following the plan; neither fact required me to sit poised over a screen.

An email from the training program arrived as I cleared my plate. The admissions office had attached its accommodation procedure and a sample section on client screening. Practical assessments required demonstrated coaching competence, the message explained, but requests to adapt how that competence was demonstrated went through an individual review. Approval was not automatic. The reply also stated that trainers did not diagnose unexplained symptoms or prescribe treatment.

I opened the attachment. One sample scenario described a client reporting dizziness during exercise. The answer began with stopping the activity, not lowering the weight and trying again. I read the paragraph twice, then marked a question about who supervised referral decisions during practical training. This was useful evidence, not yet a reason to pay a deposit.

Beside it, I started a note for the trainer I hoped to become: *Ask what happened. Listen to the answer. Know when coaching needs to stop.*

I nearly added *Make them feel believed*, then paused. Belief needed something concrete behind it. I replaced the sentence with: *Do not ask them to reproduce a symptom to earn help.*


The sentence stayed on the screen while I rinsed my plate. *Do not ask them to reproduce a symptom to earn help.* I dried my hands and read it again. It sounded obvious written down. So had stopping when something hurt, until a trainer stood over me with a stopwatch and called stopping a habit. I saved the note, then carried my laptop from the kitchen to the desk beside the living-room window. The accommodation attachment was difficult to read on my phone, and I wanted the questions and the policy open together.

Outside, someone in running clothes waited at the crossing, bouncing lightly on their toes. I watched until the light changed, then adjusted my chair. There had been a time when seeing another person exercise made me feel accused. Even now, I caught myself calculating what I ought to have done before breakfast. I put both feet on the floor and opened the document instead.

The procedure asked applicants to describe which parts of the course might present barriers. Beneath that was a blank field labeled *Requested adjustment*. My fingers rested on the keyboard. I knew how to describe the person I had been trying to appear to be: dependable, energetic, never missing a session. Describing what I needed was harder. I typed, *I am undergoing medical evaluation for fatigue and weakness. I cannot currently commit to unrestricted exercise demonstrations.*

I stopped before adding the weight gain. That belonged in my medical history; it did not explain what access to a classroom required. Instead, I listed questions about seated participation, rest breaks, and rescheduling physical demonstrations while restrictions remained in place. I specified that I was asking what could be considered, not assuming approval. Then I reached the supervision question I had marked earlier and called the number in the admissions email.

The person who answered transferred me to a course adviser. Hold music crackled through my speaker while I found a pen. When the adviser came on, I read the dizziness scenario aloud.

“What happens if a student recognizes a problem but the client wants to continue?” I asked. “During practice, who backs up the decision to stop?”

“The instructor is responsible for supervising the session,” she said. “A student should stop the activity and bring the instructor over. They aren’t expected to make a medical diagnosis.”

“And if the instructor thinks they’re being overcautious?”

There was a pause, filled by keyboard taps.

“Are you asking about an incident here?”

“No. I’m trying to understand the procedure before I apply.”

She directed me to the section on incident reporting, then explained that a student could raise a safety concern with the course lead if it remained unresolved. I wrote down the role, not just the assurance. It was not a promise that everyone would listen. It was a route I could check.

“I’m interested in coaching,” I said, “but I’m currently following activity restrictions. I don’t want to enroll on the understanding that I’ll push through them.”

“Please don’t,” she said. “The accommodation team would need to review your request. We can discuss possible formats, but I can’t approve them over this call.”

The answer disappointed me in a small, practical way. I wanted certainty before risking an application fee or imagining myself in the classroom. Still, she had not asked whether I was sufficiently motivated. I asked her to email the supervision policy and confirm whether I could obtain an accommodation decision before making a nonrefundable payment. She said she would check the payment procedure rather than guess.

After we hung up, I sat with the pen uncapped. My old trainer had made uncertainty sound like a weakness of character. The first doctor had made it sound like a gap in my food diary. Now I had a page of questions that could be answered by someone other than me. I did not have to supply every answer through greater effort.

I opened the clinic portal and requested the brief letter Dr. Rao had offered: evaluation in progress, current activity restrictions, no reliable return date yet. I asked whether the office needed separate requests for the gym and the training program. I left out the long explanation I had drafted about why each mattered. Her last words returned with surprising clarity: *That was a reasonable answer.*

My shoulders ached from sitting forward. I leaned back, leaving the pen on the desk rather than getting up to find another task. The room held its ordinary clutter: laundry over a chair, a charging cable looped beneath the window, the gym bag I had not unpacked. None of it resembled the bright training studios in the course brochure.

I pulled up my future-coaching note beside the notebook and added a question beneath the sentence about symptoms: *What would make this session workable for you?*

Then I tried saying it aloud. The first attempt sounded rehearsed. The second sounded like a question I could wait to hear answered. I left space underneath it. Before I could learn to guide anyone through a session, I needed to learn what to do with that space.


I left the note open beneath the accommodation form and tried to imagine a classroom that did not require me to hide. The course brochure showed trainers demonstrating squats beside racks of polished weights, smiling as if every body arrived with the same instruction manual. I had wanted that world badly enough to tolerate the trainer who told me attendance was a character issue and the doctor who treated my weight gain as proof that I was careless. Wanting it still did not mean I had to enter it unsafely.

A notification appeared from the clinic portal. I reached for the mouse, then stopped with my hand above the desk. The impulse to open it immediately felt physical, like a twitch. I had promised myself I would not turn every message into an emergency, but the letter request mattered. I clicked.

Dr. Rao had uploaded a document.

I read it once without understanding much beyond the heading. Then I enlarged the text. It stated that I was under active clinical evaluation for fatigue, weakness, and unexplained weight change. It confirmed that I had been advised to avoid unrestricted exercise and to stop activity if unusual weakness, dizziness, or other concerning symptoms occurred. It did not claim a diagnosis. It did not offer a return-to-training date. It did not make me sound unreliable.

At the bottom, Dr. Rao had added a sentence in the clinical section: *The patient’s reports are being taken seriously and are guiding ongoing evaluation.*

I stared at that line until the letters blurred. A medical letter was supposed to establish facts for other people, not repair the damage done by earlier appointments. Yet that sentence reached somewhere the test orders had not. It named the thing I had spent months trying to prove: I was not inventing a problem to escape effort.

The portal included a message from the nurse explaining that the letter could be downloaded for the training program and gym, but that I should send only the minimum information each organization required. She repeated Dr. Rao’s instructions about contacting the clinic if the weakness returned or worsened and seeking urgent assessment for sudden one-sided weakness, speech changes, or facial drooping. I typed back that I understood. Before sending, I added, “Thank you for explaining what to watch for without asking me to recreate it.” The nurse replied with a brief acknowledgment and a reminder to rest.

I downloaded the letter into a folder labeled *medical documents*, then made two copies with different filenames. One included “training-program accommodation”; the other included “gym activity restriction.” The distinction felt oddly powerful. I was not handing over my entire history to anyone who asked. I was choosing what information belonged to which decision.

The admissions email arrived while I was doing that. The subject line made my stomach tighten: *Accommodation Inquiry—Next Steps*. I opened it carefully. The program could conduct an individual review before payment, the adviser had confirmed. I would need to submit the letter and describe the practical activities affected. The review might result in modified demonstrations, alternate observation-based assessments, delayed physical components, or a determination that some requirements could not be adapted. No payment was due while the review was pending.

I read the paragraph twice, searching for the catch. There was one, but it was an honest one: nothing was guaranteed until the accommodation team assessed the request. Still, the procedure gave me a path that did not begin with surrendering money and hoping compassion appeared afterward. I attached the limited letter, wrote that I could participate in theory, screening, communication, and observation-based work, and stated again that I could not commit to unrestricted exercise demonstrations. Before sending, I added the supervision question: if an adapted practical assessment involved a client scenario, who would make the final decision to stop or refer?

For one breath, I heard my old trainer’s voice telling me that serious people did not negotiate with discomfort. Then I remembered Dr. Rao saying that a symptom did not have to be reproduced to count. I pressed the button.

The confirmation page gave me a reference number and stated that the team would contact me through the admissions office. I copied it into my notebook beneath the clinic request number. Two separate processes now had records, names, and obligations. Neither depended on me sounding cheerful enough to deserve help.

I pushed the laptop back and opened my coaching note. Under *What would make this session workable for you?* I wrote: *Ask before touching equipment. Offer choices without making the client defend them. Stop when they report unusual symptoms. Explain what happens next. Document the concern. Bring in qualified medical or supervisory support instead of pretending certainty.*

My legs felt heavy after sitting, so I stood slowly and walked to the window, using the desk edge only for balance. Across the street, the runner from earlier had disappeared. The sidewalk was ordinary again: a delivery cart, a bent sign, leaves gathered near the curb. I did not measure how long I could stand there. I watched my breathing settle, then returned to the desk and saved the note.

The application was not yet approved. The cause of my symptoms was not yet known. But the letter, the inquiry, and the questions I had learned to ask moved together now. I was no longer waiting for someone else to decide whether my experience qualified as real. I was building a record—and, slowly, a way of caring for people that did not require them to suffer convincingly first.


The reference number was still in my open notebook when the admissions office called. For a moment I assumed it was a recorded reminder and let the phone vibrate against the desk. Then the screen displayed the program’s general number, and I sat straighter without forcing myself to look composed. I opened the folder containing Dr. Rao’s limited letter, the accommodation form, and my notes about supervision. When I answered, I kept the phone on speaker so I could write without pressing it to my ear.

“Hi, this is the admissions office calling about your accommodation inquiry,” a voice said. “Is now still an appropriate time?”

“Yes,” I said, though my pulse had quickened. “I can talk.”

The admissions representative confirmed that my documents had been received and read back the activity restrictions as she understood them: no unrestricted exercise demonstrations, and stop for unusual weakness or dizziness. She also confirmed the supervision guidance: no expectation that I diagnose a client or determine a referral alone. Hearing the words repeated by someone who had not been in the room for any of my humiliating appointments made them feel less fragile.

“I want to make sure the practical requirements are evaluated accurately,” I said. “I can participate in instruction, screening theory, communication practice, observation, and written work. What I cannot do right now is demonstrate every movement on demand or push through symptoms so an assessor can decide whether I’m committed.”

“That distinction is helpful,” she said. “The accommodation team may ask the course lead whether demonstrations can be completed by an instructor, partner, or video resource. They may also consider delayed physical components. I can’t promise the outcome, but your inquiry is being reviewed before payment.”

I wrote *before payment* in the margin, underlining it once. The last gym had charged me for a package while telling me that the solution was to attend more consistently. When I later asked to pause it, the trainer had said the unused sessions were evidence that I did not want change badly enough. I had walked home carrying the receipt like an accusation. This call was not undoing that, but it was giving me a chance to enter a new system with its boundaries visible.

“There’s one more question,” I said. “If an adapted assessment involves a client scenario and I notice symptoms or a safety concern, who has final authority to stop the activity?”

“The instructor supervising the assessment,” she replied. “The student should stop the activity and alert them. If the instructor is unavailable or the concern isn’t addressed, the student can report it to the course lead through the incident process.”

“And would using that process affect my evaluation?”

“No. Raising a safety concern should not be treated as a failure of motivation.”

The sentence landed so sharply that I had to look away from the screen. My old trainer had turned every boundary into a referendum on my character. Even doctors had sometimes seemed to join him, asking whether I was exercising enough before asking what the exertion did to me. Dr. Rao had reversed the order. She had asked what happened, when it happened, and what could be done safely while the cause remained unknown. Her letter had not solved my fatigue or explained the weight that had gathered around my body despite my efforts. It had done something smaller and more immediate: it had stopped other people from pretending uncertainty meant permission to dismiss me.

The representative asked whether I wanted the request to remain open while the accommodation team consulted the course lead. I said yes. She said she would send them through the admissions portal and reminded me that approval was not guaranteed. I thanked her for saying that plainly.

After the call ended, I read through my notes. The room seemed too still around the words *not guaranteed*. I felt the familiar urge to solve the uncertainty by volunteering for more than I could safely do. If I offered to demonstrate just one session, perhaps two, I could prove I was reasonable. I could make the team’s decision easier by making my body carry the risk.

Instead, I closed the laptop and stood. I carried my mug from the desk into the kitchen, moving slowly enough to notice the heaviness in my legs without testing it. The kettle clicked on. While it heated, I opened the notes app on my phone and dictated a message to myself: *An accommodation is not a favor if the standard activity creates a documented barrier. Asking for a safe format is part of professional responsibility.*

Back at the desk, I reopened the laptop and added a new section to my coaching note titled *When a client is uncertain*. I wrote that I would not demand a perfect explanation before responding to a report of weakness, pain, breathlessness, dizziness, or sudden change. I would ask what they needed in that moment, stop the activity, and involve the appropriate supervisor or medical professional. I would never use another person’s weight, attendance, or enthusiasm as evidence against their symptoms.

The admissions portal refreshed while I was saving the note. I copied the update beside the reference number, then leaned back and let my hands rest flat on the desk.

The program still might decide that its practical requirements could not be adapted. The medical investigation still had no final answer. But I had moved the application forward without abandoning the restrictions that kept me safe. I was not asking to be excused from becoming a trainer. I was insisting that becoming one begin with the kind of care I had needed all along.


The admissions portal refreshed while I was still sitting at the desk. I expected another acknowledgment, perhaps a reminder that the review remained pending. Instead, a green banner appeared beneath the reference number: *Accommodation review complete.*

The program could not promise that every future course would use the same format, but it had documented this decision and identified whom I could contact if an instructor treated the accommodation as optional.

I had not solved the cause of my weight gain, fatigue, or weakness. I had not become an expert overnight.
