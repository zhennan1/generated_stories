## Chapter 1: The Last Song

The city had forgotten how to shine.

Anahya remembered light in its windows: blue lamps nursed in shells, green fire running along the bridges, the soft gold of creatures that rose from the trenches to feed. As a child she had followed those lights home, slipping between the towers while her mother called after her to slow down.

Now she found her way by touch.

Her fingers passed over a balcony whitened to bone. The coral crumbled beneath them. She drew her hand away and watched the powder drift through the water, settling over the street where no one had walked in nineteen years.

Her gills contracted.

She stopped swimming until the spasm passed. Four narrow slits beneath each ear, once delicate as folded silk, had thickened into ridges of scar. Water scraped through them. Some days the burning faded into the background of her body. Today each breath felt sifted through ground glass.

Above the avenue, a translucent bag opened and closed in the current.

For a moment she mistook it for a moon jelly.

Even now.

Anahya hooked it with her spear and pulled it from the doorway it had snagged. Beneath it lay a nursery shell, large enough for three infants. Someone had carved little fish around the rim. She remembered the custom: every visitor added one, until the sleeping child rested inside a shoal.

She released the bag. There was nowhere to put it that was not the sea.

At the city's western arch she paused beside a broken pillar and loosened the cord around her throat. The filter hung there in a cage of woven wire: charcoal, shell grit, and a folded scrap of surface cloth. She pressed it against the intake above her gills and drew a cautious breath.

The water tasted of metal.

When she tipped the cage, black sludge oozed between the wires.

“No,” she said.

The word scarcely traveled.

She took the filter apart, working carefully despite the trembling in her hands. The cloth had rotted through. Her last piece. She rinsed it anyway, as though care might persuade it to hold together, and watched it separate into soft threads.

Beyond the arch, the open water lay dim and empty.

There was a wreck near the shore. She had seen it three tides ago, its lower chambers lodged on a reef of concrete, its upper decks lifted clear of the sea. Surface vessels sometimes carried fine-meshed filters in their pumping rooms. She had avoided this one because reaching it meant crossing the shallows, where the water grew warm and bitter enough to strip the skin from her palms.

Now she gathered the useless cloth and tucked it into her belt.

Before leaving, she turned toward the city.

The calling-song began low, with a note felt through the teeth before it reached the water. Her mother had taught her to sing it when currents separated them in the market. Three rising phrases; a pause; then the long descending invitation.

Here. I am here.

Anahya sang into the empty avenue.

Her voice moved through roofless halls and under fallen bridges. It touched the nursery shell, the pillars, the pale gardens where her people had once tended food among the coral.

She held the final note until her gills seized.

Then she listened.

A loose shutter tapped somewhere below. Sand whispered across stone.

She waited longer than she needed to.

At last she turned west.

The journey took most of the afternoon. She kept close to the seabed, where the current was weaker, passing fields of tires and a fishing net stretched between two towers like abandoned lace. Nothing struggled in it. There had been years when she cut such nets open. Later, she had searched them for food.

Now she scarcely looked.

As the bottom rose, sunlight thickened to a tarnished yellow. Her tail stirred a sediment that smelled of old fuel. Ahead, the vessel's shadow lay across the sand.

It was larger than she remembered.

She entered through a split in its side, spear first. Inside, her movements disturbed flakes of rust that spun like tiny red leaves. Pipes crowded the ceiling. She followed them through a narrow passage, testing doors until one gave way with a shriek conducted through the hull.

The pumping room was half crushed.

For a while she could only stare at the wreckage.

Then she wedged her spear beneath a buckled panel and pulled.

The coral shaft bent. Pain opened along her thumb. She braced her tail against the wall and pulled again, baring her teeth, until the panel shifted enough to free a cylindrical housing.

Inside lay a roll of pale, tightly woven mesh.

Anahya touched it with one finger.

It held.

She laughed once, a startled burst that hurt her throat, and cradled the cylinder against her chest.

By the time she reached the surface, the sky had darkened.

Air struck her wet face, bitter with oil and ash. She could breathe a little through her mouth, enough to rest her gills, but it left a dry ache behind her ribs. Beyond the wreck stretched a shoreline of cracked mud and dead reeds. Buildings stood farther inland, their windows black. No smoke rose from them. No birds moved above them.

A low rumble passed across the water.

She looked up.

Clouds were folding over one another beyond the headland. The wind caught her hair and flung it across her eyes. Already the tide was lifting, slapping hard against the vessel's exposed ribs.

Anahya pushed the cylinder onto a sloping deck, then hauled herself after it. Rust bit into her elbows. Her tail dragged heavily without the water to support it.

“Come on,” she whispered, to herself or the salvage.

She rolled the cylinder beneath a tilted sheet of hull and crawled into the shelter beside it.

There she fitted a square of new mesh into her filter, saving every scrap. When she lowered herself far enough to wet her gills, the first filtered breath still burned, but less.

She closed her eyes.

Rain began to strike the metal overhead. Beneath its gathering clatter, she caught herself listening for another swimmer approaching the wreck, someone drawn by her song.

Anahya pulled the cylinder closer, away from the rain, and made room beside it anyway.

## Chapter 2: The Two-Faced Land Creature

Something scraped above the rain.

Anahya stopped winding the spare mesh around its cylinder. Beneath her shelter of buckled hull, seawater rose and fell against her ribs. The sound came again: a slow dragging, followed by a small hard click.

Not wreckage shifting. Something choosing where to put its weight.

She tucked the mesh beneath a strut and reached for her spear.

Lightning bleached the deck. For a heartbeat she saw only rivets, torn cable, the white ribs of a broken chair. Then a shape moved beside the shoreward rail.

It stood on two legs.

Anahya had seen land bodies before, sealed in the cabins of drowned ships or caught in the windows of sunken towers. This one wore strips of cloth bound around narrow limbs. Its bare feet gripped the sloping iron.

Above its shoulders were two faces.

One turned toward her with eyes so old she thought of stones worn hollow by tides. Water ran from them, tracing a shining cheek before falling from the jaw. The other face angled toward the shore. Its lips were cracked black, and with each breath a little grey ash slipped between them.

The watching face blinked.

Anahya lunged.

Her spear caught in the torn edge of her shelter. She wrenched it free, scraping her knuckles, and drove herself across the flooded deck with a blow of her tail. The creature stumbled backward. Her point tore the cloth beneath its shoulder.

It cried out in two voices.

Then brine struck her.

The blast came from the weeping face, hard enough to whip her head sideways. Ash followed, hot against her eyelids. She fell into the deck water, choking, her wounded gills clamping shut.

Through the sting she saw its hand reaching toward her.

She stabbed at it.

The creature snatched its wrist away. Coral struck iron and splintered at the tip. Anahya swung the shaft across its knees. One leg folded, and it crashed against the rail with a sound she felt in her teeth.

“No,” it said.

The word was rough, almost lost beneath the rain.

She knew that word. Surface speech, learned from the salvage recordings her mother had once forbidden her to touch.

“No,” she answered, and thrust again.

It caught the shaft between both palms. For an instant they strained together, close enough that she smelled salt beneath the burnt reek of its breath. The weeping eyes stared into hers. The other face coughed cinders over her arm.

She twisted the spear. Its palms slipped, leaving dark streaks on the coral.

It kicked her in the chest.

Pain burst through her gill scars. She slid backward into a collapsed hatch, striking her hip against its rim. Above her, the creature staggered away rather than following.

That frightened her more. It could afford to wait.

The ship groaned.

Beyond the rail, the sea had withdrawn from the mudflats. The storm wind faltered, and in that sudden thinning of noise Anahya heard a deeper sound: water breaking against the outer shoals.

She turned.

A wall of grey rose beyond the wreck.

Her spare mesh lay beneath the hull sheet.

She flung herself toward it as the first water struck. Her fingers closed around the cylinder; then the deck tilted beneath her and the shelter tore loose.

The wave took everything.

Iron scraped her back. She lost the spear, found it again by its trailing cord, and wrapped the cord around her wrist. Something struck her shoulder—a foot, a hand. The two-faced creature tumbled past, limbs spread helplessly in the churn.

Anahya kicked for deeper water.

Instead the surge hurled her inland.

Mud, stones, a length of wire flashed beneath her. She struck a submerged wall and rolled through an opening where a door had once stood. The creature slammed into her. Together they plunged down shallow steps into darkness.

Behind them came a cracking like ice breaking.

Anahya dragged herself aside as blocks fell through the doorway. One struck the creature’s leg. Its scream became a mouthful of seawater.

Then the entrance collapsed.

For a while there was only the thunder of water forcing itself between stones.

Anahya surfaced beneath a low ceiling and drew a painful breath. Dim light entered through cracks overhead. The chamber smelled of wet mineral, old salt, and ash.

The creature was struggling beside the fallen blocks, one leg pinned. Its weeping face dipped beneath the water. The other coughed against the stone.

She lifted her spear.

It saw her. Both hands rose, empty.

“No,” it whispered.

She could leave it. The water was rising; soon its legs would not matter.

Anahya looked toward the room behind her. Wide steps descended beneath the flood. Along their edges, carved crescent shells overlapped in a pattern she knew.

Merfolk work.

She looked back at the creature. Its fingers scrabbled uselessly against the block.

With the broken spear braced beneath the stone, she pushed.

It stared at her.

“Pull,” she said in the surface tongue.

The creature dragged its leg free. She let the stone drop, and they recoiled from each other, sending small waves across the chamber.

It climbed onto a ledge. Anahya remained in the water, clutching her mesh cylinder against her belly. Blood seeped from a long scrape along her flank. Across from her, the creature held its injured leg.

“Why?” it asked.

She had no answer she wanted to give.

Above them, thunder rolled through the buried building. Anahya recognized another carving beside the ledge: a circle pierced by seven narrow rays. An observatory mark. She had never seen one above the seabed.

The creature followed her gaze but said nothing.

“You live here?” she asked.

It shook its head. Ash drifted onto its wet chest.

“Others?”

Again the head moved.

“Gone,” the weeping mouth said.

The other mouth worked before adding, “No place.”

Anahya loosened her grip on the spear.

The creature touched its chest. “Khel.”

She listened to water dripping from the ruined entrance.

“Anahya,” she said at last.

Khel repeated it carefully, both voices catching on the middle syllable. Then he gestured toward the water behind her, toward the invisible sea.

“Yours?”

She understood what he was asking.

Her jaw tightened. “Gone.”

He lowered his hands.

Between them, rainwater threaded through the ceiling into a shallow hollow in the ledge. Khel cupped some and drank. After a moment he held out a trembling palmful to her.

Anahya did not take it.

But when he shifted farther along the stone, leaving room near the steps, she did not raise her spear again.

## Chapter 3: The Drowned Observatory

The water rose another finger’s width over the step between them.

Anahya watched it swallow a carved shell. Beyond the fallen masonry, the storm struck the buried entrance with a sound like stones grinding in a giant mouth.

“Other way?” Khel asked.

His ash-mouth formed the words. His other face watched the water.

“Perhaps.”

She did not know the word for observatory in his language. Instead she touched the seven-rayed mark with her spear, then pointed into the dark.

Khel hauled himself upright. His injured leg shook beneath him. For a moment she thought he would ask her help, and stiffened against the possibility, but he caught the wall.

They followed it inward, Anahya swimming above the submerged floor while Khel felt for footing along a narrow ledge. Beneath her filter, each breath dragged against the bruising in her chest. She kept the mesh cylinder pressed beneath one arm. Whatever else this place contained, she would not lose the thing that might keep her alive.

The passage opened into a round chamber.

Through a crack high overhead came a blade of stormlight. It crossed the water and broke against a wall of stars.

Anahya stopped.

Not stars: holes cut into black stone, each cupping a pale mineral bead. Silver lines joined them into constellations she knew from the undersides of temple ceilings. The Swimming Mother. The Net Without Knots. The Seven Returning.

Here, though, there were more stars than she remembered. Some constellations had been crossed out and carved again.

She swam closer. Small letters curved beneath the charts.

Her mother had taught her those letters by tracing them in sand. Anahya had not read a sentence written by another hand in nineteen years.

“Third correction,” she whispered. “The shore moves east beneath the winter light.”

“What?”

“Writing.”

She touched the next line.

“Observations shared at the lower gate.”

Shared with whom?

Below the charts, a mural ran around the chamber. Merfolk rose from a patterned sea toward figures standing on steps. Land-dwellers. Their hands extended toward one another, holding objects too weathered to name.

Anahya scraped silt away with her thumb.

Her mother’s stories had begun with the poison. Always the poison: rivers turned bitter, hatcheries emptied, children coughing blood into their mothers’ hands. Shorefolk had poured death into the sea, and the sea had endured until it could endure no more.

There had been no steps. No offered hands.

“Your people?” Khel asked.

“Yes.”

“And those?”

She did not answer.

At the chamber’s center stood a stone table. A crystal grew from it, tall as her forearm, cloudy within. Khel leaned against the table to spare his injured leg. His wet fingers slipped into a groove beside the crystal.

A low note trembled through the water.

Anahya felt it inside her teeth.

“Move.”

Khel tried. The groove shone beneath his palm.

The crystal answered with another note.

She knew that sound. Memory houses had once kept voices in resonance stone. Her mother had taken her to hear a dead singer, and afterward Anahya had dreamed for weeks that someone lived inside the walls.

“Let go!”

“I am—”

Light opened between his fingers.

The chamber vanished.

***

She stood in water without feeling it.

Above her, towers shone along a living shore. Boats moved between white piers. Beneath them spread gardens of coral, thick with fish, their colors so fierce that she reached out before she remembered these things were dead.

The vision lurched.

A river mouth emptied black water into the bay.

Fish turned on their sides. A merwoman lifted a child from a nursery bed, shaking him as though sleep were something she could dislodge. Onshore, men in sealed masks rolled barrels down a ramp.

Anahya’s grip tightened on her spear.

Here. This she knew.

Then the water darkened with bodies gathering.

Merfolk stood in rings around enormous stones. Their mouths opened together. She could not hear the words, only the force of them: a pressure that bent the seabed, that made the bones inside her head ache.

Light spread through carved channels.

Offshore, the harbor floor rose.

A tower leaned, slowly at first. People crowded its windows. The pier split beneath running feet, and the sea climbed the streets as though called by name.

Stop, Anahya tried to say.

A merfolk singer faltered. Another seized her wrist and pulled her back into the ring.

The tower fell.

The vision broke into fragments.

Ships trailing poison.

Coral citadels collapsing beneath iron charges.

Merfolk turning currents against crowded evacuation boats.

A shore child holding an empty bowl.

A sea child floating among pale anemones.

Anahya shut her eyes. The images continued.

At last she saw the observatory whole. Water filled its lower chambers; dry stairs rose toward open doors. Sea and shore had met here once. Now both approaches were barricaded.

Far below, something vast was being sung awake.

Far above, fire crossed the sky.

The two forces met.

There was no explosion she could understand. The horizon folded. For one impossible instant, stars shone beneath the seabed and waves broke across a sky full of roots.

Then the boundary tore.

Water rushed through streets that were no longer streets. Ash descended into the deep without cooling. Voices overlapped—thousands of them, speaking words she could understand only because grief needed so few.

I did not know.

I should have stopped.

Come back.

In the torn place, the voices gathered around a small, unfinished shape. Brine ran over it. Ash clothed it. Two profiles strained from the same darkness, facing away from each other.

Anahya saw Khel reach toward them.

Or perhaps they reached toward him.

***

The crystal went dark.

She struck the table shoulder-first. Her spear clattered away beneath the water.

Khel was on his knees upon the ledge. Both his hands were lifted, though nothing held them now. Seawater streamed from one face. The other coughed a thin grey cloud.

For a while neither spoke.

Anahya found her spear by touch. She needed the familiar shaft beneath her fingers.

“Was that you?” she asked.

Khel looked at the crystal.

“I don’t know.”

“The thing at the end.”

His mouths tightened. “I dream it.”

She waited.

“Before I had feet. Before I woke.” He pressed a palm against his chest. “All wanting to take it back.”

Anahya looked toward the mural. The offered hands had become visible where she had brushed away the silt.

“We didn’t,” she began.

The words would not finish.

She remembered the singer dragging her companion back into place. The windows crowded with people. Nothing in the vision made the poisoned nurseries less true. Nothing let her put the falling tower aside.

Khel shifted against the stone, leaving room on the ledge.

This time, when Anahya pulled herself out of the water beside him, she did not need to be invited.

## Chapter 4: The Thorn Reef

The water reached the ledge before either of them spoke.

Anahya watched it darken the stone beneath her tail. Somewhere beyond the blocked entrance, the storm was forcing more sea into the observatory. Each pulse brought a taste of rust through her filter.

Khel drew his injured leg closer.

“We should move.”

She wanted to remain beside the dead crystal until it gave her a different history. Instead she took up her broken spear.

Beyond the round chamber, a passage descended beneath an arch. Seven rays had been cut into its crown. The water within was still, untouched by the storm’s breathing.

Anahya slipped from the ledge.

“Wait.”

Khel stood awkwardly, bracing a cut palm against the wall.

“I’m looking,” she said. “Not leaving.”

The distinction troubled her as soon as she made it.

She swam through the arch, trailing one hand along the stone. Her fingers found shallow hollows where lamps had once grown. At the passage’s end she surfaced in a narrow room beneath a sloping ceiling. A stair emerged beside her, rising toward a sealed door. Along the walls stood shelves of clay cylinders, most split by roots or pressure.

Khel followed along a submerged step, the water rising to his chest. His ash-breathing face coughed; grey dust settled briefly on the surface.

“Air above,” Anahya told him. “Beside the door.”

While he climbed, she examined the shelves.

Storm records. Tidal corrections. Names of observers, paired in columns. Some were names her mouth knew how to shape. Others were written in the angular shore-script she had seen on salvage.

On the lowest shelf, a cylinder lay wrapped in thin copper bands. Its cap bore a branching mark unlike the observatory’s seven rays.

She broke the mineral crust with her spear butt.

Inside was a rolled sheet of beaten silver.

“Something useful?” Khel asked.

She spread it against a stair. Letters emerged beneath her thumb, fine and close as fish bones.

“‘Final keeping,’” she read. “‘For those who come after the gardens.’”

Her hand stopped.

Below the words was a reef, etched in branching black strokes. Around it, currents curled through a chain of islands toward a narrow opening in the seabed.

“The Thorn Reef.”

Khel lowered himself one step.

“What is it?”

She read farther, silently at first.

The record described a sheltered place beyond the Barrier’s Wound, where the old gardeners had carried their last living colonies. The reef had been seeded with the memory of the ocean before the blight: its seasons, its countless small hungers, the exchanges by which one life sustained another.

“‘What survives here may teach broken waters to sustain life again,’” she read aloud.

For a moment she could hear shrimp clicking in the walls of her childhood room.

Then her gills contracted. She bent over her filter, dragging water through charcoal and grit until the pain loosened.

“Alive?” Khel asked.

“When this was written.”

“But perhaps still.”

“There were gardens everywhere once.”

“This one was hidden.”

She stared at the etched branches. Beneath them were two marks: a merfolk name, and another in shore-script.

Her nails pressed into the silver.

“They knew,” she said.

“Who?”

“Someone knew there was somewhere living things might survive. While we were starving in the cities.”

“We don’t know what happened to them.”

“We know what happened to us.”

Khel’s weeping face turned toward her. The other remained in profile, its cracked mouth tight.

“We saw what happened to both.”

She struck the sheet flat against the stair.

“Do not make them equal.”

“I didn’t.”

“They poured poison into our hatcheries. My mother wrapped cloth around my throat because I could no longer breathe our own water.”

“I believe you.”

“If there are shorefolk left, they should have to see it. Every empty room. Every child we carried out.”

“And then?”

She looked at the spear across her lap.

Khel saw.

“You would carry the war to whoever happened to survive it?”

“You can breathe above it,” she said. “You can climb away.”

He flinched as though she had raised the spear.

For a while there was only water ticking from the shelves.

“Where?” he asked.

Anahya said nothing.

“Where should I climb?”

Ash sifted down his chest.

“I have walked until my feet split. I have spoken into houses because sometimes wind moves a door and I think someone is inside.” Both mouths faltered. “Once I stayed beside a well for three days because it answered.”

“An echo.”

“I know that now.”

Her anger remained, but she could no longer hold it toward him.

She looked down at the silver sheet. A tear from his other face struck the stair beside it.

“I sing,” she said.

He waited.

“Every morning I can bear it. My mother’s calling-song.” She rubbed a crust of salt from the edge of the record. “Sometimes I stop halfway through. To listen before my own voice spoils the silence.”

“Has anything answered?”

“No.”

The word seemed worse spoken to someone.

Khel settled on the step, leaving room between them.

“I cannot give them back,” he said. “I don’t know what I can give. But if that reef is alive, perhaps we could help it remain alive.”

“You don’t even know what it needs.”

“No.”

“Neither do I.”

“Then we would have to learn.”

She almost laughed. Two injured creatures in a flooded ruin, proposing to learn how to mend the sea.

Yet she bent over the map again.

A dotted channel led from the observatory’s lower gate toward the drowned coast. Beside it, a small instruction read: Release inward latch before ebb.

Anahya looked toward the sealed door above them. A green-crusted lever rested in a recess beside its frame.

“We may have a way out.”

Khel followed her gaze.

“And after?”

She carefully rolled the silver, preserving the route inside.

“I’m not forgiving anyone.”

“I didn’t ask.”

“And if it’s dead, we leave.”

He nodded.

She tucked the record into the cylinder beside her spare mesh. Then she offered him the unbroken end of her spear to help him stand.

“We’ll go and see.”

## Chapter 5: Submerged Highways

The lever would not move until they pulled together.

Anahya braced her tail against the stair. Khel planted his uninjured foot above hers, both faces tightening with the effort. Green crust broke beneath their hands. Somewhere inside the wall, a weight fell.

The sealed door opened inward.

Water knocked Khel backward. Anahya caught his wrist as his feet left the steps, and for a moment they hung in the rushing dark, tethered to the lever by her other hand. Then the pressure eased. Beyond the doorway, pale daylight trembled over a submerged passage.

“Hold my shoulder,” she said.

He obeyed.

She swam slowly enough to spare his lungs. At the passage’s end, they surfaced beneath an arch whose seven rays had been worn almost smooth. Behind them the observatory settled into silence. Ahead lay the drowned coast.

Two broad roads crossed above the water on broken pillars. A third descended between roofless buildings and vanished beneath a skin of oil.

Khel coughed ash onto the surface.

“Those were roads?” Anahya asked.

“Yes.”

“So much stone just to walk on.”

“Not walk.” He nodded toward a rusted shape beneath them. “Those carried people.”

She looked down. Four wheels, empty window holes, a compartment full of silt. She had scavenged such shells before without imagining them alive with passengers.

“How many?”

“In that one? Perhaps five.”

She counted the wrecks until she wished she had not.

***

The silver chart led east along what had once been the coast, but the sea had entered streets the map still marked as land. They traveled through a drowned avenue, Anahya swimming beside the upper floors while Khel picked his way along balconies and fallen beams.

His limp worsened. He tried to hide it by stopping to examine their route.

“That roof won’t hold,” he told her when she pointed toward a low building ahead.

“It looks whole.”

“Look below the edge.”

The wall beneath it bulged outward. Through the water she could see reinforcing rods peeled free of the concrete like ribs.

“Then where?”

He indicated a narrow ledge on the next building. “That one still has its corners.”

She had learned to judge coral by color and the feel of water moving through its branches. Khel read these ruins with the same attention. A dark line meant a floor had fallen. A stair enclosed on three sides might offer shelter. A door standing open below the tide could conceal a current strong enough to pin her.

At a painted sign, he paused.

“What does it say?”

“School.”

Anahya looked through the windows. Small chairs lay tangled against the ceiling of a drowned room.

She swam on without asking anything else.

***

By midday, a sweet metallic taste penetrated her filter.

Anahya stopped so abruptly that Khel, balanced on a tilted streetlamp, nearly stepped into the water.

“Stay there.”

Ahead, the avenue opened into a broad basin. Rainbow films shivered across its surface, beautiful where the weak sun touched them. Beneath them the water was unnaturally clear. No scraps drifted. Nothing grew on the sunken walls.

She took one stroke backward, then another. Her gills clamped painfully.

“Can we cross above it?” Khel asked.

The overhead road had collapsed into the basin. Only its pillars remained.

“No.”

She led them north through a narrow alley, keeping beneath the slick wherever it had spread between buildings. Once she had to press herself flat against a submerged doorway while Khel crossed a beam overhead. Her flank scrape burned. Through the cracked wood beside her, a pale strand of plastic stirred.

When they reached moving water again, she stayed under until the gill spasms eased.

Khel waited on a concrete shelf with her spear across his knees.

“I thought clear water was safe,” he said when she surfaced.

“So did many of us.”

He looked back toward the basin, then handed her the spear.

***

Beyond the buildings, the road rose onto an embankment. Water washed across it in shallow sheets, leaving Khel a path beside her channel.

Anahya found a tiny living snail beneath a slab of fallen pavement. Its shell was dull brown, scarcely larger than the nail of her smallest finger.

She held it up.

Khel bent close, both faces intent. “Food?”

“No.”

“I wasn’t asking to eat it.”

“You looked hungry.”

“I am hungry.”

“So am I.”

They watched the snail extend one cautious feeler.

“There were fish that cleaned our teeth,” she said. “Little blue ones. You opened your mouth and waited.”

“That seems unwise.”

“You trusted them.”

“And they never bit?”

“Sometimes.”

His ash-breathing mouth twitched. She realized he was smiling.

She set the snail back beneath its stone and told him about rays that buried themselves in sand, about the slow lantern-bearing animals of deep water, about silver shoals turning so closely together that they seemed a single creature.

“Did you see all of them?”

“Not all. My mother saw more.”

Khel kept pace along the road. When she stopped speaking, he did not ask her to continue.

***

They rested beneath the surviving span of a bridge.

Anahya divided the last strip of dried kelp from her salvage pouch. Khel contributed a hard brown root he had been carrying inside his coat. He shaved away its bitter skin with a piece of glass and offered her the softer center.

It tasted of dust and faint sweetness.

“The skin makes you sick,” he said.

She showed him how to soak the kelp before chewing it.

For a while they ate without speaking. Water tapped the pillars. Far overhead, a loose strip of metal lifted and fell in the wind.

Khel shifted his injured foot into the shallows. Anahya moved aside to make room, then pushed a folded scrap of filter cloth beneath his bleeding heel.

He looked at her but said nothing.

She leaned against the pillar beside him. Tomorrow they would need to find more food, and a route beyond the broken road. For now, she let her eyes close. When his foot slipped on the wet stone, she steadied it without looking.

## Chapter 6: Ash and Salt

In the dark beneath the bridge, Khel began to drown.

There was scarcely a hand’s depth of water around his feet. He sat against the embankment with his injured heel on the folded cloth, his two faces turned toward separate shadows. Yet his chest heaved, and the mouth beneath the weeping eyes opened without drawing breath.

Anahya woke to the sound. For an instant she was back in the last nursery, listening through a wall while her mother tried to sing water through a child’s failing gills.

She pushed herself upright in the channel.

“Khel.”

His other mouth opened. Ash spilled down his chest.

“No,” it said. “Under the table. Stay under.”

Anahya caught his wrist before his hand struck the concrete. His skin was cold.

“Wake. You’re beneath the bridge.”

Both faces flinched. His fingers closed around hers with painful strength, then released.

For several breaths there was only the suck of water through the bridge piers.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

She let go of his wrist. “What were you seeing?”

“Not seeing.”

The ash-faced mouth trembled. He wiped it with the back of his hand, leaving a dark streak across his knuckles.

“Being there.”

Above them, something loose tapped against the underside of the road. Anahya settled where the channel deepened enough to cover her gills. She waited.

“The crystal woke things,” he said at last. “Before, they came apart. A color. Someone calling. A pain with nowhere in my body to put it. Since the observatory, I can remember what happened before.”

“To you?”

Khel looked down at his hands.

“I don’t know which word to use.”

A tear slipped from the face nearest her. He caught it on one finger and held it between them.

“I remember a bowl made from a shell. White outside, pink within. My mother put little stones in it when she went away. One stone for each sleep before she returned.”

Anahya’s gills moved against the water.

“The waiting bowl,” she said.

His eyes found hers.

“You know it?”

“My mother used a hollow in the wall.”

He nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed the shape of something he had feared imagining.

“There were three stones left. I was supposed to move one after sleeping. But I moved them all because I wanted her home.”

His voice had become small, though it remained his own.

“Then the tide came through the doors. It smelled sweet. People were swimming above me, knocking into the roof. Someone pulled me out of my sleeping hollow. I remember their nails.”

His fingers tightened against his palms.

“They carried me toward open water. But there wasn’t any open water. It was all the same.”

Anahya knew that sweetness. She had tasted it that afternoon beyond the ruined road.

“I tried to breathe harder,” he said. “I thought I wasn’t trying enough.”

She looked away.

On the opposite bank, a strip of plastic caught and loosened in an eddy, caught and loosened again.

“How old?” she asked.

“I don’t know. Small enough to be carried.”

For a while he said nothing. Then his other face turned toward the gap beyond the bridge, where the night held a dim red stain from some distant burning.

“There was a table,” he said. “A real table. Wood, with one short leg. My brother had folded paper under it.”

Anahya watched ash gather in the cracks of his lips.

“The roof made a sound like pebbles falling. I wanted to look. He told me it was hail, but he wouldn’t let me go to the window.”

“The fire?”

“After the sky broke.”

His shoulders hunched.

“It came through the roof. Grey flakes. They looked cold.”

He rubbed his thumb over two fingertips.

“I caught one.”

Anahya had no answer ready for that.

“My brother put me under the table. Then he went to the door. I was angry with him for leaving me.”

The ash came more thickly. Khel turned aside until he could speak again.

“That was the last thing I felt. Angry. I wanted him to come back, and I wanted to hurt him for going.”

“You were a child.”

“So was he.”

Water lapped against the embankment. Anahya remembered the vision: the unfinished body, the grieving voices gathered around it, everything reaching across a boundary that no longer held.

“Two children,” she said.

Khel nodded.

“I remember dying in both places.”

“And waking?”

“Later. There were voices around me. Not words. Wanting.” He pressed a hand against his breastbone. “The water-child was here. And the other. Neither could get away.”

“Are they still separate?”

“Sometimes, when I sleep. But I remember both. I miss both mothers.”

Anahya studied the two profiles. She had thought his tears an affliction, his ash a weapon. He had used them against her; she had given him reason.

“The seawater,” she said.

“It comes when something remembers. Even when I don’t know what.”

“And the ash?”

He watched it settle on his sleeve.

“All the things that should have been different. My brother coming back. The tide turning clean. Everyone who wanted to take something back.”

She could not absolve those voices. She could not even imagine their number.

But she knew a child counting stones to hasten a mother home.

Anahya lifted her hand. Khel went still as she touched the damp cheek nearest her, then brushed a streak of ash from the other with her thumb. There was ordinary skin beneath it, warm where the tears had not chilled him.

“Does this hurt?”

“No.”

She left her hand there.

He closed his eyes on both faces.

“I don’t know what I am,” he whispered.

“Neither do I.”

His mouth tightened.

She shifted closer, her bruised ribs protesting, until her shoulder rested against his knee.

“But I know who’s here.”

Beyond the bridge, the current carried its broken scraps toward the sea. Khel’s breathing slowly steadied. When Anahya lowered her hand, he caught it—not with the desperate grip of waking, but gently—and held it while they waited for morning.

## Chapter 7: The Greenhouse of Ghosts

Morning came gray beneath the bridge.

Anahya woke with her cheek against stone and Khel’s hand still loosely holding hers. His seawater face watched the channel; his ash-breathed face watched the road above. When his injured heel shifted, he flinched.

“Your foot,” she said.

“It remains attached.”

“That was not my question.”

His mouths answered in turn, and despite herself she nearly smiled. She tightened the filter over her gills, gathered her spear and pouch, and rose. Her ribs and flank complained. The channel had receded during the night, leaving a skin of rainbow oil along its edges.

Khel pointed east. “There is a structure beyond the embankment.”

Anahya followed his gaze. Through the fog stood a long skeleton of glass and metal, its remaining panes reflecting the blank sky.

“Could be a trap.”

“Everything is a trap when one is tired.”

They climbed from the shelter. The ground beyond was a crust over drowned streets, packed with salt, ash, and dead reed roots. Khel leaned on a length of railing, favoring his heel. Anahya tested the path with her spear. Twice the earth trembled beneath them. Once black water opened with a soft gulp and released the stink of rotten eggs.

The greenhouse stood on raised foundations, though the sea reached halfway up its supports. A faded sign hung above the door. Beneath the shore-script, someone had carved the seven-rayed circle.

Anahya touched it. “This was ours.”

“Perhaps it was shared.”

She glanced at him. “You are fond of that word.”

“I find it useful.”

The door had swollen shut. Together they forced it open. Glass slid from the roof and chimed across the floor.

Inside, dead plants stood in ordered beds: gray stems, curled leaves, vines hardened into wire. Racks crowded the walls with clay jars and sealed cylinders. Dust, mold, and an old sweetness filled the air, making Anahya’s gills tighten.

At the center lay a circular basin crusted white. Labels remained fixed in the soil.

SEAGRASS—SOUTHERN NURSERY  
TIDE-MOSS—LOWLIGHT STRAIN  
CORAL MEMORY—SECOND GENERATION

The last words were written in merfolk script.

Anahya knelt and scraped away the crust. Beneath it lay a black coral shard, brittle and empty. She recognized dead coral by its weight.

Khel had found a cabinet marked in both scripts. He pried it open with the railing. Inside rested thin memory plates. Anahya lifted one and pressed her thumb to its edge. Faint light moved through the carved lines.

A voice, weak with age, spoke.

“Record forty-two. The eastern dead zone has expanded three leagues. The ironworks deny discharge. The river carries their denial into every nursery.”

A second voice answered in merfolk words.

“Record forty-three. The singers have completed the seabed turn. We were told the harbor would be emptied before the surge.”

Images unfolded above the plate: a crowded coast, boats and towers, then enormous stones glowing beneath the sea. Blue force traveled through them. The water rose in a curving wall around the harbor.

People ran.

The wall broke.

The harbor vanished.

Anahya dropped the plate. It struck the floor without shattering.

“No.”

Khel bent to retrieve it. “There is more.”

“I heard enough.”

The next plate displayed a map. Red stains spread across the ocean beside notes: *poison discharge*, *iron charge*, *current inversion*, *silt burial*. Some dead zones began with mines and factories. Others had been made by merfolk war-magic—currents turned against reefs, seabeds lifted over nurseries, poisoned water drawn into breeding grounds.

At the bottom, several hands had written:

WE HAVE EACH LEARNED TO CALL DESTRUCTION DEFENSE.

Anahya stared until the letters blurred.

Her mother’s voice had always given her a simple history: shorefolk poisoned the rivers, destroyed the hatcheries, and began the war. It had been true.

It had not been the whole truth.

“We should leave,” Anahya said.

“The Thorn Reef lies beyond the Wound.”

“Let it lie there.”

“We came to see what survived.”

“What survives?” Her laugh was sharp. “Proof that we deserved to lose everything?”

His ash face turned toward her.

“I am carrying my mother’s hatred like a weapon,” she said. “The people I mourn were killers too. Every song I learned may have helped break the sea.”

She seized her spear. The cracked coral shaft groaned in her grip.

“I will not go to the reef and ask it to forgive me.”

“I did not ask you to.”

“You asked me to hope.”

“No.” Both mouths spoke together. “I asked you to look.”

He limped toward a row of overturned trays. Beneath one, sheltered from the salt wind, lay a narrow glass vessel. Khel lifted it carefully.

Inside was a coral splinter no larger than a fingernail.

It was alive.

A green pulse moved through it, faint but steady. One tiny branch curled upward, tipped with translucent thorns. The light shone through Khel’s seawater tears and silvered the ash around his other mouth.

Anahya forgot to breathe.

“Put it back,” she whispered.

He knelt. “Help me.”

They cleared a hollow beneath the broken shade cloth and set the vessel into the damp soil. Khel poured a little water from his flask around it. Anahya added clean condensation collected by her filter.

The coral brightened.

Not enough to promise survival. Enough to answer.

Anahya lowered herself beside it. Around them, dead stalks clicked in the wind. Above, broken panes admitted a sky the color of old bruises.

“They tried to save it,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Both of them.”

“Yes.”

She touched the glass. The living shard pulsed beneath her finger.

“I don’t know how to carry this.”

“Carry it badly,” Khel said. “Carry it until you learn.”

They remained there until the light strengthened.

At last Anahya gathered the memory plates and returned them to the cabinet. She did not forgive the singers. She did not forgive the shorefolk. But she closed the door gently, as if the records inside were wounded creatures sleeping.

When they left, the coral remained beneath the shattered roof, glowing green in the dead shore.

Anahya looked back once.

Then she turned east, and Khel followed.

## Chapter 8: The Barrier's Wound

The Barrier’s Wound

By afternoon, the sea had lost its color.

It spread beneath Anahya in bruised layers of green and black. Ahead, the island chain rose in broken silhouettes: drowned hills, salt-white cliffs, and the ribs of towers jutting from the water. Between the islands, the chart showed a narrow opening in the seabed.

The Barrier’s Wound.

Anahya touched the copper-bound cylinder beneath her belt. The silver chart inside had survived fire, flood, and nineteen years of her keeping. Its lines promised a passage. The water warned her away.

It tasted of metal.

“Stop,” she told Khel.

He halted on the strip of asphalt they had followed along the drowned coast. His injured heel trembled. Both faces turned toward her: the brine-eyed one solemn, the ash-faced one alert.

“What?” they asked, almost together.

Anahya pointed to the sea. A pale vapor hovered between the islands. It did not drift with the wind. It folded and unfolded in slow, breathing pulses.

“The wound begins there.”

Khel studied it. Brine gathered beneath one eye and slipped down his cheek. From the other face came a thin breath of ash.

“I hear voices,” he said.

Anahya heard them too.

At first they were only pressure inside her skull. Then the whispers separated. Some spoke in merfolk syllables, some in shore-script, and some in voices too old for either.

Turn back.

You left us.

The reef is not for you.

Her fingers tightened around the spear.

“They want us afraid.”

“Perhaps they want to be heard.”

She glanced at him. “You always make the dead sound reasonable.”

“No. I make the living sound certain.”

They entered the water together.

The first current seized Anahya below the ribs and spun her sideways. Pain burst through her scarred gills. She clamped her mouth around the filter and kicked, but the water had no single direction. It rushed down, then up, dragging silver silt from the seabed.

Khel floundered beside her. The current twisted him end over end, ash streaming from his mouth.

Anahya caught his wrist.

Something brushed her ankle.

Below them, pale shapes rose from the rift. Fingers—or roots, or bones wrapped in weed—reached through the dimness. Around them drifted scraps of uniforms, shell armor, work coats, nursery cloth. The dead had worn both worlds.

The current pulled her down.

Khel seized her other arm. “Anahya!”

The water changed around them. Fish-shaped things flashed through the green, each with too many fins. Coral erupted from a submerged car, branching in seconds, then blackening and dissolving. The rift was still breaking.

She tried to swim upward, but the pressure drove her down.

Khel’s brine face opened its mouth.

A long, trembling sob poured into the water.

Brine spread in shining threads. Wherever it touched the current, the turbulence slowed. The reaching bones sank. The voices thinned.

“Again,” Anahya gasped.

His face wept harder. Salt carried the weight of remembered tides, calming the water around her ribs.

Then ash-fog descended.

It poured from above in a gray curtain, clogging Anahya’s filter at once. Her gills spasmed. She lost sight of Khel.

“Khel!”

His ash face answered with a roar.

He exhaled. Cinders burst outward, red at their centers, and the fog recoiled—not burned away, but parted. A narrow corridor opened through it.

The ash face coughed. The brine face sobbed.

“They cannot do this separately,” Khel said, his voices breaking over each other.

“Then don’t separate them.”

He looked at her through the murk.

Anahya had trusted currents, charts, old songs, and the hard point of her spear. She had trusted no living creature for years. Trust seemed another name for giving someone the power to wound you.

The fog surged toward Khel.

She moved between him and it.

“I’ll hold you,” she said.

His eyes widened. “You cannot.”

“I know.”

She wrapped both arms around his waist. His body was cold beneath one hand and fever-warm beneath the other. The brine face wept against her hair. The ash face drew breath above her shoulder.

“Now.”

He released both faces at once.

Brine streamed outward as ash thundered through the water. The forces met—not fighting, but circling. Salt cooled the cinders. Cinders gave the brine shape. Together they formed a spiral, a clear passage through the poisonous fog.

Anahya held on.

The dead screamed around them.

She saw a merfolk mother pressing a child beneath a dock as fire fell from the sky. A shoreman wading through black water toward a nursery of eggs. Singers raising their hands. Machines belching poison. Boats overturned by a wall of green water. Two children, separate and terrified, reaching for mothers they could not save.

Then the visions collapsed into one another.

Khel convulsed in her arms.

“I am here,” Anahya said, though her own lungs burned. “Khel. You are here.”

The spiral carried them forward.

The seabed rose beneath them, jagged and torn. They passed between cliffs of melted stone. Behind them, the fog closed with a sound like a thousand mouths biting down.

Ahead, the water cleared.

Anahya broke through first and dragged Khel beneath a rocky overhang. They lay among smooth black stones, breathing hard. His heel had opened again; dark blood curled into the water. Her gills still burned, but each breath no longer sharpened the pain.

For a while, neither spoke.

Then Khel turned both faces toward her.

“You held me,” the brine face whispered.

“You were being stupid.”

The ash face gave a faint, cracked laugh. “That is not what you meant.”

“No,” she said.

Beyond the cavern, the passage widened into a hidden channel. Far ahead, behind a veil of clear water, something pulsed green.

Anahya swam closer.

Black coral rose from the seabed in thorned ridges, enclosing a narrow lagoon concealed between the cliffs. Its light was faint, sickly, but alive. The entrance lay beyond a curtain of stone marked with the seven-rayed circle.

The Thorn Reef.

Anahya pressed her palm against the rock. A green pulse answered beneath it.

Khel came beside her, leaning heavily against the wall.

“We found it,” he said.

Anahya looked at the living light, then at the dark wound behind them.

“Yes,” she said. “Now we find out what it costs.”

## Chapter 9: Thorn Reef

The clear water closed behind them with a soft, glassy sigh.

Anahya floated beneath the rocky overhang and stared into the lagoon.

The Thorn Reef was real.

Black coral rose from the seabed in narrow ridges, every branch twisted into a hook or barb. It looked burned, hardened for survival. Yet within its darkness, a green pulse moved from thorn to thorn, faint as a heartbeat beneath skin.

Then the light faltered.

Anahya’s gills clenched. She had imagined this place for half her life: a hidden kingdom bright with living gardens, where the lost merfolk waited beside untouched nurseries and sang the names of the vanished.

There were no singers.

No guardians surfaced from the shadows. No silver fish moved through the coral. The lagoon was small enough to cross in a few strokes, and the reef occupied barely half its floor. Black residue filmed its branches. In places, the green light had gone out entirely.

Khel limped through the opening behind her. His heel had opened again, and blood unwound from it in a dark ribbon. The brine face watched the coral with wet, ancient eyes. The ash face lowered its cracked mouth, breathing a thin veil of gray.

“Is it dying?” he asked.

Anahya swam toward a stone pillar at the lagoon’s center. It was nearly hidden beneath the reef’s growth. She brushed away the dark film, uncovering letters in the script her mother had taught her.

“For those who come after the gardens,” she read.

Her fingers shook as she cleared more of the stone.

“Carry the living thorn beyond the wound. Give it water unclaimed by either crown. Give it soil unworked by either hand. Give it blood mingled from the two remembering lines.”

Khel came beside her. “Two lines.”

“Sea and shore.” She read farther. “No single heir may wake what both peoples buried. No single grief may carry the garden.”

A shallow groove circled a hollow in the stone, shaped like a cup.

Khel’s faces turned toward it.

Anahya felt anger rise, swift and familiar. The merfolk had lost their cities, their children, their songs. Shorefolk had poisoned rivers and filled the water with oil, metal, and burning powder. Her mother had died coughing through a torn filter. Nothing she had learned could make the shorefolk innocent.

Still, the inscription asked her to join blood with them.

“To transplant it,” Khel said.

“To make it live beyond this place.”

His brine face wept harder. Drops drifted through the water like clear pearls.

The ash face gave a dry laugh. “It asks for the thing we have spent centuries refusing to share.”

Anahya turned on him. “You think this is amusing?”

“No. I think the dead are fond of difficult instructions.”

She wanted to strike him. Instead she looked at the reef.

Tiny fragments had broken from its larger branches. Most lay dull and lifeless. One trembled with green, then dimmed. The Thorn Reef was not waiting for them to admire it. It was running out of time.

“What happens if we leave it?” Khel asked.

“It dies.”

“And if we try?”

“It may die.”

“And if the inscription is wrong?”

Anahya pressed her palm against the coral. A thorn pierced her skin. Pain flashed up her arm, sharp and clean. A bead of blood drifted into the lagoon.

The reef brightened.

A green flicker ran along the branch beneath her hand, pausing at the blood as if tasting it.

Khel inhaled. Ash gathered at his lips, then vanished.

Anahya pulled her hand back. The cut was narrow, but its blood spreading among the thorns opened something in her chest. She saw the harbor from the observatory’s memory: merfolk singers raising their hands, stone and current answering them. Boats overturning. Nurseries buried beneath silt.

She had carried her spear toward every shore ruin believing the world owed her an enemy.

Once, after her mother died, she had dreamed of a wave tall enough to cover the continents. In the dream, she stood upon its crest and watched every shore burn beneath the water. She had awakened smiling.

The memory sickened her now.

Not because the shorefolk were innocent. They were not. But vengeance had become another kind of poison, and she had been drinking it because it was the only thing that kept her moving.

Khel touched the pillar. His fingers were scarred, the nails dark with ash.

“You don’t have to forgive them,” he said.

“I know.”

“You don’t have to forgive your people either.”

“I know.”

His faces regarded her differently: the brine face with sorrow, the ash face with guarded fear.

“Then what are you doing?” he asked.

Anahya placed her wounded palm against the coral again.

“Choosing what happens next.”

The reef pulsed beneath her skin.

She thought of her mother teaching her the calling-song: Here. I am here. Not a claim. Not a demand. A signal sent into darkness, asking only to be answered.

She had believed the song was meant to find the lost.

Perhaps it was meant to tell the living where to begin.

“I will give it my blood,” she said. “If yours is truly of both lines, it can have yours too.”

Khel went still. “Anahya.”

“I am not doing this for the shorefolk.”

“I know.”

“I am not doing it to cleanse the merfolk of what we did.”

“I know.”

“I am doing it because something is alive.”

The brine face closed its eyes. The ash face bowed until his forehead nearly touched the stone.

When he spoke, both voices came together.

“Then I am here.”

Anahya’s throat tightened.

She reached across the space between them and took his hand. His skin was warm on one side, cool on the other. Salt dampened his knuckles. Fine ash settled between their fingers and dissolved in the lagoon.

For a moment, neither moved.

The reef’s green pulse passed beneath their joined hands, weak but steady.

Anahya looked into the dark water beyond the coral, where the drowned world waited with its graves and unanswered songs. She could not mend it. She could not absolve the dead. She could not pretend either people had been innocent.

But she could refuse to add another wound.

Together, she and Khel turned toward the stone cup.

The Thorn Reef brightened once, as if it had been listening.

## Chapter 10: First of the New

The stone cup waited between them, pale with age and smooth from vanished hands.

Anahya held her palm above it. Blood gathered at the base of her thumb, dark in the reef’s green pulse. Across from her, Khel leaned against the central pillar, his wounded heel trembling. The seawater face watched with sorrowful eyes. The ash face breathed a thin grey smoke.

“Are you certain?” the seawater mouth asked.

“No.”

The ash mouth gave a dry laugh. “Good. Certainty built most of the ruins.”

Anahya looked at the hooked branches rising around the lagoon. Black residue filmed them. Their green light faltered, stopping and starting as if the reef had forgotten how to live.

She remembered the dead coral cities, her mother’s voice teaching her the calling-song, and nineteen years of singing into empty water.

Here. I am here.

The world had answered with silence.

She pressed her cut palm into the cup.

Khel lowered himself beside her and drew a coral shard across his hand. Blood surfaced beneath the ash face; brine welled from the other. Then he placed his hand over hers.

Their blood mingled.

The cup drank.

For a breath, nothing changed. Then green light rushed through their joined hands and along every thorn. It plunged into the seabed. Black silt convulsed as fine cracks spread through it, glowing from below.

Heat tore through Anahya’s arm, chest, and ribs. Her gills clenched shut. She folded over the cup, unable to breathe, while something vast and bright moved through her.

She saw water as the reef saw it: not a poisoned weight, but a body of passages. Root and fish, salt and leaf, current and stone had once sustained one another. She felt every wound the world carried and every place where healing had been abandoned.

Her gills opened.

Water entered without burning.

Anahya gasped. Then she gasped again as air rushed into her lungs with the same strange gentleness.

She could breathe both.

Faint dark lines appeared beneath the skin of her arms, branching like thorns. They climbed her wrists and shoulders before vanishing beneath her collarbones. Her scars remained, but their edges softened into the pattern.

Khel screamed.

His voices began separately—one drowning, one choking on ash—then twisted together until Anahya could no longer distinguish them. She seized his arm.

“Khel. Look at me.”

Both faces turned toward her.

For an instant, they overlapped. Brine streamed from both eyes. Ash escaped from both mouths. His features drew inward, not erased but joined, until one face remained. Grief and wonder still lived there, but they no longer pulled him apart.

Khel touched his cheek.

“How many faces?” he whispered.

“One,” Anahya said.

He breathed. No ash came out.

“Does it hurt?”

“Yes.”

“Then I remain myself.”

A startled laugh escaped him. Anahya laughed too, tears mixing with the lagoon.

Below them, the reef began to grow.

Roots pushed through the dead seabed in black-green cords. They entered cracks in the stone and followed the old channels of poisoned currents. Each root divided, spreading a living web across the lagoon floor.

The central thorn broke free and floated between them. It was no longer black but green at its core, its hooked branches tipped with tiny lights.

“Where do we put it?” Khel asked.

“Where the water still moves,” Anahya said. “Where something might hold.”

They carried the cutting through the veil.

Outside, the poisoned ocean waited, dull beneath storm-stained light. Anahya expected the old pain when she entered it. Instead, the water touched her gills like cold rain. Oil and poison remained; nothing had been cleansed. But she could endure them.

Together they crossed the dead seabed. Khel limped beside her as they planted the thorn in a hollow between fallen towers. Its roots seized the silt, and green light spread in a narrow circle.

They carried another cutting to the drowned highway, where it curled around a rusted vehicle. Another went beside the greenhouse ark. Another settled in the shadow of the observatory wall.

Each time, the reef took hold.

Each time, the dead ground stirred.

At last they rested on a ridge overlooking the water. Khel’s single face turned toward the horizon. Wind touched Anahya’s wet skin. It no longer clawed at her lungs.

She thought of her mother, the unanswered song, and all the names that had fallen silent.

Then she sang.

Her voice was not the old voice exactly. It carried scars, polluted currents, and the memory of ash. Yet beneath them moved a note belonging to neither shore nor sea alone.

Here. I am here.

The song traveled over the newborn reef.

For a moment, there was only wind.

Then one green point answered from below.

A second voice rose from the water, faint and wavering. A third joined it. The reef carried the sound through drowned roads and broken coral cities, across channels where no living song had passed for generations.

Anahya pressed her thorn-marked hand to her chest.

Khel stood beside her.

She was no longer last.

Neither of them was alone.
