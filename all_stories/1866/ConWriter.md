The city was shedding its towers when They returned to look for the well. A balcony of white coral broke loose above the avenue, turning slowly through the brown water. They folded their fins and let it pass. It struck the paving without a sound They could distinguish from the endless grinding of the reef. Once, the avenue had carried so many voices that a person could lose their own song among them. Now They heard each scrape of their tail against the silt.

They had crossed three dead gardens to reach this quarter. Black threads clung to the openings of their gills, and swallowing brought a taste of copper. Somewhere beneath the old tide-house was a spring, sealed against the sea when the sickness first came. They remembered carrying cups from it as a child. They remembered arguing about whose turn it was. Such a small, extravagant thing to have had: someone with whom to argue.

The tide-house roof had fallen across its entrance. They put a shoulder beneath a beam, pushed, and felt the softened coral crumble against their skin. No passage there. Their water flask hung empty against their ribs. They pressed an ear to the wreckage, hoping to hear the spring through the stone.

Three knocks answered.

They jerked back. Silt billowed around their hands. For a moment, the city acquired all its windows again; someone might look out, might call them foolish for taking so long.

Then the knocking came again, from above.

They swam up through the broken roof into shallower water, where a drowned stairway climbed toward the surface. Something lay across its upper steps. Two jointed legs. A sodden coat. One hand gripping a rusted railing, the other tapping it with a stone. The creature’s mouth hovered just above the water.

A shore creature. Alive.

They surfaced beyond its reach. Oily light spread around their shoulders.

“Don’t strike,” the creature said.

They had not heard spoken words outside their own mouth in so long that understanding arrived after the sound.

The creature turned its head. A narrow face watched them, lips blue with cold. Then it turned farther, impossibly far, and another face appeared on the other side of its skull: broader, older-looking, with one eye swollen shut. Both faces were flesh. Water dripped from both chins.

They sank until the sea covered their mouth.

“Fair,” said the narrow face. “I would stare too.”

A wave lifted the coat. Beneath it, a length of fishing line had wound around the creature’s ankle and vanished into the submerged stairs. Each surge pulled its head lower. They knew that line: transparent enough to disappear, strong enough to hold a body long after the body stopped struggling.

They took their shell knife from its sheath.

The open eye on the other face followed the blade. The creature went still.

“Keep hold,” They said. Their voice rasped from disuse.

Underwater, They found the line caught beneath an iron door. They could cut it easily, but the door bore a circle of seven carved fish, each biting the next one’s tail. The spring seal. They scraped slime from the handle, tried it, and felt no movement. Above, heels knocked against stone.

They cut the line first.

When They surfaced, the creature had dragged itself onto a higher step. It was shaking so hard that its teeth clicked. They caught a sleeve before it slipped back.

“There,” They said. “Stay there.”

“Trying.”

The creature’s hand closed over theirs, warm despite the water. They almost pulled away. Instead, They held until the shaking eased, then pointed beneath the surface.

“I need that door open.”

The creature followed their gesture. From inside its coat it drew a flat brass object on a cord. Seven fish circled a central hole.

They stared. “Where did you get that?”

“The pumping house on shore. I came down its stair.” The narrow face grimaced; the other coughed seawater. “Most of the stair is gone.”

“That seal belongs to us.”

“Look at the back.”

They turned the brass over. Beneath a crust of green were two figures: a merperson holding a bowl, a legged figure pouring into it. Between them, small letters spelled words They knew from childhood well-cups: *What rises inland is shared below.*

They rubbed the figures with their thumb. Their grandmother had called the shore people thieves of rivers. Yet here were their hands, joined in the metal.

“The pumps stopped,” the creature said. “But I heard water underneath them. I followed it.”

They looked down through the murk. Not another promise on a distant horizon. A door beneath their hand.

With the creature bracing the cord above, They fitted the brass into the lock and turned. The mechanism resisted, then gave. A thin current slipped through the opening, cold against their fingers. They tasted it. No copper.

They filled their flask before allowing themselves another mouthful.

Above, the creature waited on the broken stair. They surfaced beside it and offered the flask.

“Only a little,” They said.

Both faces turned toward them. Carefully, the creature drank.


The creature lowered the flask and held it between both hands. A bead of spring water trembled at the rim. They watched it vanish into the creature’s mouth, watched the narrow face swallow, then the broad face turn toward the flask as if measuring what remained.

“Enough,” They said, taking it back.

“Enough to remember thirst,” said the narrow face.

The broad face opened its good eye. “The current is changing.”

They felt it then: a cold thread slipping from the open spring and winding through the warmer seawater. It tugged at the silt below the stairway, carrying pale grains toward the drowned avenue. Beyond the tide-house, scraps of black weed lifted from the paving and streamed east.

They capped the flask and tucked it against their ribs. If the spring ran into the city, the polluted water might follow it. The city’s dead channels could fill with something clean—or the copper sickness could be drawn toward the spring and poison it from within. They could not leave the door open without learning where the current went.

“Can you walk?” They asked.

The creature pushed itself upright. Its coat dragged from one shoulder, and a cut at its ankle stained the water a thin pink. The narrow face looked toward the broken stair above. The broad face looked down at the submerged door.

“I can walk,” the narrow face said.

“I can fall,” said the broad one.

“Then hold to me.”

They slid an arm beneath the creature’s uninjured side. It was heavier than it appeared, all wet cloth and hard bones. The creature placed one hand on Their shoulder. Its other hand gripped the railing, and together they worked their way up the stair, They hauling their tail over each ledge. Each movement shed a cloud of silt. At the top, They pulled clear of the oily skin of the sea into air that smelled of salt, rust, and rotting kelp.

The drowned city spread around them in broken tiers. Coral towers rose from the water like ribs, their windows stuffed with nets. Farther inland, the old avenue disappeared beneath a brown floodplain. The waterline had climbed over the market wall and left strips of gray foam in the carved mouths of the guardian fish.

The creature pointed past the wall. “The pumping house is there.”

They saw only a ridge of brick and a crooked iron chimney. They remembered the quarter from before the sickness: shore traders arriving with river fruit, merfolk bringing pearl dust, children from both sides daring one another across the tide gates. Now the pumping house leaned over the inland flood, its lower windows submerged and its upper windows blind.

“Why did you come down its stair?” They asked.

The creature’s two faces turned in opposite directions, one toward the pumping house and one toward the ruined city.

“The pumps stopped,” said the narrow face. “The water beneath them did not.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the one we have.”

The creature touched the brass seal hanging at its chest. “This was fixed above the pump controls,” said the broad face. “The fish were worn smooth from hands. We removed it when the building began to sink.”

They brushed mud from the brass object. The joined figures on its back caught the weak light: one merperson offering a bowl, one shore figure pouring into it. Below them, the old words remained legible. What rises inland is shared below.

They had thought the inscription was a promise made by strangers. Now, with the spring running behind Them and the pumping house standing across the flood, it looked more like an instruction.

They guided the creature along the tide-house roof, using the exposed coral ridge as a path and pulling themselves along by their hands. Twice the ridge broke beneath Their weight. The second time, They plunged waist-deep into water and caught the creature’s sleeve before it went after them. Both faces shouted at once, their voices striking strangely together.

They pulled free, coughing. The creature steadied Them by the elbow.

“Careful,” said the narrow face.

“I am.”

“You are bleeding.”

They looked down. A sharp edge had opened the skin along Their palm. The blood spread in a dark fan, and tiny silver scavengers gathered at its edge. They wrapped the hand in a strip torn from the creature’s coat.

“Keep the cloth,” the broad face said.

“It is already ruined.”

“So is the city. We still keep what holds.”

The words settled somewhere beneath Their ribs. They did not answer. They crossed the last stretch of broken roof and entered the floodplain.

The water there was shallower, but the mud caught at Their tail and sucked at the creature’s every step. They moved slowly toward the pumping house, following the cold current as it braided through the brown water. Beneath the surface, buried pipes emerged one by one, their mouths marked with the same seven-fish seal. Some had collapsed. Others pointed inland, toward drowned farms and settlements They had never visited.

At the pumping house wall, They found a narrow maintenance hatch above the floodline. Its iron wheel was furred with salt, but the seven fish had been carved into its center. They placed Their bandaged palm against the mark.

A vibration answered from inside the wall.

Not a knock. Not machinery.

Water, moving through a great hidden throat.

The creature leaned close. The narrow face smiled without mirth; the broad face stared at the dark hatch.

They tightened Their grip on the flask. The spring’s significance no longer ended at the tide-house. If the old channels still connected sea and shore, then the city had not been abandoned by water. Someone—or something—had closed the route.

They set both hands on the wheel.

“Help me open it,” They said.

The creature braced its feet in the mud and placed its hands over Theirs. Together they turned. The rust cracked. Behind the hatch, water began to rise.


The wheel kicked beneath Their hands.

They let go with a cry, and the creature caught Their wrist before the spokes could drag the bandage between them. Behind the iron, water struck hard enough to shake rust from the hinges. A brown jet hissed through the lower seam.

“Back,” They said.

The creature stumbled onto its sound foot. They braced Their tail against the wall and pulled it aside. The hatch opened a finger’s width, stopped against a retaining chain, and spat water across the floodplain. Grit rattled over Their scales. For several breaths They could do nothing but hold the creature clear and listen to the furious draining.

They had wanted a door. They had nearly made a weapon of it.

When the jet weakened, They lowered Their good hand into it. Warm water first, smelling of trapped mud; then a colder pulse. The spring had reached this place before them. Opening the tide-house door had disturbed more than the little chamber beneath the stairs.

“Can we close it?” the narrow face asked.

“Perhaps. But not with our hands in the wheel.”

They waited until the water fell below the seam. Then the creature held the wheel steady while They loosened the retaining chain with Their knife. Together they eased the hatch outward. Beyond it, a shallow channel ran beneath a vaulted ceiling. A maintenance shelf followed the right wall, just wide enough for the creature’s feet. On the left, water spilled over a stone lip into a deeper chamber.

They pulled themselves across the sill first. The iron scraped Their belly scales. Inside, They turned and offered Their forearm; the creature gripped it, ducked both faces beneath the frame, and stepped onto the shelf. Its injured ankle folded. They caught its knee against Their shoulder and held until it found its balance.

“Still trying,” the narrow face said breathlessly.

They remembered the drowned stair and almost smiled.

Light entered through cracks overhead. In its thin stripes, They saw three enormous pump housings, their shafts motionless and their lower halves buried in sediment. The water moved without them. It entered through a narrow opening inland, gathered behind a stone partition, and escaped through an overflow toward the sea.

Their flask knocked against the sill as They dragged Their tail farther in. They uncapped it. Less than two mouthfuls remained.

The creature watched, saying nothing.

They shared what was left. This time They did not count the creature’s swallow. Then They followed the cold flow upstream along the channel, keeping Their cut hand above it. The creature limped beside Them on the shelf, one hand sliding along the brickwork.

Above the inland opening stood a small stone basin. Water welled through its bottom and spilled from a worn notch. Its rim bore seven fish.

They remembered this shape. The well-cups of Their childhood had fitted beneath just such a notch; Their grandmother had scolded them for dipping dirty cups into the basin itself. They held the flask beneath the falling thread. Clear water struck its bottom with a sound so ordinary that Their throat tightened.

“No copper?” the broad face asked.

They tasted a drop. “None that I can taste.”

It was not proof. They knew how long the sea had looked beautiful after it began killing them. But the channel feeding this basin lay above the stagnant sump, and the water came cold from inland stone. They drank slowly, then passed the flask up.

While the creature drank, They cleared slime from the partition. Beneath it appeared carved lines: an inland pool, the pumping house, branching channels descending into the city. Small bowls marked places along the branches.

Not one well. A system of wells.

They traced the lowest line to an outlet blocked by a slab. Its handle had been secured with an iron pin. The pin’s bent ends lay flattened against the brackets.

“This did not fall shut,” They said.

The creature crouched awkwardly, holding the flask against its chest. Both faces turned toward the fastening in succession.

“Can you pull it?”

They reached, then stopped. Beneath the slab, a dark ribbon of sediment stirred with each pulse of fresh water. Green crust furred the metal. Beyond that outlet lay the channels beneath the coral houses, the gardens, the avenue where the towers were coming apart.

If They opened it now, the first rush would carry everything trapped here into the city.

“Not yet.”

The refusal hurt. They had crossed dead gardens for a drink; now clean water pressed against a barrier within Their reach.

They looked again at the carving. Beside the blocked outlet, another line curved downward to a square pit. Its narrow gate stood partly open beneath the maintenance shelf. Through it, dirty water trickled into a chamber packed with silt.

“We clear that first,” They said. “Give the mud somewhere to go before we send water below.”

The creature returned the filled flask and lowered itself onto the shelf.

“Show me what to hold.”

They placed its hands around the small gate’s lever, then slipped into the channel beneath it. This time, before touching the obstruction, They checked where the water would run.


They lowered themselves into the square pit, keeping one hand on the slick ladder and the other pressed against the wall. The water reached Their waist, cold enough to seize the muscles in Their tail. Above, the creature held the gate lever with both hands. Its narrow face watched the channel; its broad face watched Them.

“If I say pull, pull,” They called.

“If you say run?” asked the narrow face.

“There is nowhere to run.”

The broad face gave a low, humorless sound. “Then we will pull.”

The pit had once been a settling chamber. Its walls sloped inward toward a drain clogged with gray-brown sediment. Shell fragments, rust flakes, and threads of black weed had packed together into a dense plug. Each pulse from the spring pushed against it, making the mass shiver. They dug their fingers into the upper crust and drew out a lump the size of a fist. It broke apart in Their palm, releasing a bitter metallic smell.

Copper.

They dropped it at once. The fragment struck the water and sank through a green cloud.

“Bad?” the creature asked.

“Not in the spring. Here. This is what the old flow carried away from the city.”

They scraped again, working around the edges rather than disturbing the center. The mud clung beneath Their nails. Their wounded palm burned where the water entered the torn cloth, and the ache sharpened whenever They twisted. Still, the plug loosened by grains. A narrow thread of dark water began to pass through.

Above, the creature shifted its weight. The lever groaned.

“Hold,” They said.

“I am holding.”

The narrow face glanced toward the channel. “The gate wants to open.”

They looked up. The creature’s ankle trembled, but its hands remained fixed. They could not leave it to fight the mechanism alone. They climbed two rungs, took the lever below its grip, and pressed their shoulder against it.

Together they forced the gate downward.

The channel fell silent for one breath.

Then the sediment plug collapsed.

A surge struck Their chest and threw them backward. Their tail slammed against the pit wall. Brown water filled Their mouth, thick with grit and the copper tang. They caught the ladder with one hand, but the current tore the cloth from Their palm. Above, the creature shouted in both voices and hauled the lever closed. Its injured ankle buckled. One knee hit the shelf, yet it did not release the handle.

They kicked against the drain and found open water beneath the sludge. The surge was descending, not rising. That mattered. They hooked an elbow around the ladder and waited while the chamber emptied through the newly opened passage.

When the water cleared enough to see, a pale object rested in the drain mouth. They reached down and pulled free a flat disk of bone, carved with seven fish. Unlike the brass seal, this one had been broken cleanly in two. A line of dark residue marked its fractured edge.

They climbed from the pit. The creature released the lever only when They had both hands on the shelf.

“You are bleeding again,” said the broad face.

They looked at Their palm. The wound had opened, and diluted red curled down Their wrist.

“You are limping worse.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the one we have.”

The narrow face studied Them, then tore a fresh strip from the remaining lining of its coat. They expected it to offer the cloth. Instead, the creature wrapped it around Their hand itself, pulling the knot tight with its teeth while the broad face kept watch over the channel.

The gesture made Their throat constrict more than the polluted water had.

“Thank you,” They said.

The narrow face’s expression changed first. “That word is shore-made.”

“Not only shore-made.”

They placed the broken disk beside the map carved into the partition. Its seven fish aligned with the lowest branch. Beneath the mark, a shallow groove formed the shape of a hand pouring water into another hand. The line continued past the blocked city outlet and ended at a jagged symbol They recognized from the old sickness records: a wave crossed by three short cuts.

A warning against backflow.

The spring had not merely been sealed to protect a source. The city outlet had been closed to prevent the poisoned channels from reaching inland. But the seal had failed, and the opened spring was now pushing clean water toward every drowned street.

“The water is going to the city,” They said.

“It already was,” the broad face replied.

“Now it has enough strength to carry the sickness back out.”

The creature looked toward the vaulted ceiling, where faint vibrations traveled through the brick. Somewhere beyond the wall, buried channels were waking one after another.

They lifted the broken disk. Its underside held a smear of fresh silt, and within that silt glimmered a single copper scale from some dead machine.

“We cannot open the city outlet yet,” They said. “But we can clear the settling pits and learn how much poison remains.”

“And then?”

They filled the flask from the basin, stopping before the water reached the neck. Drinkable water pressed against Their ribs in a precious, sloshing weight. They corked it and secured it beneath the bandage at Their waist.

“Then we follow the spring upstream,” They said. “If there is a way to turn the flow, we find it before the city drinks what it should have buried.”

The creature extended its hand. They took it, and together they crossed the narrow shelf toward the next gate, leaving the loosened sediment to drain into the dark pit below.


The next gate stood beyond a break in the shelf.

They stopped with the creature’s hand still clasped in Theirs. Below the gap, water slipped toward the settling chamber they had cleared. The current carried flakes of rust, turning them slowly beneath the surface. Across the break, a ladder climbed beside the gate into an opening above the waterline.

The creature leaned forward. Its injured foot touched stone, then lifted again.

“No,” They said.

“I have not tried yet.”

“You have been trying on that ankle since the hatch.”

They lowered themselves onto the shelf and drew the flask from Their waist. Its cork held; the cloth around it remained dry. They drank once, passed it over, and waited until the creature settled beside Them. Without its support, Their wounded hand began to throb.

The creature drank, then offered the flask back.

“Keep some,” They said.

Both faces turned toward Them.

“For after we cross.”

They unfastened the loose end of Their palm dressing with Their teeth. The inner layers had darkened, but the bleeding had slowed. Rather than uncover the wound again, They tucked the loose cloth securely beneath the knot. Then They pointed at the creature’s ankle.

“Your turn.”

It extended its leg reluctantly. Swelling bulged above the edge of its shoe. They loosened the fastening and eased the stiff material away without pulling it off. The creature gripped the shelf; neither mouth made a sound.

They remembered hands too small to wrap properly around a spear, Their grandmother binding a split fin with woven weed. That patient touch had once seemed an ordinary part of being alive. For so long afterward, They had treated each injury as a private inconvenience: something to endure because nobody remained to help.

They folded what remained of the creature’s coat lining into a pad and bound it around the ankle with the remaining strip.

“Too tight?”

“Enough,” the broad face said. The narrow face added, “Thank you.”

They kept Their eyes on the knot until They could trust Their voice.

“Not only shore-made.”

Across the gap, the gate had no lever. A square socket occupied the center of its iron plate, and sediment pressed through the lower seam. They could not see where its far side drained. Opening it would mean repeating the mistake at the hatch, with less room to escape.

Instead, They studied the ladder. Its lowest rung had fallen across the broken shelf, one end submerged. Above it, a narrow ledge passed behind the gate frame.

“We go over,” They said. “Leave that shut until we can see the other side.”

The creature’s broad face tipped upward. “Can you climb?”

“Badly.”

“So can I.”

They freed the fallen rung from the water. It was a stone bar with iron sleeves at either end, heavy enough that They had to cradle it against Their forearms. The effort pulled at Their palm. They paused, breathing through the pain, then slid the bar across the gap. One sleeve scraped onto the opposite shelf.

The creature steadied the near end with its sound foot.

They tested the bar with Their weight before easing across it, belly low, tail trailing in the current. Halfway over, the iron sleeve shifted. They froze. A small chip broke from the shelf and vanished below.

“Back?” the narrow face asked.

They looked at the far wall, close enough to touch.

“Hold.”

They stretched Their good arm and caught a ladder bracket. With the bracket taking Their weight, They dragged the rest of Their body onto the ledge. Their tail struck the gate with a hollow clang.

Nothing opened. The water continued beneath Them at the same steady pace.

They secured the bar deeper against the wall, then reached across. The creature crossed sideways, hands braced on either side of the gap. Its sound foot landed first. When the injured one slipped, They caught its forearm and pulled while it pushed against the wall. Both of them ended pressed against the ladder, breathing hard.

For a moment They rested Their forehead against its sleeve. Neither face spoke.

The climb took longer than the crossing. They pulled upward by the brackets, keeping Their bandaged palm against Their chest whenever possible. Below, the creature supported the weight of Their tail; above, They braced while it brought its bad foot onto each rung. They passed through the opening together and crawled into a low inspection passage.

Here the spring ran in a separate stone trough. Beneath a removable floor slab lay the far side of the unopened gate: another settling pit, almost filled with gray sludge. Copper fragments lay embedded in its surface. They replaced the slab without disturbing them.

“We were right to leave it,” They said.

Beyond the trough, the passage widened around a masonry pier. Water divided there. One branch descended toward the pits; the other disappeared behind a raised stone shutter. A wooden handle projected from the shutter’s frame, split with age but still attached.

They crouched beside it. Before touching the handle, They followed the second branch with Their eyes. Through an inspection slit, They saw an empty channel sloping into darkness, its floor crusted white rather than green.

The creature lowered itself nearby and uncorked the flask.

They accepted a mouthful, then set Their good hand against the shutter’s frame, feeling for movement as the spring pressed steadily on its other side.


They kept Their good hand against the shutter and felt the vibration travel through the stone. The spring pressed from the other side in slow, patient knocks. The wooden handle trembled beneath Their fingers but did not turn.

“Is it a door?” the creature asked.

“A diversion.”

The narrow face leaned toward the inspection slit. The broad face watched the trough behind Them, where the water slid away from the unopened gate. “Diversion to where?”

They crouched lower. The slit was scarcely wider than two fingers, but the air coming through smelled different from the spring: dry mineral, old ash, and something sharp enough to sting Their nose. They pressed Their cheek near the opening. Beyond it, water ticked in a hollow channel, though the visible branch remained empty and white-crusted.

“Not empty,” They said. “The flow is below the floor.”

They traced the shutter’s lower edge. A line of mud sealed it against the threshold, but the mud had cracked in three places. Between the cracks, a thread of clear water shone and vanished beneath the stone. The raised slab was not holding back the spring itself. It was covering a lower sluice.

They drew the bone disk from the pouch at Their waist. Its broken edge was still stained dark. Seven carved fish faced the shutter. When They set the disk against the stone, the vibration changed. The fish seemed to tremble beneath Their palm.

The creature reached toward the disk, then stopped short. “The mark?”

They turned it over. The underside bore the shallow image of one hand pouring water into another, but here the poured stream pointed toward the white channel.

“An old control sign,” They said. “The spring fed this branch before it fed the city.”

“Before the sickness?”

“Perhaps before the city forgot what it was for.”

They searched the frame and found a narrow slot beneath the split wooden handle. Rust had filled it, but a thin copper scale protruded from the grime. They scraped it loose with the edge of the bone disk. The fragment was warm despite the cold passage.

Their wounded hand pulsed. They wrapped the fingers around the handle anyway and pulled down. The wood groaned. Nothing else moved.

“Help me brace,” They said.

The creature positioned itself beside Them, setting its sound foot against the masonry pier. Its injured ankle remained wrapped in the shared cloth, and They shifted Their shoulder beneath its arm so it would not have to bear weight on that side. Together they leaned into the handle.

The shutter lifted the width of a finger.

A breath of air escaped from below. White dust sprang from the threshold and coated Their lips with a bitter taste. Beneath the raised stone, a current flashed—not clear, not brown, but opalescent, carrying tiny flakes that glittered like fish scales. They released the handle at once. The shutter dropped with a heavy thud.

“Poison?” the narrow face asked.

“Concentrated residue. The channel has been dry long enough for it to crust.”

“Then we leave it closed.”

They nodded, but Their hand remained on the handle. The old mechanism had not resisted because it was broken. Pressure from below had held it shut. If They lifted it fully, whatever lay in the lower sluice would be carried into the inspection passage and toward the city outlet.

They pointed to the white branch. “We need to see where it goes without opening this.”

A thin ledge ran along the wall beside the channel. It disappeared behind the shutter, then reappeared farther upstream through a low arch. They pulled the flask free and tied its strap around Their wrist, leaving both hands available. The creature passed behind Them, one hand gripping the back of Their belt while They tested the ledge with Their tail.

The first step held. The second crumbled.

They caught the wall with Their bandaged hand. Pain flashed from palm to elbow, and Their tail struck the trough. The water splashed against the stone, carrying a rusty sheen toward the lower pit.

“Stop,” said the broad face.

“I am stopped.”

The creature’s fingers tightened on Their belt. They waited until the pain receded, then shifted sideways, placing Their weight against the wall rather than the ledge. The creature followed, supporting Them from behind and keeping its injured foot lifted whenever the stone narrowed.

They reached the low arch. Its ceiling dipped so close that They had to crawl. On the far side, the white-crusted channel continued upstream, no longer empty. A thread of water moved beneath the crust, slipping toward a round chamber where three pipes entered the wall.

One pipe bore the seven-fish carving.

They scraped away the crust with the bone disk. Underneath, the stone was stained green-black, but the carved fish remained clean. Beside it, someone had etched a second symbol: two faces turned away from one another, joined at the throat by a single line.

The creature came to rest beside Them. Both faces studied the mark.

“Two mouths,” it said.

“One passage,” They replied.

Behind the symbol, a hairline seam outlined a small inspection plate. They wedged the copper fragment into it and twisted. The plate clicked open, revealing a cavity packed with folded strips of treated cloth. Most had rotted into gray threads. One remained legible: a diagram showing the white channel, the spring, and a third route rising toward the buried shore.

They could not read all the old notation, but one warning had survived in clear strokes.

Do not turn the water while the lower city is full.

They folded the strip carefully and tucked it beside the broken seal. Then They looked toward the dark route beyond the three pipes. It climbed, not descended, and a faint current of fresh air moved through it.

“The upstream path is there,” They said. “We can follow it without opening the shutter.”

The creature steadied Them as They rose. Together they crossed beneath the arch, leaving the raised stone shutter closed behind them, while the spring continued knocking against its hidden sluice.


Beyond the inspection arch, the channel narrowed until Their shoulders brushed both walls. They followed the thread of water uphill, keeping to its edge rather than dragging Their tail through the white crust. Behind Them, the creature’s uneven footfalls interrupted the softer scrape of cloth against stone. The shutter remained closed. They listened until its knocking faded beneath the sound of their breathing.

The fresh air came from above a steep ramp. Water occupied a groove down its middle, too shallow to support Them and too narrow for the creature’s foot. On either side, the stone had been scored with parallel scratches. They laid Their good palm against one. The depression fitted the heel of a hand.

“People pulled themselves up here,” They said.

The creature crouched beside Them. Its broad face turned uphill while the narrow one examined the grooves. “People like you?”

“Perhaps.”

They had expected steps everywhere the shore began. The thought that someone had once shaped a passage for bodies like Theirs made the abandoned ramp harder to look at.

They took out the folded diagram and spread it over a dry patch. The rising line passed through a small rectangle before reaching the shore. Beside the rectangle were two short strokes, one on either side. They had mistaken them for damage when they first unfolded the cloth. Now They looked from those strokes to the grooves beneath Their hands.

“A resting place,” They said. “There should be a level stretch above.”

“Should be,” the broad face repeated.

“Yes.”

They lifted the flask without drinking. They wetted Their fingers and pressed them beneath the drying edge of Their tail. The skin there had begun to tighten. There was not enough water to waste on comfort, but cracking skin would turn the climb into something worse.

They started upward with Their forearms braced in the grooves. The creature followed close enough to support Their tail when it slipped sideways, then leaned against the wall while They held still for its next step. Neither could move far without making room for the other. Before they had climbed half the ramp, Their wounded palm began leaving small red marks on the stone.

“Wait,” the creature said.

They stopped, jaw clenched. Its narrow face appeared beside Their shoulder. A hand lifted the flask strap clear of Their wrist, where it had caught beneath Them.

“Not that hand.”

“I have only two.”

“And I have two. We need not use the damaged ones first.”

A laugh escaped Them, brief and painful. They shifted Their weight onto Their elbow while the creature folded a loose edge of the shared cloth beneath Their palm. Then it steadied Their forearm as They pulled. The next body-length cost less.

Near the top, a slab had fallen across the ramp. Its lower edge trapped dead roots and a clot of pale grit. The water threaded beneath it, but the remaining space would not admit either of them. They pressed one shoulder against the slab. It did not move. Above it, a dark opening showed between the broken stone and the wall.

The creature reached toward the roots.

“Leave those,” They said. “They may be holding the grit back.”

Its hand withdrew at once. They studied the opening instead. A sound stone lip lay within reach, but the climb would lift Their entire weight off the ramp.

“Over?” the narrow face asked.

“Over.”

They let the creature brace Their tail while They caught the lip with Their good hand. For a moment Their body hung against the slab, helplessly heavy. Then the creature pushed beneath Their hip, and They rolled onto the upper surface. Pain tightened Their throat. They stayed flat until They could breathe without making a sound.

From there, They could reach down. The creature climbed using its sound foot, both hands locked around Their forearm. They held against the stone and felt it tremble with effort. When its injured ankle struck the slab, both mouths gasped. They tightened Their grip and drew it the last short distance onto the landing.

Neither moved for several breaths.

The landing was the rectangle on the diagram. A shallow basin occupied its center, fed by the same thin stream, with an overflow returning downhill. They checked its bottom before lowering Their tail into it. No copper flakes glittered there; no opalescent skin gathered around the edges. Still, They kept Their wounded hand out.

Across the basin, a carving survived beneath a veil of roots: a finned body resting in water, a legged body seated beside it. Between them stood a jar. Above both heads, the two-faced sign had been cut into the lintel.

They touched the basin’s worn rim.

“A place to wait for each other,” They said.

The creature lowered itself beside the water. One face watched the ramp; the other turned toward the carving. “Useful.”

They nodded. Useful enough that strangers had worn hollows into the stone.

Beyond the landing, the passage continued toward a gray seam of light. They drank one small mouthful, then passed the flask across the basin. This time, while the creature drank, They allowed Their tail to rest.


The water eased the tightness beneath Their scales, but it did nothing for Their palm. They held the hand above the basin and watched blood darken the cloth beneath the fingers. Across from Them, the creature returned the flask and stretched its injured leg along the rim. Its broad face watched the gray light. The narrow face remained turned toward the carving, where a stone jar stood between two bodies that had never needed to ask whether they belonged together.

They studied the sign above it. In the chamber below, the paired faces had marked an inspection plate. Here they marked shelter. They had followed the symbol as though it were an instruction, yet it might have been something simpler: a sign recognizable from either direction, to travelers arriving from the sea or the shore.

“You look like the mark,” They said.

The creature lifted a hand toward its joined jaws. “Not exactly.”

“No. Not exactly.”

They resisted the urge to ask whether it remembered others like itself. There were questions They could endure only while moving. They tucked the flask securely against Their waist and drew Their tail from the basin. Water ran from the fin in thin strings. The creature reached out, offering its forearm rather than its hand, and They used it to lift Their weight onto the passage floor.

The way beyond the landing rose gently. That was mercy enough. They advanced on Their good palm and opposite elbow, with the creature supporting Them whenever the floor roughened. At a low bulge in the wall, They stopped so it could lean against Their shoulder and lift its injured foot across. Behind them, the basin dwindled to a silver oval. The gray seam widened ahead, and the smell changed from enclosed stone to wet earth.

A root curtain covered the opening. These roots were living, pale tips shining among the darker cords. They touched one without pulling. Beneath Their fingers, it bent instead of breaking. Through the gaps lay a patch of cloudy sky.

They had seen sky above poisoned seas so often that its presence should not have mattered. Still, They paused. Out there, wind could reach Them without first passing through a pipe.

“Room?” the creature asked.

They pressed the roots aside with Their forearm. “Enough. Slowly.”

They crawled through first onto a sloping apron of masonry, then turned to hold the curtain away from the creature’s faces. Its heel slipped on wet stone. They caught its wrist with Their good hand, braced Their tail against the passage mouth, and held until it found its footing. Then it crouched beside Them beneath the open sky.

They had emerged into a roofless court buried almost to its lintels. Earth pressed against the outer walls. Leafless shrubs grew through paving, and a narrow runnel carried water from beneath a slab into the passage they had just left. Beyond a broken parapet stood a mass of pipes, their exposed ends green with corrosion. The buried shore was not open country yet. It was another ruin, with soil where the sea would have laid silt.

They moved toward the runnel, following it to a stone trough wide enough to wet Their tail. Before entering, They examined the bottom. Dark sediment gathered in the corners, but the moving water showed none of the glitter they had disturbed below. They lowered only the tip of Their fin and waited. Cold spread through it. No sting followed.

The creature settled beside Them with a sharp intake of breath.

“Ankle?” They asked.

It nodded. They shifted closer so it could prop the injured leg on Their folded tail rather than the gritty paving. The weight hurt less than They expected. For a little while, both rested without speaking.

From this lower angle, They noticed a carving beneath the trough’s rim. A jar tipped toward two channels. One descended beside the finned figure; the other passed beneath a row of upright bodies. They took out the cloth diagram and compared its branching lines with the stone.

“This water went inland too,” They said. “Not just down to the city.”

The creature’s narrow face inclined toward the pipes. “Through those?”

“Perhaps. We should look before touching anything.”

They remembered the shutter’s resistance, the bright flakes rising beneath it. Here, one broken pipe overhung the trough. A green tear had dried on its lip; beneath it, the stone bore a matching stain. They drew Their fin back before the slow bead forming there could fall.

The creature followed Their gaze. It picked up a loose piece of paving, then stopped and looked at Them.

“Not underneath,” They said. “We might splash it.”

Together they found a flatter shard and laid it across the stained corner, sloping away from the moving water. The next drop struck stone and crept toward the dry paving instead.

It was a small diversion. They watched until another drop followed it, then refolded the diagram. Across the court, beside the pipe bank, a broad groove climbed through the accumulated earth. Handholds showed along one edge.

They pointed it out. The creature offered its forearm again, and They took it, leaving the trough for the path above.


The groove began beyond the last broken pipe, but reaching it meant crossing a strip of paving stained the same green as the diverted drip. They stopped before Their tail touched it. The creature halted with Them, its offered arm still firm beneath Their good hand. Behind them, a drop clicked against the shard they had laid beside the trough.

“Along the wall,” They said.

There was room there, barely: a raised border of pale stone, dry except where roots had split the joints. They turned sideways and eased Their tail onto it. The creature went ahead one careful step, then braced while They dragged Themselves forward. Its injured ankle remained lifted whenever it could manage. Neither looked graceful. They had once crossed whole avenues with a movement of Their fin; now the width of a hand determined whether They could pass.

At the foot of the groove, They looked back. The shard still carried the pipe’s slow leakage away from the trough. They wished They could leave something more durable, something that would not shift beneath the first hard rain. But there was no one coming behind them whom They could warn, and no strength left for repairing every danger they found. They turned uphill before that thought could make Them remain.

The groove had been cut through stone, not earth. Soil spilled into it from both sides, and small roots stitched a thin crust across the bottom. They tested the first handhold with Their elbow. It held. Farther up, another showed beneath a mat of dead leaves.

“Can you reach that one?” They asked.

The creature stretched, broad face tilted upward, narrow face angled toward Them. Its fingers closed around the hollow.

“Yes.”

“Then wait there. I’ll come to you.”

They climbed in short exchanges: the creature bracing while They pulled, then Their shoulder taking its weight while it brought its sound foot higher. The cloth around Their wounded palm caught on a rough edge. Pain shot through Their wrist, and They made a sound They could not swallow.

The creature immediately lowered itself against the slope.

“Stop.”

“If we stop here—”

“Your hand.”

It freed the snag with two fingers, carefully, without tugging the cloth across the wound. They rested Their forehead against the cool stone. Beneath Their ribs, Their heart worked with an ugly insistence. The creature stayed still until They lifted Their head.

“I can use my forearm,” They said.

“Use mine too.”

They did. The next pull brought Them level with a shallow ledge where both could rest without sliding. Their tail lay heavy across the groove. Below, the roofless court had become a hollow filled with pipes and shrubs, the trough scarcely visible beneath its rim. They had gained perhaps three body-lengths. It felt an unreasonable distance.

A broken tile pressed against Their side. They shifted it away and found a pattern underneath: overlapping arcs, worn almost smooth. For a moment They thought of scales. Then They saw the straight edge bordering them, and the tiny upright marks above.

“Water,” They murmured.

The creature leaned closer.

They brushed loose earth aside with Their good hand. The arcs continued beneath a carved boat. Beside it, half concealed by the ledge, a finned figure reached toward an upright one. There was no jar between these two. Both held the same rope.

They traced the rope without touching its fragile edges. In the drowned coral avenues, ropes had meant fishing lines, nets abandoned until they tightened around whatever still moved. Here, someone had carved one as a thing shared between hands.

“A landing place?” the creature asked.

“Perhaps this was the shore.”

They looked downhill again. Earth filled the space where water might once have stood. The court’s walls could have faced a harbor before they became walls around a buried garden. They could not prove it from one boat. Still, the groove suddenly seemed less strange: a way out of water, built for someone who could not climb steps.

Above the ledge stood two short stone posts. Between them lay a fallen gate, its wooden bars softened into dark ridges. The opening beyond was clear, but the crosspiece blocked the groove.

“We go around,” They said.

The creature tested the right-hand post, shook it once, then nodded. They used it as an anchor while lifting Their tail over the gate’s end. Dry scales scraped stone. They clenched Their jaw and kept moving. Once across, They braced behind the post and offered Their forearm. The creature stepped over with its sound foot, then brought the injured one through slowly, both mouths open with effort.

Beyond the gate, the groove flattened beneath a low arch. They crawled through into a covered landing, pulling Their tail after Them until nothing remained in the exposed passage. The creature followed and sat against the inner wall.

A stone basin stood empty beside them. They ran Their fingers over its rim. It was broad enough for Their tail, with a seat built against one side.

They let out a tired breath. Another place made for both.

The creature reached across the dry basin. They placed Their good hand in its hand and rested before looking toward the passage beyond.

The creature’s hand was warm around Theirs. They held it until the trembling in Their forearm eased, then loosened Their fingers. Beyond the empty basin, a strip of daylight lay across the floor. Wind moved something there with a dry, irregular scraping. They listened through several strokes before deciding it was a branch, not a loose door.

“We should see where it opens,” They said.

The creature drew its injured foot closer. “Slowly.”

They almost laughed. There was no other speed left.

Before leaving the covered landing, They examined the basin’s underside. A channel entered through the wall and ended above its rim, choked with compacted earth. They touched the blockage with one finger. Dry grit crumbled away, but no dampness followed. Whatever had supplied this resting place no longer reached it. They withdrew Their hand rather than dig farther. Opening an unknown channel would not make its water safe.

The creature offered the flask. They took only enough to wet Their mouth, then returned it.

“Some for you.”

It drank, and They waited while it secured the stopper. Their tail had begun to ache where the scales met the fin. They spread the loose end of their shared cloth beneath that tender edge before shifting forward. The creature knelt to straighten it without touching the bandage on Their palm.

The passage beyond the landing ran nearly level. They advanced along its left side, where the stone was smoothest, while the creature moved beside Them with one hand against the wall. At a fallen roof tile, They stopped and braced Their shoulder beneath its arm. It stepped across, rested, then turned to help Them slide Their tail over. By the time they reached the light, dust had whitened both their arms.

A thornless branch scraped the outer arch. The creature pushed it aside, revealing a shallow stone terrace above a slope of mud and broken masonry. They crawled beneath the branch and out of the passage, then waited while their companion followed. Air moved freely over Their face. Below the terrace, reeds stood in scattered clumps along a narrow stream. Farther away, dull water showed between ridges of spoil.

They had reached the buried shore.

Not the shore They had imagined. No broad inlet welcomed boats; no crowd waited beside bright moorings. Yet iron rings remained set into the terrace wall, well above the present trickle. One held a knot of mineral-hardened fibers.

They touched the stone around it.

“Here,” They said. “The boats could have come here.”

The creature lowered itself beside Them, keeping its injured ankle clear of the edge. Its broad face turned toward the distant water while the narrow one angled down toward the ring.

“Before the earth filled it.”

They nodded. Beneath the ring, a groove worn by rope curved toward the ramp they had climbed. The carving below was no longer their only evidence. Someone had hauled against this stone often enough to hollow it.

Along the terrace’s inner wall, a relief continued beneath a shelf of dirt. They brushed away what They could reach. A boat rested against a slope; a finned figure lay partly aboard it, holding a rope that passed into the hands of two upright figures. Beside them stood the familiar jar, its streams running in opposite directions.

Their throat tightened. They had mistaken so much ruined work for barriers because They had known the world only after it broke.

“They helped us out,” They said.

The creature studied the relief. “Or you helped us bring something in.”

They looked again. The boat carried jars. The rope joined the travelers without showing which had begun the labor.

“Both, perhaps.”

They sat with that uncertainty. It was gentler than the certainty They had carried through the dead coral streets: that shore and sea had always been separate, that Their solitude was the last shape of an ancient division. The worn ring beneath Their hand suggested ordinary crossings, repeated until stone gave way.

Below them, water clicked over a broken lip. They followed the sound to a narrow descent at the terrace’s end. This ramp was shorter than the buried groove and still clear enough to use. A pale trickle emerged from stone farther uphill, while a rust-colored ditch entered the stream downstream.

“We could reach the upper edge,” They said. “Stay above that ditch.”

The creature leaned forward, then pointed to a patch of smooth stone beside the reeds. “There?”

“There.”

They descended together, resting twice. At the bottom, the creature steadied Their elbow while They lowered the tip of Their fin into the moving water. Cold eased the dry ache. They waited, watching the sediment and the places where water touched Their scales. No sting came, though They knew that proved little.

The creature sat within reach, ankle resting on dry stone.

They made room beside Their tail and laid Their good hand between them, palm upward. Its fingers settled into Theirs. Beyond the reeds, the ruined coast remained quiet. They could not call it healed. But here was water they could examine, a bank they could share, and a place to rest before deciding what they could safely do next.
