## Chapter 1: The Last Song

The city had forgotten how to shine.

Anahya passed beneath an arch of antler coral, keeping her left side toward the stone so the current would not strike her worst gill. Once, light had run through these branches whenever someone swam beneath them. Blue for a stranger. Gold for someone coming home. Her mother had told her that, though Anahya remembered only the blue, and later the faint green rot that had replaced it.

Now even the rot was gone.

She pulled herself through with one hand. Her tail stirred a powder of broken shell from the avenue. It rose in little columns where the houses had been, then settled over thresholds too narrow for anyone to enter anymore. Above them, a torn sheet of plastic inflated and collapsed. For a moment it had shoulders, a bowed head.

She looked away before it could become someone she knew.

The burning began again beneath her jaw. She closed the scarred slits for three heartbeats, letting her body spend the small reserve it held, then opened them carefully. Water scraped through. There was a taste to it like a bitten coin.

In her belt, the old filter clicked against her knife.

She took it out. A palm-sized cup of pierced shell held a felted knot of fiber, black now instead of cream. She had rinsed it, scraped it, turned it inside out. Yesterday she had pressed it over one gill and felt nothing change. Today the fibers were coming apart beneath her thumb.

“Another day,” she told it.

Her voice sounded indecent in the empty avenue.

At the old gathering court she stopped. The singing stone stood where it always had, its hollow crown choked with silt. Anahya brushed the crown clear with the edge of her hand. As a child she had needed both arms to embrace its stem. Now she could circle it easily, and the discovery made her furious.

She put her mouth to the hollow.

The first note broke. She swallowed against the pain and began again.

The calling-song had no words until the end. It rose slowly, leaving spaces for answers: a higher voice from the gardens, a low one beneath the bridges, children entering too soon and being shushed. She held each space open longer than the song required.

Water moved through an empty window.

Something tapped inside a drain.

She sang the last words softly.

“I am here. Where are you?”

The stone carried the question down the streets.

Anahya waited until the ache in her gills became a cramp. Then she took her hand from the stone and swam toward the surface vessel.

It lay beyond the city, on the slope that climbed to shore. She had seen it two days earlier through a thinning of the brown water: a long iron belly split across the rocks, its forward half sunk and its stern still lodged above the tide. Land vessels sometimes carried things that cleaned water. She had taken one apart years ago with a woman whose name she still could not say aloud.

Inside had been white cylinders, close-packed as teeth. They had made foul water tolerable for a season.

She needed one cylinder. One unbroken tooth.

The climb exhausted her. With every stroke the water grew warmer and less willing to feed her. She passed a fish hanging vertically in a snarl of line, its mouth opening on nothing. She cut the line with her shell knife. The fish drifted sideways, then made a weak movement into the murk.

“Go,” she said, though there was nowhere better.

At the wreck she entered through a rent in the plates. Her coral spear caught on a dangling wire; she freed it with an impatient tug and laid it across the opening. Beyond, cupboards yawned above a floor made of walls. She searched by touch where the darkness thickened, finding glass, soft packets, a small shoe with its fastening still closed.

She left the shoe where it was.

The water-cleaner was bolted beneath a counter. Its case had burst, but two cylinders remained inside. One crumbled when she gripped it. The other held.

For a while she could only clutch it.

Then she worked the bracket loose, levering with her knife until a flake snapped from the blade. Rust bloomed around her hands. When the bracket finally gave, she struck the counter hard enough to split a knuckle.

She tucked the cylinder into a mesh salvage bag and gathered a length of narrow hose and a shallow metal basin. The filter would need a housing, perhaps a seal. She did not know whether it could still work. She knew only that she could not leave it.

By the time she retrieved her spear, the light outside had turned yellow.

Anahya surfaced beside the stranded stern.

Air lay against her face, dry and bitter. She drew a little through her mouth, enough to taste oil and ash, and coughed until she had to lower her gills into the wash. On shore, dead trees leaned above a beach crusted with pale debris. Farther inland stood the broken rim of a dome. Nothing moved between it and the sea.

Overhead, clouds folded into one another. The first thunder sounded behind the headland.

She hauled the bag onto a low shelf inside the exposed hull, then dragged herself after it. Rust rasped her belly. Her tail remained in a pool fed by each incoming wave; she shifted until the wash could reach her gills. Above her, loose iron knocked against iron.

“Shelter,” she said. “Then mend it.”

There was no one to agree, or to warn her that the tide might rise too far. She placed the basin under a leaking seam and laid the precious cylinder beside it. Her blood marked its white casing.

Rain began, tapping the hull in hundreds of small, hurried footsteps.

She turned before she could stop herself.

Only rain. Only the long, rusted chamber and her bag on the ledge.

Anahya took up the broken knife and began scraping a place for the filter, while outside the storm covered the sound of the sea.

## Chapter 2: The Two-Faced Land Creature

The first thing the storm gave her was clean water.

Anahya held the basin beneath the leaking seam and watched rain gather over its rust-stained bottom. She dipped two fingers, tasted, then poured a little along the raw slit beneath her left jaw.

The relief was so sharp she bowed over it.

Not enough to live in. Enough to remember that breathing had once been something she did without thought.

She wedged the white cylinder into her mesh bag, winding the hose around it. There was no fitting inside the stern that would hold it tightly enough. She needed a collar, a clamp, something from the vessel's stubborn little cupboards. Each new wave lifted her tail higher against the ledge. She would have to search quickly.

She had dragged herself as far as the next partition when metal scraped behind her.

Not the irregular knocking overhead. A single scrape, followed by silence.

Across the opening in the hull, someone stood on the rocks.

For a moment she saw only legs. Two of them, long and bare below a ragged garment, feet braced where the waves broke white. A land creature. She had seen their bones, their pictures sealed behind glass. Never one alive.

Then lightning showed its head.

There were two faces.

They rested side by side above a thick neck, turned slightly apart as though refusing the same view. One had eyes sunk deep in pale folds, ancient eyes in a face without age. Water ran from them steadily, down its cheek and throat. The other face was dark and dry. Fine grey ash escaped its cracked lips with every breath.

Anahya could hear her own gills opening.

The wet face looked at the basin. The dry one watched her hand move toward the spear.

It raised an arm.

She struck before it could finish.

The coral point glanced off the hull as the creature twisted. She lunged again, driving herself forward with her tail, and felt the spear enter flesh along its upper arm.

A cry came from both mouths, one sound breaking into two.

Then the air filled with salt.

Brine struck her eyes hard enough to blind her. Hot cinders followed, stinging her cheek, lodging in the wet hollows beneath her jaw. She flung herself sideways, clawing at her gills. Her shoulder hit a rib of iron.

The creature came through the opening.

She could not tell whether it meant to kill her or escape the rocks outside. There was no room in her terror for the difference. She drove the butt of her spear into its knee. It fell against the partition, caught the shaft, and wrenched.

“Leave!” she shouted.

Its wet mouth moved.

She bared her teeth and pulled. Coral tore its palm. A foot kicked the basin, spilling her small store of rainwater over the rust.

That hurt worse than the blow that followed.

She slashed at its gripping fingers with her chipped knife. It let go, and she struck its ribs with the spear shaft. The dry face exhaled a cloud that made her cough until black flecks spotted her tongue.

Beyond them, the sea withdrew from the rocks.

The sudden quiet stopped them both.

Through streaming eyes Anahya saw the exposed beach, the long channel gouged between wreck and shore, and beyond it the broken dome. Water was gathering farther out in a ridge too high to be an ordinary wave.

The creature reached toward her.

She raised the knife.

It pointed past her instead.

The surge hit the stern broadside.

Iron shrieked. The ledge broke beneath her, and she caught the mesh bag by its strap as the world became water and tumbling metal. Her spear dragged across her wrist; she closed her hand around it and curled over the bag.

For several heartbeats she knew where she was. She could swim. Beneath the churn there would be a current to follow.

Then a length of cable wrapped her tail.

It pulled tight, anchoring her beneath the falling hull. She saw a pale hand in the brown water, reaching past her knife. Fingers caught the cable and hauled it clear. She kicked free just as a plate slammed down where her tail had been.

The hand vanished.

She surfaced in a rushing channel, struck stone, and went under again. On her next rise she saw the creature being swept toward a low arch beneath the dome. It caught the edge, lost it, caught again.

Anahya drove her spear into a crack beside the arch. The shaft bowed. She clung to it while the creature scrambled inward, then hauled herself after it.

Behind her, a section of the entrance fell.

The impact shook grit from the ceiling. Water punched through the remaining gap, pushing them up a shallow flight of steps into darkness. Then the rush slackened. The fallen stones held, leaving only narrow mouths through which the storm spat foam.

Anahya lay in a pool with the spear across her chest.

The creature crouched two body lengths away. Blood striped its arm. Ash drifted onto the water and spread in tiny grey islands.

Neither moved.

As her sight cleared, she made out a carved border along the steps: seven waves beneath a ring of stars. She knew that mark from the roofless schools of her city. A place for watching the heavens. An observatory, built when her people still came this close to shore.

She could not imagine what they had wanted to see.

The creature touched its wounded arm. Its wet face studied her spear; the other shut its eyes.

“Not,” it said hoarsely.

Anahya tightened her grip.

“Not hurt.”

“You did.”

It looked at the cuts on its hand, then at her knife. For an instant its two faces wore the same exhausted expression.

She lowered the point a finger's width.

“You pulled the wire.”

A small nod.

“Why?”

Its mouths opened at different moments. Neither answered.

Thunder rolled through the stone above them. Anahya checked the bag by touch: cylinder, hose, the yielding tangle of her spent filter still at her belt. Something practical to count while another living thing looked at her.

“Others?” she asked. She pointed toward the blocked entrance. “Like you?”

The creature shook its head.

“Where are yours?”

The question was badly shaped in its mouth, but she understood it.

She looked down at the water between the steps.

“Gone.”

It drew its knees closer. “No place,” the dry mouth whispered.

Anahya thought of the empty gathering court, every doorway open to a voice that never came.

“No,” she said. “No place.”

After a while it pressed an uninjured hand against its chest.

“Khel.”

She had not expected a name. She sat with it through three uneasy breaths before giving her own.

“Anahya.”

Outside, another wave struck the fallen stones. She kept the spear across her knees, but when Khel shifted away from the cold water, she did not lift it.

## Chapter 3: The Drowned Observatory

The storm spent itself against the stones.

For a long while, the observatory entrance held only the sounds of dripping water, Anahya’s harsh breathing, and the faint, dry rasp of ash escaping Khel’s right-hand mouth. They had moved farther up the steps. Her shallow pool ended below them, where the floodwater stirred with rust and black sand. Khel sat with his injured arm pressed to his chest. Anahya kept her spear across her knees.

Neither trusted the quiet.

At last Anahya lifted her eyes to the walls. Beneath curtains of weed and mineral crust, carvings traveled upward into darkness: rings of stars, spirals of currents, and long-bodied figures carrying bowls of light. The script was old, but it belonged to her people. She traced one line with a wet fingertip.

“Observatory,” she said.

Khel’s two faces turned toward her.

“A place to watch the sky,” she explained. “And the tides beneath it.”

He glanced at the collapsed entrance. “Sky gone.”

“Not yet.”

She did not know whether she meant the sky or the possibility of reaching it.

The inner door had been forced open by the surge. Beyond it lay a round chamber, half flooded, its ceiling split by a jagged shaft where starlight would once have entered. Anahya slid down from the steps and crossed the threshold. Khel followed after a hesitation, limping but silent.

The floor was tiled with a map of the coast. Most of it had buckled. Inlaid shells marked reefs that no longer lived, while dark green stone showed forests beneath waters that now carried oil and plastic. Along the walls, murals unfolded in bands.

Anahya stopped before the first.

Merfolk and land-dwellers stood together beneath a bright, unbroken sun. Nets stretched between them. Children of both peoples held bowls toward one another. The sea was painted blue enough to hurt her eyes.

“Before,” she whispered.

The next band showed towers rising along the shore. Land-dwellers shaped stone and metal. Merfolk raised pillars of coral from the seabed. At first the work looked shared. Then the figures turned away. Spears appeared in merfolk hands. Smoke rose from the coastal towers. Ships crowded the water, their bellies spilling black streams.

Khel made a low sound.

Anahya moved to the third band. Merfolk stood in a crescent around a knot of blue fire. Their hands were lifted, their hair streaming as if in a current. Beyond them, a coastal city leaned toward the sea. The water climbed its streets.

She stared at the carved faces of her ancestors.

“They sank it,” she said.

The words tasted impossible. Her teachers had spoken of the shore as a thief and poisoner, never as a victim. Yet here the stone recorded a wave curling over roofs, while land-dwellers fled carrying children.

Khel pointed to the next scene. Towers along the shore poured dark lines into the ocean. Fish floated belly-up. A merfolk city dimmed beneath a cloud of black sediment.

“They poisoned it,” he said.

“Yes.” Anahya’s fingers closed around the spear. “They poisoned our water.”

“And you drowned their homes.”

The wet face said it gently. The ash face said it like an accusation.

She turned on him. “Your kind began it.”

“Your kind finished it.”

For a moment the chamber narrowed to his two faces and her own reflection trembling in the floodwater. Then a pale glow pulsed from the center of the room.

A crystal stood there on a low pedestal, no taller than her hand. It was clouded with age, but veins of blue light moved inside it. Anahya recognized the device around its base: a resonance cradle, used to preserve voices and impressions in the old observatories.

“Do not touch that,” she said.

Khel had already reached toward it.

His fingers brushed the crystal.

The chamber vanished.

Anahya stood on a shore beneath a red sky. The air boiled with ash. A line of merfolk rose from the surf, their arms linked, singing a note so deep she felt it in her bones. Across the beach, land-dwellers fed fires with black fuel. Their machines groaned like wounded whales.

The song struck the ground.

The sea rose.

Coastal walls folded inward. Harbors disappeared. But the land-dwellers answered with pipes and towers that released shining poison into the waves. The water turned silver, then green, then a color for which Anahya had no name. Coral cities cracked. The dead drifted upward through streets where sunlight had never reached.

She saw merfolk children hiding beneath a bridge of bone. She saw a land child clutching a red scarf as ash rain consumed the houses behind her. She saw hands—scaled and unscaled—reaching for one another through the catastrophe, too late.

Then the boundary broke.

Sea poured into sky. Ash fell upward. Currents crossed through stone. Every cry became one long sound, and within that sound were thousands of regrets.

Khel stood at the center.

Not as he was now, but as a small, shapeless knot of brine and soot. One face formed from the memory of the drowned merfolk child. The other formed from the land child in the ash rain. Their grief twisted together, refusing to separate. Around them gathered the last remorse of both peoples—the wish to have stopped, turned back, chosen differently.

Anahya heard a voice without a mouth.

Born of what they would not mourn.

The vision shattered.

She awoke on the floor, choking seawater that had not entered her lungs. The crystal had gone dark. Khel knelt beside her, both faces pale beneath their different stains. His hand hovered near her shoulder, not touching.

“What did you do?” she demanded.

“I saw.”

“Were you there?”

His wet eyes spilled faster. Ash trembled from the other mouth.

“I was made there.”

Anahya looked again at the murals. The old story inside her had been clean: shore as murderer, sea as innocent, her vanished people as the rightful mourners of the world. The carvings offered no such comfort. Both sides had raised their hands. Both had believed pain would make them safe.

“And the faces?” she asked.

Khel pressed both palms against his chest. “One remembers salt. One remembers fire.”

“Whose memories?”

He looked toward the broken shaft of sky.

“Everyone’s.”

Outside, the storm was leaving. A thin blade of daylight entered the chamber and touched the dead map beneath their feet. In its glow, a line of tiny symbols became visible beside the crystal: a destination, perhaps, or a warning. Anahya could not yet read all of it. She recognized only two words carved in the oldest script.

THORN REEF.

She closed her hand around her spear, but did not raise it. The world had not become kinder. The dead were still dead, the sea still poisoned, and Khel was still a creature born from a history she had never been taught.

Yet when he began to shake, she set the spear down between them and offered him her uninjured hand.

He stared at it with both faces.

Then he took it.

## Chapter 4: The Thorn Reef

By morning, the observatory had begun to remember the sea.

Water slid down the walls in silver threads. It gathered in the hollows of the old map, filling drowned valleys and making the shell-marked reefs shimmer beneath Anahya’s hands. Khel slept against a pillar, one knee drawn up, his wounded arm tucked beneath the ragged cloth at his chest. The seawater face rested with its eyes closed. The ash face watched the doorway.

Anahya did not sleep. Every time she shut her eyes, she saw the coastal city folding beneath her ancestors’ song. She saw the land child’s red scarf vanish under grey rain. She saw Khel as a knot of brine and soot, made from grief no living creature had wanted to carry.

She withdrew her hand from his.

His fingers tightened in sleep, then loosened.

“Do not make me regret that,” she whispered.

The words were meant for him, but the chamber gave them back in her own voice.

She returned to the wall beside the crystal. Once the first veil of silt had been washed away, more carvings appeared beneath it: a narrow passage descending through the observatory’s foundations, bordered by thornlike coral. At the passage’s end, a black reef rose from a lagoon. Tiny marks covered its branches, each one shaped like an eye.

Anahya pressed her fingertips to the image.

The coral was shown feeding on dark clouds that poured from broken pipes. Wherever it touched the poisoned water, green light spread. Behind it, thin shoots had been planted in rows across a barren seabed.

A record, not a dream.

She found a loose plate hidden beneath the map’s rim. It came free with a soft click, releasing a cloud of ancient grit. Inside lay a sheet of hammered shell, folded around a strand of hardened coral. The writing on the shell was cramped and faded, but she knew the old ceremonial hand. Her people had written it for those who might come after the end.

The Thorn Reef is the last garden that remembers the first ocean.

It was seeded before the wars, when the sea still carried songs from one horizon to another. Its branches learned the shape of clean currents. Its roots held the memory of spawning grounds, kelp forests, warm shallows, and creatures whose names had been forgotten everywhere else. When the waters sickened, the reef withdrew into a hidden lagoon and fed upon the poisons that sought it.

If any living hand finds it, the record continued, let that hand bring no crown, no weapon, and no claim of innocence. Bring what remains of both worlds.

Anahya read the final line twice.

The reef may teach the dead water to live.

“Teach,” she said bitterly. “As if water were a child that only needed better lessons.”

Khel stirred. The wet face opened first. “What found?”

She held up the shell plate.

“Something your people left behind.”

“Your people too.”

His ash mouth breathed a thin stream of grey. Anahya’s anger, which had been waiting beneath her ribs all night, found its door.

“Do not say that as though it makes us equal.”

Khel pushed himself upright. Pain crossed both faces differently: salt tears on one, a shudder of embers on the other. “Equal dead.”

“My people did not pour poison into the currents.”

“They broke the shore.”

“They fought back.”

“After.”

The word struck harder than a spear point.

Anahya snatched up the shell plate. “You know nothing of fighting. You hide in ruins and watch strangers bleed.”

Khel rose too quickly and caught himself against the pillar. “I watched you.”

“You attacked me.”

“You attacked first.”

“Because you were a monster.”

Both his faces turned away from her. For a moment, the only sound was water ticking into the map.

Then the ash face said, “Yes.”

Anahya’s anger faltered.

“I am monster,” Khel continued. “No home. No mother. No shore. No sea. When I wake, I am still here. When I sleep, children drown again.” His wet face looked at her. “You think lonely belongs to you?”

The question opened something she had sealed so deeply that even her grief had grown around it.

She looked toward the cracked shaft where the morning sky showed pale and distant. “The last song I sang had no answer.”

Khel waited.

“I sang it in the ruins of my city. I sang until my gills bled. I sang for my mother, for the nursery caves, for every voice that went silent when the water turned black.” Her grip tightened on the shell. “No one came. Not even the dead.”

“I came,” he said.

“You were there by accident.”

“So were you.”

She wanted to deny it. Instead she laughed once, without humor. It sounded like a stone breaking underwater.

Khel limped to the wall and studied the carved passage. “This reef. We go.”

“We?”

“Both.”

Anahya pictured the shore above them: its poison rain, its smoking bones, its land-dwellers’ descendants—if any remained—breathing behind masks while the sea died below. The old hatred rose easily. She could follow the reef’s trail, find whatever shore-born hands had helped bury it, and make them answer. Perhaps the Thorn Reef held not healing, but a weapon. Perhaps its green light could be turned against the land.

“I want the shore to suffer,” she said.

“I know.”

“I want them to understand what they stole.”

“I know.”

“And if the reef can make them feel it—”

“Then reef dies too.”

His certainty enraged her because it sounded like wisdom. “You would forgive them?”

Khel’s two mouths moved at once, but only the wet face spoke. “No.”

The ash face added, “Forgive not same as restore.”

He touched the picture of the black coral. His cut fingers left a faint red smear on the stone. “Maybe can make something. Not forgive. Make.”

Anahya stared at the little blood mark, at the image of roots crossing a dead seabed. Hope was a dangerous creature. It had teeth sharper than despair because it invited the heart to open again.

She folded the shell record and placed it inside her mesh bag beside the filter cylinder and hose. The bag was light, almost empty. Still, carrying the record made her feel as though she had accepted a burden from the dead.

“I will search for the Thorn Reef,” she said. “Not for forgiveness.”

Khel nodded.

“Not because your shore deserves saving.”

Another nod.

“I want to see whether hope is real before I waste my life on it.”

Khel looked at her with both faces. “Then we see.”

Anahya lifted her coral spear. Together they descended toward the hidden passage, leaving the dead map and the sleeping crystal behind. At its threshold, she glanced back once.

The observatory’s carved stars seemed to shift in the water. For the first time, the darkness between them looked less like an ending than a direction.

Ahead, beneath layers of stone and poisoned sea, something living waited with thorns.

## Chapter 5: Submerged Highways

The passage ended in a drowned road.

Anahya emerged beneath a low stone arch and found white lines running across the seabed. For a moment she thought them the ribs of something enormous. Then the current lifted a veil of grit, exposing a flat black surface, cracked into plates. Rusted vehicles stood along it in patient rows.

Above her, Khel climbed through a break in the arch onto a ledge. He kept his heads above water while she swam below him. The passage had risen gradually, following the tide toward daylight; now a pale sky lay trapped between buildings whose upper floors still stood dry.

“High road,” he said.

“Not anymore.”

His ash face made a small sound. After a moment she realized he was laughing.

The road curved away from the observatory headland. Beyond its sunken railings, deep water opened between towers, but here the tide scarcely covered Khel’s waist. He moved carefully, favoring his wounded knee. Anahya slowed without saying why.

A green sign hung from a bent frame. She studied its white scratches, waiting for them to become letters she knew.

“Which way?” she asked.

Khel pointed along the road.

“Does it say reef?”

“No. Hospital.”

“What is that?”

“Place for hurt.”

She looked at the dark windows beyond the sign. Curtains streamed from one of them, faded almost transparent.

“Did it work?”

“Sometimes.”

He spoke with a certainty unlike his account of the observatory, then frowned, as though wondering where the word had come from. Anahya did not ask. There were already too many dead traveling with them.

They passed vehicles with trees of rust growing through their wheels. Khel showed her how the raised strips along the road had once separated walkers from machines, how stairways behind doors could lead to roofs, how a red mark on a wall meant fire had once been kept in a small cylinder there.

“You read all this?” she asked.

“Some. Some I guess.”

He pointed to a rectangular shelter with a bench inside. “Waiting place.”

“For what?”

“Something that came back.”

Anahya looked away first.

Farther on, a slick lay across the road, silver at its edges and brown where the water thickened beneath it. She smelled it before Khel did: a sweetness that scraped her throat. Her gills clenched.

“Stop.”

He froze with one foot lifted.

Beyond the slick, the road dipped into a hollow filled with trembling colors. Violet slid over gold; green opened into blue. Nothing moved beneath them. No weed had taken the railings. Even the drifting scraps of plastic seemed to have caught at the boundary.

Khel’s wet face watched the colors. “Beautiful.”

“Dead.”

She caught his garment and drew him back. A narrow ribbon of cleaner current threaded beside a building, too shallow for her to swim without scraping her belly. Beyond it lay a fallen balcony bridging the worst of the pool.

“You go over,” she said. “I go around.”

He looked at the jagged concrete. “Your tail.”

“It has scraped before.”

Nevertheless, he waited on the balcony, testing each foothold while she dragged herself through the narrow channel. When her fin caught beneath a twisted railing, he lowered his unwounded arm. She used it to pull free.

Neither mentioned the first time he had freed her from wreckage.

On the far side she rested against a step, breathing hard. A thin red line uncurled from her tail and disappeared.

Khel sat above her. He did not ask whether she could continue.

Instead he pointed at a shape painted on the building: a blue creature with a smiling mouth and a curved fin.

“Was real?”

“Dolphin.”

She corrected the painted shape with her finger in the water: longer jaw, narrower body, a curve that held motion even in memory.

“They traveled in families. They stole fish and pretended innocence.”

“Like people.”

“Better at it.”

This time she knew his laugh when she heard it.

She named rays for him, and turtles, and the little cleaner fish that had once worked between her grandmother’s teeth. He listened gravely to that last description.

“Brave fish.”

“She was very gentle.”

The words surprised her. For so long, remembering her grandmother had meant remembering the bed where she died. Now there were her hands again, folded patiently while a silver fish inspected her mouth.

Anahya let the memory stay.

They found food in a half-flooded shop. Most of its packages had burst or dissolved, but Khel retrieved a small tin from a shelf above the old waterline. He inspected its seams and pulled a ring in the lid. Inside were pale beans in cloudy liquid.

He tasted one, waited, then offered the tin.

“Shore eggs?” Anahya asked.

“Seeds.”

She ate cautiously. They were soft and tasted mostly of salt. After a while she passed them back without counting how many remained.

They sheltered beneath a stairway while rain dimpled the street. Khel sat on a dry step; she lay in the water below, her spear within reach and her mesh bag tucked against her ribs. The folded record pressed through the netting beside the useless weight of the untested filter.

She should have tried it. She should have studied the shell again, measured the distance they had traveled, done anything but rest.

Instead she watched Khel turn the empty tin in his hands.

He set it beneath a drip from the stair above. Slowly, clear water gathered inside.

“For later,” he said.

Anahya nodded.

Beyond their shelter, the drowned road continued between broken buildings toward the open coast. They would follow it when the rain eased.

For now she listened to the drops striking metal, and to another creature breathing nearby. When Khel shifted to make room on the step, she laid her tired head beside his knee.

## Chapter 6: Ash and Salt

Rain worried at the broken roof all afternoon. It slipped through the ceiling in silver threads, struck the tin beside Khel, and filled it drop by drop. Beyond the stairway, the drowned highway blurred beneath gray water. Somewhere in the submerged rooms, something knocked whenever the current shifted.

Anahya woke with her cheek against Khel’s knee.

For a moment she did not move. His garment smelled of wet cloth, old smoke, and the mineral damp of ruins. Beneath her temple, his leg was warm. She had slept too deeply, and that frightened her more than the storm. Sleep belonged to creatures who expected morning.

Khel’s ash-face watched the rain. Fine gray powder escaped its cracked lips with every breath. His other face had turned toward her. Water gathered in its ancient eyes and ran down the seam of his strange body.

“You’re awake,” he said. The sea-face spoke first, its voice soft as water under ice.

“So are you.”

“I have less practice sleeping.”

Anahya pushed herself upright. Her tail cramped, and she hid the wince. “Because you are two people?”

The question settled between them. Rain filled the silence.

Khel’s ash-face gave a dry exhale. Cinders drifted over his chest. “Because I am not certain either of them is allowed to.”

Anahya looked from one face to the other. The observatory’s vision had shown him standing where two deaths met, but memory had been all light and ruin. It had not shown her the daily burden: two mouths opening on different griefs, two gazes carrying two endings.

“Tell me,” she said.

Khel’s fingers closed around the empty bean tin. “You may regret asking.”

“I regret most things I do not ask.”

The faintest tilt touched his salt-wet mouth. Then he leaned back against the stair.

“The face that weeps,” he began, “remembers the sea.”

His sea-face blinked. Fresh water spilled from its eyes.

“There was a child,” he continued. “A merfolk child. Small enough to hide beneath a table of shell maps. She had a red thread around her wrist, so her mother could find her in a crowd.”

Anahya’s fingers tightened on the mesh bag. The filter cylinder knocked softly against the hose.

“What was her name?”

“I don’t know.” Khel touched the seam between his faces. “Names were lost when we joined. I remember the red thread. I remember her mother singing seven notes to call her home.”

Anahya stopped breathing.

Not her song. Not exactly. But close enough that the old calling-song stirred in her throat, the one she had sung over dead coral and empty water.

“The tide had turned black,” Khel said. “Not night-black. Chemical black. It foamed around the city pylons. The child fell when the lower bridges gave way. Her mother reached for her, but the current pulled them apart.”

The sea-face shuddered. Water soaked the cloth at Khel’s shoulder.

“She drowned?”

“She tried not to. That is what the memory keeps.” His voice broke into two tones, one wet and low, one rough with soot. “She held her breath until her chest hurt. She thought someone would come. Someone always came before the water was poisoned.”

Anahya closed her eyes. She had known the sea’s silence, but not the shape of the first child who expected rescue.

“And the other face?”

The ash-face lifted toward the ruined ceiling.

“Land.”

Its voice was harsher, scraped raw by smoke. A thread of cinders curled from its mouth.

“A child on a roof,” Khel said. “The shore had become a furnace. The catastrophe had torn the boundary open. Magic came down with the ash—blue fire inside the gray. It burned through stone. It burned through skin.”

“Were the child’s parents there?”

“One was. Perhaps both. The memory has no faces for them. Only hands pushing the child beneath a table, as if wood could bargain with the sky.”

Anahya saw the observatory again: towers sinking beneath green light, ships vomiting poison, waves rising with the force of spells. She had wanted one villain. Hatred was easier when it had a single shore to point toward.

“The child died in the ash rain,” Khel said. “The merfolk child died in the poisoned tide. Their deaths met where the world broke.”

He pressed both palms to his chest.

“I was not made afterward,” he whispered. “I was made in the moment between. The sea carried one memory upward. The ash carried the other downward. Regret was the thread that bound them.”

Anahya stared at him. “You remember being them?”

“I remember being afraid in two directions.”

Rain intensified. Water ran down the stairwell and pooled around Anahya’s tail. Khel’s two faces no longer seemed monstrous. They seemed like doors left open in a burning house.

“Which one are you?” she asked.

“Neither. Both. The one who survived without surviving.” He looked at her with both faces. “The seawater is not my body’s water. It is memory. I weep what the child could not. The ash is regret. I breathe what the other child could not take back.”

Anahya reached toward her spear, then stopped. The instinct was old: stranger, danger, strike first. She had obeyed it on the shore and nearly lost him in the surge. She had obeyed it all her life, turning grief into a weapon because a weapon was easier to hold than an empty hand.

“I thought you were a punishment,” she said.

“Perhaps I am.”

“No.” She studied the water falling from his cheek. “Punishment does not ask whether anyone can be saved.”

Khel went still.

Anahya remembered the drowned city, the empty schools, the coral bones. She remembered the woman whose name she could not bear, and the cleaner they had dismantled together. Her grief had always felt singular, a chamber no one else could enter.

But Khel lived in two chambers at once.

“I know what it is to carry someone who is gone,” she said. “I have carried all of them.”

The sea-face’s tears slowed. The ash-face lowered its eyes.

Anahya extended one hand. Her knuckles were still split; salt stung the cuts. She touched his throat, where damp skin met a dusting of ash.

Khel flinched as if she had placed a blade there.

“I’m not touching a monster,” she said. “I’m touching my friend.”

The word frightened them both. It sounded too bright for the drowned shop, too alive for the road outside.

Yet Khel leaned into her palm. Salt wet her fingers. Ash settled across them like gray snow.

“Friend,” he repeated, testing it.

Anahya nodded. “For now.”

“For now is a beginning.”

They remained beneath the stairway until the rain softened. Then they rose, Khel taking the empty tin and Anahya the mesh bag. They left the shelter side by side, carrying two kinds of sorrow toward the open coast.

## Chapter 7: The Greenhouse of Ghosts

The shore opened before them at low tide, a long gray wound between the drowned highway and the sea. Khel walked above the waterline, favoring his knee. Anahya moved in the shallows, her tail dragging through mats of kelp gone white and brittle. The rain had stopped, but the air still tasted of iron, salt, and the sour reek of things that had died without sinking.

They found the greenhouse because its roof caught the morning light.

At first Anahya mistook it for another wrecked vessel. A skeleton of curved metal rose from the mud, half buried in dunes of ash. Glass panels leaned against one another like broken scales. Vines, black as drowned fingers, hung from the frame.

Khel lifted his empty tin and pointed. “Land people grew food in those.”

“Food?”

“Before the soil turned bitter.”

Anahya studied the structure. Beneath its collapsed eastern wall, green lettering showed through the grime: ARK NINE—COASTAL PRESERVATION. The letters had been painted by a careful hand. Someone had believed the place would last long enough to need a name.

Inside, the greenhouse smelled of dust, mold, and a faint sweetness that did not belong among the ruins. Rows of ceramic trays stretched beneath the fallen roof. Most held only powder. Some were stamped with pictures of leaves: bean, wheat, fig, reed. Others bore shell spirals and tiny coral branches.

“Seeds,” Khel said. His ash-face breathed a thread of gray over a tray, and the powder lifted like a dead breath.

Anahya reached toward a sealed glass jar. A label beneath it read WATER SAMPLE: NORTH BASIN, YEAR 3. The liquid inside had turned the color of old tea. Beside it stood another jar, cracked but empty, marked CORAL—LUMINOUS VARIETY.

Her chest tightened.

The land-dwellers had preserved coral.

Not merely for ornament. Not merely to study what they had ruined. The shelves were crowded with attempts: salt-tolerant moss, plankton cultures, filtered water, spores sealed in wax, fragments of reef wrapped in damp cloth. Some labels had been written in the sharp block letters of the shore. Others were in merfolk script.

Anahya froze before one of those.

MERIDIAN CITY CONTRIBUTION: WAR-CORAL COUNTERMEASURE.

She knew the symbol beneath the words. It belonged to the old battle schools, where young merfolk learned to shape current and pressure into weapons. Her city had taught that magic was a shield. Its masters had called the sinking of coastal towers a victory.

She pulled a metal drawer open.

Inside lay thin plates of treated shell, each etched with dates and observations. Khel came close, his sea-face wet with sudden tears.

“What does it say?”

Anahya read aloud. “The first dead zone formed after the western bombardment. The water remained warm for eleven years. No larvae survived.”

She turned the plate.

“Counter-current spell deployed beneath the shelf. Result: three-mile collapse of the seabed. Residual magic continues to poison spawning grounds.”

The greenhouse seemed to tilt around her.

“No,” she said.

Khel did not answer.

She seized another plate. “Industrial discharge increased the spreading zone. Shore refineries burned for months. This says that. It says the land made it worse.”

“Yes.”

“But this—” She struck the shell with a finger. “This says we made the first wound.”

The old familiar anger rose in her, searching for somewhere to go. It found no enemy. There were only hands: shore hands pouring black waste into rivers, sea hands lifting walls of water, both reaching past warning, both calling destruction defense.

A memory surfaced—not from the crystal, but from childhood. Her instructor standing beneath the blue lamps of the city school, telling them the land would understand only force. Anahya had believed him. Everyone had believed someone.

She dropped the shell plate.

“I don’t want this.”

Khel bent to retrieve it. “Truth?”

“The reef.” Her voice scraped her throat. “The search. All of it. We find the Thorn Reef and then what? I carry this knowledge to the dead? I tell the bones that their own songs helped kill them?”

She shoved past a rack of jars. Her shoulder struck the frame, and glass chimed in the dark.

“I wanted the shore to be guilty. I wanted one thing I could hate cleanly.”

Khel’s two faces watched her. “Clean hatred is still poison.”

She whirled on him. “Do not make it simple.”

“I am not.”

“You are made of regret. You should understand that regret changes nothing.”

The words struck him. His sea-face closed its eyes. Ash spilled from the other mouth and drifted between them.

Anahya’s anger collapsed as quickly as it had risen. She saw his injured knee, his split palm, the empty tin in his hand. She saw the two children he carried and the greenhouse full of efforts that had failed too late.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

“So am I.”

They stood among the dead seeds.

Then something clicked.

It came from beneath the rear bench: a small, wet sound, like a shell opening. Khel turned. Anahya followed him through hanging curtains of black vine to a chamber sealed behind a warped transparent door.

The door had been marked with both scripts. PRESERVE WHAT STILL BREATHES.

Khel pushed. The hinges shrieked. Inside, a basin of cloudy water rested beneath a cracked lamp. In the basin lay a shard of coral no longer than Anahya’s hand.

It was black, thorned, and faintly green.

The pulse was so weak she almost mistook it for reflected light. Then it brightened once. A tiny branch unfolded from the shard, delicate as a new finger.

Anahya sank beside the basin.

“Alive,” she whispered.

Khel knelt with difficulty. His sea-face wept into the water, and his ash-face held its breath. The coral’s green pulse steadied.

A tag hung from the basin: TRANSPLANT FAILED—MAINTAIN HUMIDITY. ORIGIN UNKNOWN. MERFOLK AND LAND STEWARDS AGREED: NO CLAIM OF OWNERSHIP.

Anahya placed her cut-knuckled hand against the glass.

“They tried,” she said.

“Some did.”

“Both sides.”

“Some on both sides.”

The distinction mattered. It did not absolve anyone. It made absolution impossible—and hope more honest.

Anahya looked toward the ruined coast, where the sea waited beyond the greenhouse walls.

“I won’t abandon the Thorn Reef,” she said. “Not because we deserve it.”

Khel touched the glass beside her. Salt and ash marked its surface.

“Because something is still breathing.”

“Because something is still breathing.”

They left the records where they lay and carried no seed, no jar, no fragment. The living coral belonged to the future more than to them. Outside, the tide began to return, sliding over the dead shore with a patient, poisonous hush.

Anahya tightened the mesh bag at her side. Within it, the filter cylinder and folded shell record pressed against her hip. Ahead waited the open coast, the wound beneath the sea, and somewhere beyond it the Thorn Reef.

This time, when she followed Khel, she did not pretend she was innocent.

She followed because he was not, either.

Together they walked toward what might still be healed.

## Chapter 8: The Barrier's Wound

The coast ended without ending.

Anahya and Khel followed it beneath the water, where the drowned road sloped away from the broken shore and became a pale scar across the seabed. Behind them, Greenhouse Ark Nine was already lost in the haze. Ahead, the sea darkened until it seemed not merely deep but wounded.

The wound announced itself by taste.

Salt turned metallic on Anahya’s tongue. Her gills clenched against a bitterness like rusted knives. Threads of yellow fog rose from the ocean floor and curled through the water, though fog had no right to exist below the surface. Wherever it touched the sand, the sand shivered and sprouted translucent fingers. A dead crab passed them with six new legs flowering from its shell.

Khel stopped beside a tilted tower that had once held highway lights. His ash face lifted toward the dim water. Fine grey powder escaped his cracked lips and hung around him.

“The Barrier,” he said. His sea-facing mouth spoke a heartbeat later, voice trembling. “The place where the worlds struck each other.”

Anahya looked down. The seabed had split open ahead. A black rift ran between cliffs of shattered stone, widening toward a chasm where no light reached. From it came a slow pulse. Each pulse drew the water inward. Each exhalation sent it back in a boiling surge.

“The Thorn Reef lies beyond?” she asked.

“If the record was true.”

“And if it was not?”

Khel turned both faces toward her. One was wet with seawater. The other breathed smoke.

“Then we have crossed a dead ocean for a story.”

Anahya almost laughed. The sound would have been bitter, but it loosened something in her chest. “You are learning to be encouraging.”

They entered the rift at the next inward pulse.

The current seized Anahya immediately. It twisted her tail sideways and dragged her toward the fissure wall. She struck stone with her shoulder. Her scarred gills flared open, drinking poison. Pain whitened her vision.

Khel caught her wrist, but the water tore him away. His knee buckled against a ledge. Above them, the yellow fog descended in thick ropes. Shapes moved inside it—small shapes, large shapes, shapes with fins and hands and antlers made of coral.

Whispers filled the water.

Not sound exactly. Pressure against the bones of Anahya’s skull. Names. Pleas. Accusations spoken in voices she had heard only in dreams.

Last one.

You left us.

Open the gate.

Her mother’s voice rose among them. Then the voice of the woman who had helped her dismantle the old water cleaner. Then the thin, terrified calling-song of children who had never found an answer.

Anahya kicked toward Khel. The current spun her, and for an instant she saw him multiplied in the fog: one Khel made of salt, one made of ash, one made of drowned children, one made of flame.

“Look at me!” she shouted.

His sea face found hers. His ash face faced the fog.

The two voices answered together. “I am trying.”

Another pulse struck. Khel’s fingers slipped from hers.

Anahya could have followed him, but the rift opened beneath her. A wall of water rose, carrying stones, bones, and scraps of ancient buildings. The current was not flowing around them. It was remembering how to break.

Khel pressed his wet palm against the water.

His sea-facing eye began to weep.

The tears did not vanish. They streamed outward in silver cords, threading through the current. Wherever they passed, the water slowed. The violent pulse softened into a shudder, then into a long, exhausted drift. The floating bones settled. The stones dropped.

But the ash fog poured toward them.

Khel’s other face opened its cracked lips. He inhaled. The fog rushed into him, and his thin body bent with the force of it. Ash packed his throat. Anahya saw both faces strain, each trying to save only half of him.

“Khel!”

He could not answer.

Anahya swam through the calming water and seized his shoulders. His skin was cold on one side, feverish on the other. The seam between faces pulsed beneath her hands.

“Do not fight separately,” she said. “You told me what you are. Memory and regret. Salt and ash. You are not two wounds.”

The ash face turned toward her. “What, then?”

“Both.”

The next current began to rise. Anahya wrapped one arm around Khel and anchored her coral spear in a crack. With her free hand she covered his sea-facing eye. With the other she touched his ash-stained cheek.

“I trust you,” she said.

The words frightened her more than the rift.

Khel went still.

Then his sea face wept—not helplessly, but with purpose. The tears spread in a broad veil, soothing the water into channels. At the same moment, his ash face exhaled. A dark stream poured from his mouth, not scattering into the fog but folding it inward, gathering poison into a narrow plume above them.

Salt and ash braided together.

The fog split.

Beyond it, the dead spirits surged forward. They were merfolk, land-dwellers, animals, perhaps memories of things with no bodies left to name. Their mouths opened in silent cries. Khel’s tears passed through them, and each spirit brightened briefly, relieved of its shape. His ash breath followed, carrying them upward toward the rift’s unseen ceiling.

The whispers faded one by one.

Anahya felt a hand brush her cheek. It might have been her mother. It might have been the woman from the shore. She did not turn to look. She let the touch go.

The rift convulsed. Khel’s body jerked, and the spear tore loose. They were pulled downward together.

Anahya wrapped her tail around a jut of basalt. Khel caught her waist. For three heartbeats they hung above the black depth, joined by grip, breath, and terror. Then the current changed. It carried them forward instead of down.

They shot through the Barrier’s Wound and burst into a quiet cavern beyond.

For a while neither moved.

Khel’s ash face was pale, his sea face dry. Anahya’s shoulder throbbed, and her gills burned as though filled with sand. Still, she laughed—a small, astonished sound.

Khel looked at her. “Was that encouragement?”

“No. That was surviving.”

Behind them, the rift closed to a narrow black seam. Before them, the cavern opened toward a green darkness. Far off, beneath curtains of clear water, something pulsed faintly: a living light, green as new leaves.

Thorn-shaped shadows rose around it.

Anahya tightened her grip on Khel’s hand.

“The reef,” she whispered.

Together, bruised and breathing, they swam toward it.

## Chapter 9: Thorn Reef

The passage narrowed until Anahya had to turn her shoulders. Stone scraped her bag; inside it the salvaged filter knocked against the folded shell record. She drew the bag close and followed the green pulse through the dark.

Then the ceiling fell away.

They rose into a lagoon enclosed by walls of black rock. Far overhead, a slit admitted a blade of daylight. Roots hung through it, dry and lifeless, their tips reflected in water so still that Anahya could see the exhausted shape of her own face.

Beneath her lay the Thorn Reef.

She had imagined towers. Even after everything, she had imagined avenues of living coral, branches broad enough to shelter a city, light spilling from windows where someone might be waiting.

The reef was scarcely larger than the floor of the room where she had slept as a child.

Black spines rose from a low mound, each tipped with green. Between them stretched pale membranes, thin as the skin beneath her eyes. Most hung slack. Whole branches had whitened at their bases and fallen into the sand.

Anahya stopped swimming.

Khel drifted beside her, holding his injured arm against his ribs.

“It is here,” he said.

“Yes.” She could not keep the disappointment out of her voice.

A ribbon of dirty water entered through a crack in the lagoon wall. She watched it descend, brown and oily, toward the coral. The nearest thorns inclined almost imperceptibly. Filaments uncurled between them and caught the stain. Slowly, the brown thread grew transparent.

Green light travelled along a branch.

Then it dimmed again.

Anahya lowered herself until the water passed across her gills. It still hurt, but the sharp metallic taste had lessened. She took another cautious breath.

Khel watched her face.

“Better?”

“A little.”

The words nearly undid her. A little had become an unimaginable measure.

She reached toward a broken twig lying beside the mound. Her fingers stopped above it. Its exposed end was grey; whatever life had been inside had gone.

“We cannot simply take pieces,” she said.

Beyond the coral stood a low stone table, half buried in silt. Two shallow channels crossed its surface and met in a hollow shaped to cradle a branch. Writing crowded its sides. Anahya swept away the sediment with her palm, uncovering the flowing cuts of her own language beside square shore letters.

She took the hammered-shell record from her bag and unfolded it. The injunction was still there: no crown, no weapon, no claim of innocence.

For a moment she looked at the spear in her hand. It had kept her alive. Its point had also been the first thing she offered Khel.

She carried it back to the entrance and laid it flat beneath a shelf of rock, beyond the living coral. Returning without it felt strangely like entering naked.

Khel had braced himself at the table. With one finger he followed the shore inscription.

“Not a storehouse,” he read slowly. “A nursery.”

Anahya traced the corresponding merfolk words.

“Grown to remember water before the burning.” Below that, smaller letters continued beneath a crust of salt. She rubbed them clear. “To carry its life beyond this bed, wake the severed branch with blood of water and blood of shore, mingled freely in the receiving hollow.”

Khel looked up.

She read the last line twice before speaking it.

“A branch taken otherwise will die.”

They listened to drops falling from the dead roots overhead.

“Both bloodlines,” Anahya said.

Khel turned his palm upward. The old injuries had left it puckered and tender. “I don't know what mine counts as.”

“Neither do I.”

He gave a small nod, grateful, perhaps, that she had not pretended certainty.

She studied the paired inscriptions. Different hands had made them: the shore letters were deeper, the merfolk script crowded to fit beside them. Someone had knelt here with someone else. Someone had expected survivors from both worlds to come.

“I wanted the sea to rise,” she said.

Khel waited.

“Before I met you. Sometimes I would lie in the ruins and imagine it going over every wall on shore. Every roof. I thought if there were nothing left above the water, the silence would feel fair.”

She looked down at the small reef. A membrane fluttered, failed to open, and settled against a thorn.

“I never imagined what came afterward.”

“Another silence,” Khel said.

“Yes.”

Her mother was still dead. The cities were still empty. Nothing in this lagoon would make those losses reasonable. She could keep them; she did not have to keep feeding them everything that remained.

“I cannot ask the dead to forgive anyone,” she said. “But I can stop making promises to them that only hurt the living.”

Khel moved closer until his shoulder touched hers.

For a while they rested that way, watching the coral gather another faint mouthful of poison. Anahya thought of the woman beside the dismantled water cleaner, both of them stubbornly sorting ruined parts. She let herself remember the woman's hands without turning away from what had followed.

Then she folded the shell record and secured it in her bag, beside the cylinder and hose. There might yet be use for all those small attempts to make water bearable.

She drew her chipped shell knife.

“Not all of us,” Khel said quietly, looking at the blade.

“No. Enough to try.”

She laid the knife beside the hollow, within reach of them both. Khel set his empty tin on the sand and offered her his hand.

Anahya took it. Above the waiting stone, their uncut palms rested together.

For the first time, she wanted to give the shore something that could live.

## Chapter 10: First of the New

The shell knife lay between them, its chipped edge pale in the green light.

Anahya looked at Khel’s two faces. The sea-facing one wept without sound. The ash-facing one breathed a thin grey thread that vanished before it reached the ceiling of the lagoon. Neither face looked afraid for itself. That frightened her more than fear would have.

“If it fails,” Khel said, “the branch dies.”

“Then we try again with another.”

“There may not be another.”

Anahya glanced at the black-thorned coral. Its faint pulse had weakened since they entered the lagoon. Each glow seemed a small decision not to go dark.

“Then we do not fail,” she said.

She picked up the knife. Her hand shook—not from doubt, but from the knowledge that every choice behind her had led to this hollow: the drowned city, the unanswered song, the wreck, the poisoned roads, the records that had made her ancestors guilty alongside their enemies. She had once wanted the shore to drown. Now she held out her palm to it.

Khel placed his hand beside hers above the receiving hollow. His fingers were scarred and blackened at the tips. Salt shone in the creases of one palm; ash dusted the other.

“Together?” he asked.

“Together.”

The knife passed through Anahya’s palm. Pain flashed clean and bright. Her blood fell first, dark red drops striking the stone channel marked for water. Khel watched her, then turned the blade toward himself.

His sea face closed its eyes. His ash face opened them.

The cut was shallow, but blood welled from it—red, then strangely silver at the edges, as though two memories were arguing inside it. It fell into the channel marked for shore. The two streams crept along separate grooves. At the center, they met.

For one breath, nothing happened.

Then the hollow drank.

The reef convulsed. Black thorns lifted from the lagoon floor, and a green radiance raced through them, branch to branch, until the whole hidden city shone like a star seen beneath deep water. The receiving stone cracked. Roots burst from its underside and drove into the dead seabed.

Anahya cried out as heat flooded her wrist. It traveled up her arm, across her shoulder, and into her throat. Her gills clenched. She fell against Khel, choking, while the water around them turned clear enough to reveal the white bones of old coral below.

“Breathe,” he said.

“I am.”

“Not only water.”

She understood too late. The green current entered her lungs. Instinct made her fight it, but Khel’s hand steadied the back of her neck. She opened her mouth.

Air entered.

It was cold, bitter, and full of the lagoon’s mineral scent. It hurt like a wound, then softened. Her gills opened beneath her jaw. The burning scars there began to fade. New tissue drew itself over them, tender and luminous. Along her ribs and arms, faint patterns appeared: branching thorns, no darker than seaweed shadows.

Anahya rose on trembling hands. She breathed water. She breathed air. Neither killed her.

Khel had stumbled away from the table. Both his faces were changing. The seam between salt and ash blurred, not vanishing but becoming a pale line, like a horizon. His tears no longer fell from one eye alone. His breath no longer smoked from the other mouth. Salt mist and ash curled together, silvering his cheeks.

“Anahya,” he whispered, and for the first time the name came from one voice.

She touched his face. It was warm beneath her palm.

“Are you still there?”

“I am here.” He smiled, startled by the shape of it. “Both of me. One of me. I don’t know.”

“Then be all of you.”

The reef continued to grow. Its roots threaded through the mud, finding buried poison. Where they passed, oily films broke apart. Dead anemones opened colorless mouths. A single translucent fish flickered from a crack and vanished.

The Thorn Reef had awakened, but it had not become a miracle large enough to save the world. It was a beginning. Beginnings demanded work.

Anahya climbed onto the nursery ledge. Her tail felt stronger, though her shoulder still ached and the knife wound throbbed. Khel lifted the first living cutting from the parent reef. Its thorns curled around his wrist without piercing him.

They carried it beyond the lagoon, through the green-lit passage and into the wounded sea. At the edge of the Barrier’s Wound, Anahya pressed the cutting into a patch of dead seabed. Khel held his hands over it. His tears fell as rain; his breath moved the poisonous fog aside. Together they waited until the root took hold.

Then they planted another.

And another.

They worked until Anahya’s blood dried in her palm and Khel’s knees gave way twice. They placed cuttings beside drowned towers, beneath the ribs of ships, along the highways where chemical rainbows once shimmered. Each one pulsed faintly, refusing the darkness.

At last they returned to the lagoon. The newborn roots had spread beyond sight. Green glimmers marked their path across the seabed, a constellation made not by distant stars but by hands that had chosen repair.

Anahya floated above the first nursery and drew breath through her mouth.

She sang.

The old calling-song was ragged at first. It carried the grief of empty cities and names no living mouth remembered. Her new gills trembled with it. Khel listened, one face now, his expression open to every note.

The final note traveled through the reef.

For a long moment, there was only silence.

Then, far beyond the lagoon, something answered.

It was faint—a wavering phrase from a young reef, perhaps, or a creature waking in water that had forgotten song. Another answer rose farther off. Then another, scattered across the ruined sea.

Anahya pressed her bleeding hand to the glowing coral.

She had been the last living merfolk.

Now the reef carried her song forward, not as an echo, but as an invitation.

Khel took her other hand. Together they listened while the dead ocean began, slowly, to reply.
