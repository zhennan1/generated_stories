At the beginning of each day, Iri cleaned the empty niches.

They began with the ones nearest the old council hall, where the coral had once been carved into shelves and doors. Now the hall stood below a green haze of water, its arches furred with weed. Iri brushed away the soft grit that gathered in the niches and lifted out the small, pale shells left by the current. Each niche had held someone once. Some had names scratched into the coral. Most had lost them.

For Sava’s niche, Iri used a narrow brush made from eelgrass. They worked slowly, careful not to scrape the last surviving letters. Sava. Four strokes, one for each sound. The name had been deeper once. It had been easy to read from the avenue, before the wall began to crumble.

When the dust had settled, Iri placed a small marker on the shelf: a dark bead of polished stone, no larger than a thumb joint. It had belonged to Sava. Iri had carried it through three floods and two collapses. Each morning, they set it in the same place. Each evening, they returned it to their pocket.

The gesture did not bring Sava back. Iri knew that. It did, however, give the day a shape. Clean the niches. Mark the dead. Check the supports. Find what the water had taken overnight. There were worse ways to spend the hours.

A black slick slid through the avenue beyond the hall.

It moved in a slow, oily sheet, turning the water above the paving stones dull and rainbow-bright. Iri watched it pass before continuing. The slicks were coming more often now. They clung to coral and weed, and their bitter taste stayed in the water long after the shine had drifted away. Iri kept their gills closed as much as they could and breathed through the cleaner current near the floor.

A crack sounded overhead.

Iri ducked. A piece of dead coral struck the avenue and broke into a pale cloud of sand. The cloud rose around the old fountain, obscuring its carved fish. When it thinned, the fountain was still there, but one of its fins had vanished.

Iri waited for the settling to stop. Then they went back to the niches.

Preservation was a kind of work. Work was a reason to get up. Iri had learned not to ask whether the reason was enough.

The next sound came from above the avenue: a hard, irregular clatter, followed by a shout.

Iri left the brush on the shelf and swam toward the opening where the roof had collapsed. Beyond it, the city’s upper structures broke the surface in jagged rows. The water there was fouled, a broad skin of dark color beneath the gray daylight. Something bobbed in it: a raft of splintered boards and torn metal, caught against the top of a coral pillar.

A person clung to the wreckage.

They had one face turned toward the shore and another facing back over the avenue. The forward face was pinched with strain, eyes narrowed against the light. The rear one searched the water below, mouth opening and closing as if it could taste the danger coming. Their body shook with every rise of the raft.

“Help!” the forward face called.

The rear face spotted Iri. “Don’t come through the shine!”

The two warnings arrived almost together. They were not two people arguing. They were one frightened person trying to watch every direction at once.

Iri looked at the water between them. A narrow channel ran under the floating slick, clear enough to cross if they stayed low and did not surface. But the wreckage was caught on the far side, where the polluted current pooled against the pillar. Iri could reach the person only by passing through a band of water that burned the gills and clouded the eyes.

The person’s fingers slipped on a wet board.

Iri kicked off before they could finish weighing the risk.

The water changed around them. The clean current fell away, replaced by a bitter film that crawled over their skin. Iri tucked their chin and drew short, shallow breaths. Their gills tightened. The wreckage shuddered as the trapped person shifted, and a metal strip scraped against the pillar with a sound like a blade being honed.

“Stay still,” Iri called.

“I am staying still,” the forward face snapped.

The rear face glanced down at their hands. “Not very well.”

Iri reached the pillar and caught the lowest board. It tilted under their grip. The person’s legs kicked above the surface, heels striking a broken spar.

“Your left,” Iri said. “There’s a ledge under the coral. Reach for it when I tell you.”

“I can’t breathe down there.”

“You won’t have to. I’ll pull you.”

The person stared at Iri with both faces: one wary, one desperate. Then they nodded.

Iri braced a foot against the pillar. “Now.”

The person let go.

For one instant, they dropped between the boards. Iri caught their coat and hauled them toward the coral ledge. Their weight dragged Iri’s shoulder down; polluted water surged past both of them. The rear face twisted to watch the wreckage spin away, while the forward one squeezed shut its eyes.

Iri got their hands under the person’s arms and shoved. Their chest struck the ledge. They scrabbled for purchase, coughing as the first breath of air tore out of them. Iri pushed again, and together they rolled onto the narrow shelf above the worst of the water.

The person lay curled on their side. Their two faces worked in different directions: the forward one gulped air; the rear one kept its eyes on the avenue, as though expecting the slick to climb after them.

Iri’s own gills spasmed. They dragged themself back from the edge, coughing bitter water from their mouth. The polluted film had left a chemical taste on their tongue.

“Can you stand?” Iri asked.

The forward face looked up. “Can you?”

Iri tested their shoulder. It hurt, but it held.

“Then we can both stand,” they said.

A remnant of the wreckage tapped against the pillar below. The slick moved over it, swallowing the boards one by one. Behind them, the drowned city lay in its green dimness, its empty niches waiting, its dead coral coming apart grain by grain.

Iri had left Sava’s marker on the shelf.

The stranger pushed up on one elbow, still shivering. “I’m Pell.”

“Iri.”

Pell’s forward face studied them; the rear face watched the water.

Neither offered thanks. Neither asked what came next. For now, Iri kept one hand on the ledge and the other near Pell’s shoulder, ready to catch them if their strength failed. Pell kept both faces turned outward, searching for the next danger.

They had made it out of the water together. For the length of a few breaths, that was enough.

The maintenance niche lay six steps above the ledge, behind a buckled service door. Pell climbed with one hand against the wall and the other clamped around Iri’s wrist. At the fourth step their knees loosened. Iri braced, felt the pull in their injured shoulder, and waited until Pell could move again.

Inside, a stone bench stood above old tide stains. A rain pipe from the upper terraces dripped into a basin furred with mineral crust. Iri had kept the basin clear for years; sometimes it held the only clean water along this avenue.

Pell obeyed. Their forward face was pale beneath the grime; the rear face watched the doorway. When Iri reached for their coat, Pell caught the fabric shut.

“I need to see where you’re bleeding.”

After a moment, Pell let go.

Iri paused, then tipped the cup to show them.

They worked more slowly. The cloth Iri used for polishing niche stones made two narrow bandages. As they knotted the second, their thumb searched automatically for the smooth edge of Sava’s marker. It was still on the shelf. They had left it where a surge could take it.

Pell followed the movement of their empty hand.

“It can wait.”

Iri rinsed their hands and wetted the aching folds of their gills. Beyond the doorway, water struck the lowest step with a soft slap.

Iri glanced at the torn coat, the bare ankle.

“I thought there might be another copy.”

Pell spoke carefully, watching Iri’s expression. Their rear eyes kept returning to the strip of water visible beneath the door.

They drew a key from inside their coat. Green corrosion crusted its shaft; one tooth had worn almost smooth. On the bow, three short strokes stood beneath an arch.

Iri’s fingers stopped above the bandage.

They knew those strokes. Silt collected in them on the bronze doors beneath Council Walk. Each time Iri cleared the doorsteps, they brushed the marks clean too, though no one had passed through those doors since the civic archive was sealed.

“Where did she get that?”

“From the work office here. Before they left.”

“That’s an archive key.”

Pell leaned forward. Hope sharpened their expression so suddenly that Iri looked away.

Alongside the familiar emblem, tiny shore letters crowded the key’s rim. Iri distrusted their neat angles. Notices nailed over the outfalls had carried the same script while the water beneath them thickened and the coral mouths ceased to open. Whatever those notices had promised, no one had stayed to explain it.

“Can you take me there?”

“It’s below water.”

“How far?”

“Farther than you can hold your breath.”

Pell’s hand closed around the key. For a moment Iri expected bargaining. Instead, Pell looked down at their bandaged ankle.

“Is there another way in?”

Iri almost said no. Then they pictured the service passage above the archive entrance: a narrow rise into darkness, with shore lettering stamped beside three handles. Sava had warned them away from the handles. One opened a flushing gate. The other two remained mysteries.

A movement behind Pell interrupted the thought. Watching through their rear eyes, Pell lifted one hand.

“The water’s reached the next step.”

Iri checked. A brown tongue slid over stone that had been dry when they entered. They had less time than they’d thought.

Pell noticed a metal plate beside the service door.

“Does that vent still open?”

“Why?”

“It says to open it before releasing the lower hatch. There’s a pressure warning.”

Iri studied the scratches they had passed a thousand times. Beneath the bench lay a hatch they had never managed to lift.

“You can read all of it?”

“Most. The last line’s worn away.”

The qualification steadied Iri more than a quick assurance would have.

“There are markings at the archive,” they said. “On the service controls. I don’t know what they mean.”

“I can try.”

“I’ll take you to the doors. You read them aloud before touching anything.”

“And you tell me before taking me under.”

Iri nodded.

They pulled a coil of maintenance line from a peg and tested it, a length at a time. The fibres held. Below the floating slick, the arcade offered a short crossing through cleaner water. Beyond it was the rise where air gathered above the archive vestibule. They described each turn, watching Pell measure the distance in breaths.

“If the air space is flooded, we come back,” Iri said. “If I pull twice, you follow the line.”

“Twice,” Pell repeated. Their fingers tightened around the rope Iri offered. “You’ve been there?”

“As far as the doors.”

Pell was quiet long enough for another drop to ring in the basin. Then they tucked the key away and began securing the line around their waist.

Iri checked the knot and moved to the threshold. They would have to return for Sava’s marker. For now, they counted the submerged steps, finding the places where Pell could put a foot.

Behind them, Pell struggled upright. Iri went back and offered their uninjured arm.

They had agreed on signals: one tug meant stop, two meant follow, three meant return.

At the passage’s end, steps rose toward the archive vestibule. A trembling pocket of air remained beneath the ceiling. Iri surfaced first and waited while Pell joined them, coughing once.

“Still breathable?” Pell asked.

“For now.”

They inspected the vestibule. Water dripped through a sagging ceiling, but no current entered the chamber. The archive doors stood ahead, their lower halves submerged. Beside them, Pell found a warning plate beneath a crust of salt.

“Open the shore vent before releasing the lower hatch,” Pell read. “Pressure equalization required.”

“The vent is inside,” Iri said.

“Then the door was meant for two people.”

Iri tied the maintenance line around their waist and submerged. Beneath the door, their fingers found a wheel clogged with silt. They forced it around until a hidden mechanism clicked into alignment.

“Now,” they called.

Pell placed the old key in the shore-facing lock and turned it. The doors shuddered but did not open. Iri pulled an inner lever, and the entrance groaned apart, releasing a surge that dragged them sideways. Pell held the line until Iri caught the frame.

Neither of them could have opened it alone.

Inside, the civic archive had suffered less flooding but more decay. Shelves leaned beneath swollen tablets, and copper tubes hung from the ceiling like dead roots. On the walls, inscriptions appeared in paired columns: flowing merfolk marks beside angular shore script.

The first diagram showed gates connected to treatment channels, channels feeding containment basins, and basins linked to tide regulators. It was a single system, designed for joint operation.

Pell brushed salt from a repeated symbol. “This means intermediary.”

“Your family?”

“Some shore families. We carried measurements and orders between crews and merfolk engineers.” Pell pointed to a damaged line. “My name is here.”

The words ended where the slate had been broken. Pell stared at the fragment, their expression tightening.

“This is why the missing work record matters,” they said. “It may show what happened to the intermediaries.”

Iri examined the remaining panels. They described shared inspections and repairs, but the sections explaining the later accusations had been scraped away or dissolved by chemicals. Nothing showed why the shore communities blamed the merfolk, or why the merfolk believed the shore had poisoned them deliberately.

“The truth was removed,” Iri said.

“Or never written.”

They followed a maintenance mark into a ruined avenue. Refinery slick covered the safest central route, spreading in an iridescent sheet across the water. Iri studied the movement beneath it and found a narrow current along the eastern wall.

“We go from pillar to pillar,” they said. “Stay above water until the third. Then six breaths underwater.”

“Six?”

“Four, if the current turns.”

Pell looked displeased but nodded.

Iri crossed first, feeling the pressure with their palms. Pell followed between breathing places, lowering themself into the dark and emerging with controlled, shallow breaths. At the third pillar, they reached a broken coral arch.

A crack sounded overhead.

“Back!” Iri shouted.

The arch collapsed. Water exploded through the avenue, and Pell vanished behind coral and masonry. Iri turned back immediately, though the current pulled toward the slick.

They found Pell pinned against a fallen slab, still gripping the maintenance line. Iri hauled them toward a pocket of clean air beneath the wreckage.

“Breathe here,” they said.

Pell drew air and looked past Iri toward the blocked passage. “The way out is closing.”

“Can you see anything?”

Pell twisted around. From that angle, they spotted a shore-built brace half buried under the arch, its chain still attached to a release pin.

“There. Lift the brace. I can hold the chain.”

Iri dove. The metal was hot with chemical corrosion, but they levered it upward while Pell seized the chain. Together they kept the gap open long enough to crawl through.

They reached the tide-watch station after nightfall. The raised structure overlooked the drowned avenue. Its air chamber still worked, and a dry platform remained above the tide marks. They settled there, exhausted, while their clothes steamed in the stale warmth.

Pell placed the key between them.

“My mother searched for our work record because she believed it proved we had not abandoned the merfolk,” they said. “The authorities claimed the intermediaries betrayed both communities. Then the record vanished.”

Iri watched the key. “I know shore people mostly through Sava’s warnings.”

“What warnings?”

“That shore authorities promised cooperation only until they no longer needed it. That every bridge could become a road for soldiers.”

Pell glanced toward the dark window. “Sava may have been wrong.”

The station’s paired maintenance diagrams matched those in the archive. Beside them lay thin coral plates marked in Sava’s hand. Iri read the surviving records aloud. They described joint repairs, shared patrols, and repeated merfolk complaints about refinery discharge.

The industrial authorities had forced toxic waste through treatment channels beyond their capacity. Orders had required the channels to remain open even when the containment basins were full.

Iri touched the coral plate, feeling their certainty crack.

Sava had taught that the system failed because the shore broke faith. Yet Sava had known the channels were being overwhelmed. Why had the mentor never said so?

“Did they lie?” Pell asked.

“I don’t know,” Iri said.

Outside, the tide moved through the ruins with the sound of distant breathing. On the wall, the paired scripts continued side by side until both ended at the same blank fracture.

The maintenance diagrams led them beneath the tide-watch station, through a service gallery where water pressed against Pell’s ribs. Iri went first, one hand on the wall, reading the current by its pull against their fingers. Pell followed with the key tied at their wrist. Ahead, the passage narrowed around a damaged conduit.

A tremor rattled the stone. The current quickened, and a thick, green-black pulse spilled through a split in the conduit. Iri recoiled as it clouded the water. A sting ran across their palm where the slick had touched.

“It’s not a steady leak,” Pell said, peering at the gauge marks. “The level drops between surges.”

Iri matched the marks to the diagram. The failing containment structure was releasing one concentrated volume, finite but large. The current carried it east toward the cleft where Iri could still breathe without pain. The outgoing tide would spread it west through the salt marsh below Pell’s home.

“Both routes,” Pell said.

The diagram showed an emergency basin built into the old foundations. Its inlet had to be opened, its overflow kept sealed. On the inner side, a submerged wheel and bypass pin controlled the flow. The shore-side lock and counterweight lever were marked in Pell’s script. The key fitted the lock.

“We need both controls,” Iri said.

Pell studied the instructions. “I can read them.”

The spring-tide mark was unmistakable. The next high tide would drive the pulse past the diversion point in less than a day.

They reached the basin through a gallery choked with rubble. Iri cleared grit from the wheel’s teeth while Pell scraped algae from the capacity marks. The chamber was mostly empty, its floor ringed by old waterlines. Pell compared the paired measures; Iri checked them against the conduit gauge.

“There’s room for this release,” Pell said. “Not another.”

The highest mark was stained with grit from some earlier flood. Iri looked at the narrow margin between it and the basin’s rim. If the overflow opened, the waste would escape toward the refuge and marsh. If the inlet stayed shut, the pulse would split along the two channels.

A wrapped coral plate lay behind the wheel housing. Iri recognized Sava’s careful script and eased it free. The record described an earlier emergency: Sava had approved sealing an outer merfolk district to preserve the inner city. The decision had saved those within the walls. The district beyond them had been abandoned.

Iri continued reading. Shore crews had helped evacuate merfolk families, opened the land-side gates, and balanced pressure through the passage. Sava praised their courage. A later hand had added that shore authorities ordered their names removed from the public account.

Pell held the lamp closer. “My ancestors were there.”

The record did not soften Sava’s choice, but it changed the shape of it. Sava had trusted the shore crews enough to work beside them, then chose who would be left outside. The public ritual Iri had kept for years honored the rescue and hid its cost. They had touched the marker, spoken Sava’s name, and mistaken an incomplete memory for the whole of their mentor.

“They saved the inner city,” Pell said. “And abandoned the outer one.”

“I can read.”

“Then why are you angry with me?”

“I’m not.” Iri folded the plate’s wrapping back over it. “I’m angry that you can say it as if the choice were simple.”

“It wasn’t my choice. And this release isn’t waiting for us to settle the past.”

Iri had no reply that did not sound like a defense of Sava. They left Pell with the key and lamp and took the passage toward the familiar ruins. The old route bent past broken columns and pockets of trapped air. At its end stood Sava’s coral marker, pale against the dark water. Iri had touched it after every daily ritual, as if the gesture could keep the past whole.

A roar shuddered through the passage. Iri turned as the surge drove polluted water between the ruins. Pell was trapped beyond a failing sluice, one hand clamped around a rail, the other still gripping the key. The rail bowed under the force.

“You didn’t have to break it,” Pell said.

Iri looked at the ruined niche. “I did.”

Pell could have taken the key and left. Instead, they turned toward the damaged passage. “The outer brace is slipping. We can’t reach the lock if it goes.”

Together they secured the brace, Pell following the shore marks while Iri tested each hold against the shifting current. Then they stood at the gallery’s mouth, facing the route to the controls.

“I’ll take the wheel and bypass pin,” Iri said. “You work the lock and counterweight.”

Pell closed their hand around the key. “We open the inlet. Keep the overflow sealed.”

The spring tide was drawing nearer. They had not yet diverted the pulse, and the basin could take only this one release. Their histories remained unresolved between them, but each had turned back for the other. They set out together, relying on what they had done, not on what either had promised.

The passage divided at the third pillar. Beyond it, the maintenance gallery sloped toward the submerged wheel; above, a narrow stair climbed to the shore control. Iri took the water route first, as they had before, feeding the line through their hands until it ran taut between them and Pell.

They rose to the trapped air and dragged in a breath.

A hollow crash sounded behind the wall.

At first Iri thought another part of the lock had broken. Then fragments began to drift out of the maintenance gallery: a strip of woven binding, a pale wooden splinter, a curled leaf covered in Sava’s cramped writing.

The lower archive.

The diverted pressure had reached the connected galleries. Somewhere behind those walls, water was climbing the shelves.

Iri watched the leaf turn. They recognized the red thread sewn through its edge. Sava used it for corrected channel surveys, the ones she said must never be copied without their annotations.

There were rolls below, too. Work rolls, household tallies, names preserved in the hope that someone might return to claim them.

Pell’s family roll might still be among them.

The stair to the archive lay only a short swim away. Iri knew the upper shelves, the loose door, the cupboard where Sava kept the oldest records wrapped in oilcloth. They could reach it. They could carry something back.

Their fingers loosened.

The wheel moved beneath them.

Immediately the chain above the housing rattled upward. A hard thread of current struck toward the refuge branch.

Iri seized the rim again.

The flow stopped.

They stayed with their cheek against the cold iron, understanding exactly what release would do. The pressure had not yet fallen enough for the gate to hold itself.

Another bundle burst from the gallery. Its wrapping had split; pages streamed behind it, separating in the dark.

Iri closed their eyes, then opened them. They watched until the pages disappeared.

At the shore, Pell held the lever and counted through the worst of the surge. Below, Iri kept the wheel against its stop while the basin filled.

At last the iron grew still beneath their hands.

Iri waited through another count before letting go.

Iri lifted one hand from the wheel, then the other.

The iron held.

They kept their palms close enough to catch it if it moved. The chain above the housing hung motionless; the narrow current that had threatened the refuge branch did not return. Only a faint vibration remained, passing through the spokes into the water.

Iri counted slowly, twice. Then they left the wheel and followed the inspection rail toward the basin.

Beyond the grille, waste lay suspended in dark, sluggish folds. Pale clots gathered beneath the surface. The basin’s highest measuring notch had disappeared, and the water trembled just below the stone lip above it.

It had held the release. There was almost no room left.

Iri checked the overflow seal by touch, working along the joint where the plate met its frame. No current pressed through. The packing remained seated. They stayed long enough to distinguish the small movements of settling water from a leak, then returned toward the shore ladder.

Pell crouched beside the mechanism, one shoulder braced against its housing. At Iri’s approach, Pell looked up but did not release the lever.

“Does yours hold?”

“Yes. Take your weight off slowly.”

Pell straightened a little. The lever settled against its catch with a dull click.

Both waited.

Nothing sprang loose. Pell eased away completely, then bent to inspect the pin.

“It’s seated,” Pell said. “The bracket’s bent, but the pin is through.”

Only then did Iri climb onto the landing. Their fingers resisted opening. Pell reached toward them, hesitated, and offered a forearm instead. Iri took it.

For a while they sat beside the mechanism without speaking. Water dripped from Iri’s sleeves into the grooves between the stones. Farther out, a loose sheet of metal struck a piling whenever the receding swell lifted it.

“We need to check the basin from above,” Iri said.

Pell nodded and got up with visible difficulty.

At the inspection slit, they lowered the marked sounding rod. Pell held the lamp while Iri read the wet line. Then they checked the dry channel beyond the overflow plate. Dust still lay in its corners.

Pell scratched the level and the tide mark onto the mechanism’s wooden cover.

“If it rises above this, we’ll know.”

“If someone comes to look.”

“I’ll come.”

Iri glanced at Pell’s shaking hand.

“Both of us,” they said. “We’ll have to watch the walls, too.”

Below them, the basin gave off a sharp mineral smell that caught at the back of the throat. Whatever settled there would remain dangerous. The walls needed watching; the seals would need inspection after every tide. Someone would have to find a way to remove the waste without sending it through the refuge.

“This basin’s finished taking releases,” Iri said.

Pell pressed the rod into its rack. “Then that goes in the warning.”

Beyond the sea wall, oily streaks crossed the shallows. A dead weed bed rolled slowly under the wash. The water outside had received no cure from what they had done.

They returned to the sheltered recess where they had left the things carried out of the archive before the diversion: two wrapped bundles, a board of hurried notes, and loose fragments pinned beneath a broken tile.

Pell spread a relatively dry cloth over the landing.

They worked carefully. Readable leaves went on one side, damaged pieces on the other. Where ink had begun to lift, Iri left the sheets untouched. Several fragments had fused into a soft mass inside their wrapping.

Pell tried to lift one corner, then stopped.

“Leave it,” Iri said.

“There might be names underneath.”

“I know.”

Pell lowered the corner. Neither suggested going back into the flooded galleries. Iri could still picture the red-threaded survey turning in the current. Knowing which passage had swallowed it did not mean they could retrieve it.

Near the bottom of the second bundle, Pell found a strip of stiffened paper ruled into columns.

For a moment Pell only stared.

“What is it?” Iri asked.

“The work roll.”

Pell brought the lamp closer. Along the torn edge, half a heading survived. Below it were household marks, shifts, deductions, and names.

Pell touched the cloth beside one entry.

“My grandmother used to draw that mark on our storage jars.”

Iri leaned closer. The same mark appeared beside three assignments to the shore receiving crews. Farther down, one damaged line recorded a transfer of labor through a shore household.

“Intermediaries,” Pell said. “Some of ours arranged the crews.”

There was no satisfaction in having guessed correctly.

“Can you read who was sent?”

“Two names. The others are gone.”

The missing portion included the payment columns and whatever conditions had accompanied the transfer. They could establish a role, but not every act performed within it.

Pell set the fragment down with both hands.

“They told us the records would clear everything up.”

Iri looked at the ruined bundle. “These won’t.”

Beside the roll lay two surviving pieces in Sava’s writing. One listed refuge access points, supplied to the industrial office. Iri had already seen the authorization beneath it. The other recorded Sava’s corrections to the emergency channels—the careful measurements that had helped them keep the gate closed.

Iri laid them side by side.

“She gave them the entrances,” they said. Saying it aloud hurt differently from reading it. “And she helped preserve the diversion.”

Pell studied the leaves. “Keep both.”

“The shore families signed agreements, too.”

“Yes.” Pell’s voice tightened. “But the authorities owned the works, set the deductions, and could close the food landing. That matters.”

“It does.”

“And afterward, when they copied the histories, they left out the orders. Kept our signatures.”

Pell smoothed the cloth around the work roll.

“Some people still used what power they had against their neighbors,” Pell said.

Iri nodded. Neither the agreements nor their later erasure made the old alliance harmless.

They placed the readable fragments between the dry boards. Outside the recess, the tide continued falling. Pell added the basin level to their notes, leaving space beneath it for the next inspection.

When the tide had drawn far enough down the channel to uncover the lower stones, Iri and Pell returned to the basin.

They went slowly. The water had pressed at the works all night, and a still surface proved little. Pell carried the gauge slate; Iri had the hooked pole and a length of cord marked at measured intervals. They stopped above the retaining wall and listened. Beneath the stone, something clicked once.

“That could be the gate settling,” Pell said.

“Or the seal shifting.”

Neither tried to make the other feel better.

The basin’s surface was dark beneath an oily sheen. A slow swirl circled the submerged mouth of the diversion channel, but no stream entered it now. At the overflow notch, the sealing cloth remained wedged under its iron bar, soaked black and holding. Iri pressed the pole against the bar. It resisted. Pell compared the waterline with the mark cut into the stone.

“No rise,” they said.

They measured from the opposite side. The readings agreed. Pell recorded the level and the hour, then left space for the next inspection.

“We’ll check through the next tide,” Pell said. “The level and the seals.”

The basin had taken the release that might have gone straight into the sea. It had not made the sea clean. Beyond the channel mouth, the receding water left a greasy stain between the stones, bright as polished shell. Dead fish lay tangled in the weed. Iri made themself look long enough to remember where.

They returned to the sheltered recess where they had left the papers. Their wrapping had kept most of the fragments dry, though salt had curled the edges and darkened one corner. Pell lifted each piece carefully. The family work roll had split along an old fold, so they laid it between two dry boards.

Its narrow columns were crowded with names and marks. Pell compared the legible lines with the paired symbols they had noticed before.

“This confirms the transfer,” they said. “Shore households arranged crews for the works. Ours was one of them.”

There was no satisfaction in being right. Pell touched the ragged edge where the rest of the record should have been.

“We can’t say what everyone did,” Iri said.

“No. The payment columns are gone. So are the conditions.” Pell traced a surviving mark without touching the ink. “We know labor passed through our household. We don’t know who chose, who obeyed, or what refusal would have cost.”

They had hoped the roll might unlock the past. Instead, it gave them a narrow view through a broken door.

Iri brought out Sava’s papers. One listed refuge access points, the ink still sharp beside the industrial office’s authorization. The other held Sava’s corrections to the emergency channels, measured so carefully that they had helped keep the diversion gate shut.

One page showed what Sava had given away. The other, what she had helped preserve.

“She gave them the entrances,” Iri said. The anger was familiar, but no longer simple.

“Yes,” Pell replied.

“And she helped save the diversion.”

“Yes.”

Iri placed the pages beside each other. Neither erased the other. Gratitude could not excuse the betrayal; betrayal could not make the later work disappear.

Pell studied the authorization. “The authorities owned the works. They set the deductions and could close the food landing. They had power no shore household had.”

“That difference matters,” Iri said.

“It does. But some people still used what power they had against their neighbors.”

Iri looked again at the list. A name had been crossed out and replaced with another. The paper could not say whether it was correction, warning, or something else. They would not make the uncertain mark speak more clearly than it could.

The later histories had kept the shore signatures and left out the orders that shaped them. The omission protected the authorities and made the families carry more blame. But that erasure did not turn the old alliance harmless, or every family choice innocent. To claim otherwise would only make a different false account.

They gathered the fragments between the boards. Pell added a note: “Labor transfer confirmed; duties, payments, and conditions incomplete.” Iri wrote beside Sava’s papers: “Access information supplied to the office. Later channel corrections preserved. Motive and full sequence unknown.” They left room for additions, not guesses.

Pell wrapped the records in fresh cloth, tying it loosely so the damp edges could breathe.

“Tomorrow, before the tide turns?” Pell asked.

“We check the level, the bar, and the seal,” Iri said. “And record that the release into the basin has ended—as of that inspection.”

Pell wrote the wording down.

They carried the notes back toward the settlement. Behind them, the basin held its dark burden without spilling. Beyond it, the sea withdrew through stained channels, carrying what the basin could not contain. Iri thought of the missing columns—not spaces to be filled by invention, but absences that had to remain visible. The basin would need watching. The history would need keeping. Neither was finished.

Before the next tide turned, Iri and Pell went to the basin. The water stood below the old stain on the stone, a narrow difference Pell measured twice before recording it. Iri inspected the bar across the inlet, feeling along its joints for vibration, then pressed both palms against the seal where the channel narrowed. Nothing moved beneath their hands except the slow pressure of the sea. The basin held its dark burden.

“Release into the contained basin stopped as of this inspection,” Pell said, writing carefully.

“Add the tide.”

“I have.”

“And the limitation.”

Pell looked up. “That the surrounding sea is not safe?”

“That we have not established it safe. And that the containment is permanent.”

Pell added the sentence: “This observation applies to the basin and this inspection only; it does not establish the condition of the surrounding sea or the permanence of the seal.”

“Keep it,” Iri said.

The wording felt small beside the black water, but small truths were less dangerous than large assurances.

Over the following days, the sea withdrew through stained channels and left ridges of dead growth exposed to the air. A sheen gathered in hollows and broke apart under the wind. The basin remained still, but Iri knew stillness was not recovery. A poisoned place could be quiet while its harm settled deeper.

The refuge was quiet too. Water dripping from a cracked ledge carried through the rooms; kelp against the outer wall rasped like a hand seeking entrance. Iri woke when lamps guttered and listened for a change in the current.

Pell worked over the fragments they had carried out. Each rested on mesh above a controlled current, with fresh damp cloth kept away from swollen edges. Salt had whitened some lines; other pieces had curled until their writing appeared only when viewed obliquely. Pell copied the legible marks, leaving gaps where the surface was gone and signs beside letters that might be one thing or another.

When a name appeared in two forms, both went into the record. When a heading survived but its column did not, Pell did not invent the missing entries.

“You could make a likely reading,” Iri said.

“I could make a smooth reading,” Pell answered. “That is not the same.”

Their pen paused over a broken line. “A smooth account would hide where the record fails.”

Iri thought of the later histories, which preserved shore signatures while omitting the orders that shaped them. The omission had protected authorities and left families carrying more blame. Yet the old alliance had not become harmless, and every family choice had not become innocent. Sava’s papers could show that access information reached the office and that channels were later corrected. They could not prove what Sava feared, whom Sava intended to protect, or whether protection had ever been the true motive.

Pell wrote the uncertainty into the margin.

Iri returned to the niches. The passage had narrowed beneath fallen shell and pale sediment, so they cleared it by hand, carrying each loosened piece away before it could slide back. Some niches held markers dulled by years of water. Others held only hollows. At the empty ones, Iri still bowed their head, but the gesture no longer asked the darkness to return what it had taken.

Sava’s marker lay behind a shelf of collapsed stone. Iri found its edge, then cleared around it with a soft brush, loosening silt grain by grain. They never dragged the bristles across the surviving letters. The name emerged unevenly, some marks worn almost smooth, the rest unmistakable.

Iri traced the air above it. The marker could not explain Sava’s decisions, settle whether the alliance had been coercion or compromise, or restore names omitted from later histories. It preserved one particular loss, and that was what made it worth carrying back.

They wrapped the marker in clean cloth and carried it to the refuge. Pell cleared a place beside the rescued fragments, but not among them. It was not evidence for an argument. It was a name, and the absence of the person who had borne it.

After that, their separate duties began to overlap. Pell joined Iri at the basin and recorded its level, bar, and seal. Iri sat with Pell during the fragment work, identifying residue from living water where Pell could not. They disagreed over intervals and interpretations, but neither treated the other’s knowledge as ornamental.

Their friendship was real, though new enough to contain awkward silences. Some silences were companionable; others held things neither knew how to say. They had lost different parts of the world, and neither could make the other’s grief smaller by naming it.

Iri remained the last living merfolk. The refuge offered no promise that another would arrive, and the clean water offered no promise that the poisoned channels could be restored. Each morning carried the same fact forward.

One morning, near the refuge wall, Iri found a living coral shoot in a seam no wider than a finger. Its branches were translucent at the tips, green shading into faint rose where the clean current touched them. Tiny polyps opened and closed along its surface.

It was not a city, nor proof that cities would return. It was one living thing making a beginning without knowing whether the beginning would be enough.

Iri brought the ritual bowl. They cleaned the ledge, changed the water around the shoot, and lifted fine grit from between its branches. The movements resembled those once used at an empty niche, but their meaning had shifted. Iri was tending what remained capable of growth, not only honoring what was gone.

Pell stood nearby with the record tablet.

“Should I mark it?”

“Yes. But do not call it recovery.”

“What should I write?”

“That it is alive. Where it grows. What we do to keep the water habitable. Nothing beyond that.”

Pell wrote, leaving room beneath the entry.

Together they watched the coral open to the current. Behind them, Sava’s marker rested beside the incomplete records. Ahead, the basin required another inspection, and the sea offered no guarantee beyond the next tide.

Iri touched the clean water with two fingers. The work would continue. It was not a promise of restoration, only an undertaking. For now, that was what they had, and they would keep it.

On the fourth morning after Pell’s first entry, Iri returned with the ritual bowl. The refuge was quiet except for the small clicking sounds of grit moving through a crack in the wall. Beyond the reach of the clean current, suspended sediment obscured the stone, turning the distance the color of old bruises.

The coral shoot stood in its narrow seam. Iri steadied the bowl beneath it and eased a strand of soft weed along the surrounding rock. Loosened silt settled into the bowl. They worked slowly, keeping the weed clear of the translucent tips, pausing whenever a polyp drew inward.

Their hands remembered the niches: the patient clearing of edges, the search for grit in carved letters. Once, finishing had meant leaving a clean emptiness behind. Here they had to watch for something small enough to harm without noticing.

Sava’s marker lay on a higher shelf beside the rescued fragments, its wrapping folded underneath. After cleaning the seam, Iri touched the cut of the name. They had not ceased tending the dead by tending this shoot. The old questions returned as readily as ever; there was simply another task beside them.

Pell arrived with the record tablet and waited until Iri withdrew their hand.

“Does it look longer to you?” Iri asked.

Pell held a measuring strip against the rock without touching the coral. One branch bent in the current, then straightened.

“I cannot be certain. The water is moving differently.”

Iri watched the tip sway. They had wanted the answer too quickly.

“Then put that down.”

Pell recorded the open polyps, the translucent tips, and the uncertainty about height. Below this they noted where sediment had collected. The empty space beneath the entry no longer seemed to Iri like a promise waiting to be fulfilled. It was room for whatever they found next.

On the shelf, the salvaged fragments rested in shallow trays. Folded cloth supported the weakest pieces, keeping their crumbling edges from taking the weight. Pell copied the marks whose edges survived, setting doubtful readings between two short strokes. Where stone had broken away, they left gaps. Iri held each fragment steady, resisting the urge to complete familiar phrases from memory.

One phrase came back in Sava’s voice, complete with the impatient pause before its final word. For a moment, the missing marks felt like a failure of Iri’s own attention rather than damage to the stone.

“You can add what you remember beside it,” Pell said. “Not as part of the stone.”

Halfway through the next fragment, Pell stopped. The stylus remained suspended. Iri did not ask why. They lowered the fragment and stayed until Pell reached for it again.

Later they went to the basin. Black waste lay in heavy folds within it; a slick clung to the inner wall. Nothing dark had crossed the reference scratches on the outer stone.

Iri followed the lower seam while Pell compared the upper joints with the earlier record. At one narrow line Iri hesitated.

“That joint is meant to move,” Pell said. “Watch the edge beside it. That’s where a change will show.”

Iri needed that knowledge. Fear made every crack look newly opened; familiarity could make an old danger disappear. They checked the marked edge together.

“No visible seepage,” Pell read back afterward.

Not safe, Iri thought. Not forever. Only what they had seen.

They divided the work by reach and strength. Iri would clear loose matter from the inlet and inspect the lower wall. Pell would compare the upper joints with the marks and keep the tide records. Before changing anything near the containment, they would consult each other. If a fresh stain appeared, cleaning would stop until they understood where it came from. Neither knew how to repair every possible failure.

Back at the shelf, Pell took the sediment bowl.

“I can empty this. You can stay.”

Iri nodded. Their hand rested beside Sava’s marker. It was an ordinary offer, but they were grateful not to have to explain why they needed a few more moments there. Pell carried the bowl away, and Iri remained until they could turn toward the living seam again.

Beyond the refuge, the sea remained damaged. No familiar voice came through the ruined passages. Iri was still the last living merfolk. The shoot might grow into a branching colony; it might die before the next season. Its survival was worth tending, but it could not answer that absence.

When Pell returned, they arranged to meet at the next slack tide. Pell would bring the earlier seam measurements; Iri would check the inlet first.

“And the smaller measuring strip,” Iri said. “For the shoot.”

Pell set it beside the tablet.

Iri steadied the emptied bowl beneath the seam and lifted a final thread of silt from the stone. Pell stayed nearby, leaving the silence unfilled. One polyp opened against the current. Iri waited for the grit to settle before moving their hand again.
