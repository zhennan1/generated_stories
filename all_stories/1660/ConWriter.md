The envoy leaned toward the display. “It responded to the scanner.”

“Or the scanner exposed a stored response.”

“You said it could manipulate memory.”

“I said I believe it can. The pulse alone does not prove that.”

“Your own memory is disputed.”

The accusation landed cleanly because I had carried it here myself. Orison’s command deck rose behind my eyes—the red lights, the order to fire, the certainty that I had heard the relief captain authorize engagement. Then the image lost its edges. I could not tell whether the relic had touched that memory or whether guilt had worn it smooth over the years.

“I will not use an uncertain memory as evidence against your people,” I said. “Do not use one as evidence against mine.”

The envoy looked from me to the dark object. “And what will you use?”

“The record. Independent observers. Repeated tests. Anything that survives being witnessed by people who want different answers.”

That seemed to trouble her more than a threat would have. She ordered the scanner stopped and had the readings printed onto sealed polymer strips. I signed the human chain-of-custody panel, then watched her add the Veyr seal beside mine. The relic remained isolated. Its pulse remained unexplained. But the inspection had produced something neither side could erase without leaving proof.

Outside Chamber Seven, the blockade still surrounded Khepri’s Fall, and the medical corridor would depend on a document traveling through hostile hands. I rested my palm against the cradle’s handle.

“Next,” I said, “we test whether the order reaches the outpost before the convoy launches.”

The envoy did not promise success. She opened the communications panel herself to transmit the queued copies, and together we waited until the first acknowledgment from Khepri’s Fall confirmed receipt before launch.


The acknowledgment from Khepri’s Fall remained on the panel long enough for me to read it twice. Order received. Launch held pending escort confirmation. Beneath the text, the outpost controller had attached the convoy’s cargo manifest: oxygen cartridges, surgical packs, replacement seals for the failing habitat locks. No weapons. No passengers on the inbound leg. I pressed my thumb against the edge of the printed corridor order until the polymer bent.

“The outpost has it,” I said. “Does the watch commander?”

“The transmission was addressed to both.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

The envoy closed the panel with a sharp movement. For an instant I thought I had spent the last of her patience. Then she faced the guards. “Chamber Seven remains sealed after we leave. No further scans. Record any visible change without approaching the cradle.”

I looked at the reliquary. Nothing moved within its black facets. The residual pattern existed on our jointly sealed strips, but I still could not say what it meant. “Paired observers,” I reminded her.

“They remain here.”

One guard stayed beside the manual recording apparatus; another took position at the threshold. The envoy opened the door, and I followed her across the isolation line, carrying my copy of the inspection record. We passed through a short pressure vestibule into a narrow passage. A third guard joined us there, rifle resting across his chest. Behind us, Chamber Seven’s locks engaged in sequence.

The communications gallery lay one deck above. We climbed a curved stair whose steps were too shallow for my boots, then entered beneath a display of the blockade. Khepri’s Fall hung at its center, a scarred lump of ore surrounded by ship markers. The medical approach was a thin white line through overlapping weapons envelopes. Seeing it at this scale made my stomach tighten. A pilot could hold the assigned vector perfectly and still spend the entire approach inside firing range.

The envoy led me to a terminal separated from the main stations by a transparent partition. “You may witness communications here. You may not address tactical personnel without authorization.”

“Then ask for acknowledgment of the signed order.”

She entered the request. An answer appeared almost immediately.

RECEIVED. IMPLEMENTATION SUBJECT TO WATCH REVIEW.

I let out a breath through my nose. “That isn’t escort confirmation.”

“It is not refusal either.”

“Tell the convoy to keep holding.”

She looked at me before relaying it. I hated giving that instruction. Every delay cost air at the outpost, but launching into an argument between Alliance officers would make the freighters hostages to our paperwork.

A voice sounded from the terminal, clipped by translation. “The manifest lists mining-rated pressure vessels.”

“Oxygen cartridges,” I said.

The envoy raised one finger toward me. “Watch commander, identify the discrepancy.”

“Such vessels can conceal fragmentation charges. Human vessels have previously used civilian declarations to close engagement distance.”

The guard behind me shifted his feet. I kept my hands on the terminal ledge. Somewhere in the Alliance’s histories there would be a real incident underneath that warning. Somewhere else, a retelling would have made every human freighter into the same ship.

“May I answer?” I asked.

The envoy opened my channel.

“Compare the cartridge serials with the depot’s dispatch record. The convoy can transmit both without leaving its holding position.”

“Records can be falsified.”

“Yes. What inspection would satisfy you without requiring the freighters to cross your guns first?”

The terminal remained quiet. On the display, an escort marker moved away from the corridor’s outer end. I watched it, trying not to imagine a targeting officer doing the same calculations.

“You are the captain censured at Orison,” the commander said.

My collar felt suddenly tight. I was younger than most of the officers in this gallery, and my disgrace had traveled farther than any commendation ever had.

“Yes.”

“Then you understand our caution.”

“I understand why you want evidence. You have my censure in front of you. Give the cargo records the same attention.”

The envoy’s lower eyes turned toward me. She did not interrupt.

The commander requested the depot seals, container masses, and an external image of each rack. I relayed the requirements through the authorized channel, then waited while the convoy crews worked. The first images arrived with gloved hands still visible beside the serial plates. I checked each number against the manifest as the envoy checked the Alliance translation. One blurred digit required another photograph. No one called it sabotage.

At last, the terminal sounded again.

“Escort assigned to the outer inspection point. Engines and weapons emissions will be monitored. Passage remains revocable by this watch.”

“Read back the signed non-engagement conditions,” the envoy said.

The commander did. I followed every clause on my polymer copy.

Only then did I authorize the convoy to leave its hold. On the blockade display, the first freighter’s marker edged toward the white line. The envoy saved the acknowledgment beside our corridor order and placed her seal on the record.

I set my copy beside hers, keeping the approaching freighter in view. It had permission, an assigned escort, and a commander who had repeated the conditions aloud. It had not yet crossed a single gun.


The first freighter advanced three ship lengths before its marker turned amber.

I gripped the terminal ledge. Beside the corridor order, a new line appeared: DRIVE VARIANCE—ASSESSMENT PENDING. The escort slid across the freighter’s projected course, placing itself between the convoy and Khepri’s Fall. Nothing fired. On the larger display, though, two overlapping weapons envelopes brightened around the medical approach.

“Keep my channel open?” I asked.

The envoy touched the authorization field. “For this exchange.”

“Convoy lead, report the variance. No compensating burn until the escort acknowledges.”

The reply arrived through a hiss of interference. “Port maneuvering assembly is cold. Automatic trim requested additional thrust. We rejected it. Holding present output.”

I looked at the freighter’s telemetry. Its heading remained inside the permitted band, but the plot showed a slow drift toward the edge. Holding output would not keep it there indefinitely.

The watch commander spoke before I could ask. “Additional thrust could conceal a rapid approach.”

“It could,” I said. “What correction will you authorize?”

“Maintain current output pending assessment.”

At Orison, I had mistaken urgency for permission. I could still feel the command chair’s restraint against my ribs. Here, every instinct demanded that I tell the pilot to correct the drift and argue afterward. I flattened my hands on the ledge.

“Then record the drift rate,” I said. “The ship is complying. It must not be classified as a deliberate deviation while it obeys your hold.”

The envoy leaned closer to the screen. “Commander, acknowledge that distinction.”

A pause followed. “Recorded. Current deviation is not classified as hostile.”

Behind the partition, an officer pointed toward a recessed station below the main display. The envoy followed the gesture, then turned to me. “The inspection telemetry is clearer at the liaison console. You will remain separated from tactical controls.”

She opened the gallery’s side gate. I took my polymer records and followed her down the short ramp into the liaison alcove, with the guard one pace behind. From here, the blockade display rose above us like a ceiling. The local console showed the freighter’s actual attitude rather than the simplified marker: a blunt cargo hull, frost along one flank, correction jets dark.

The envoy transferred our authorized connection. I waited for its green border before speaking.

“Convoy lead, transmit the maneuvering assembly maintenance entry and the temperature reading. Passive instruments only.”

“You suspect a fault?” the commander asked.

“I suspect we should compare the warning with something other than a story about human freighters.”

The envoy’s fingers stopped above the console.

I heard how it had sounded. “The history may be accurate,” I added. “This ship still needs its own assessment.”

The commander did not answer, but the request remained open.

The maintenance entry arrived with a depot timestamp and a warning the pilot had acknowledged before departure: reduced cold-start efficiency, starboard correction preferred. I put it beside the engine trace. The port assembly’s temperature matched the warning. No weapons emissions appeared on either the convoy’s record or the escort’s independent feed.

“Send those together,” I told the envoy.

She checked the identifiers before attaching her seal. I added mine. Neither signature made the machinery safe; they established which evidence we had actually examined.

“Starboard correction only,” the commander said at last. “One pulse, within the transmitted limit. Escort will match.”

The pilot read back the limit. I watched the numbers climb, stop, and settle. On the overhead display, the freighter’s path bent gently toward the corridor center. The escort moved with it. The bright weapons envelopes remained, but no longer tightened.

Only then did I notice the ache in my jaw.

“Correction complete,” the pilot reported.

“Received,” the commander said. “Proceed to outer inspection point. No clearance beyond that point is implied.”

I saved the exchange before another message could displace it.

The envoy watched me copy the record. “You expected the commander to treat it as a threat.”

“I expected someone to call obedience a threat.”

“And you believe the distinction is now settled?”

“For that correction. Not for the next one.”

She placed her seal beneath the saved instruction without offering agreement.

At the edge of my records lay the sealed strip from Chamber Seven. The reliquary’s residual pattern showed faintly through its cover. I turned it facedown. Whatever the secret machine could do to memory, it had no place in proving what this pilot had just done. The chamber remained sealed under paired observation; I would not ask anyone to approach it while frightened crews waited beneath guns.

The freighter reached the outer inspection point. Its marker stopped beside the escort, still amber. The second vessel waited farther out, engines steady, carrying habitat seals that Khepri’s Fall could not manufacture.

I requested acknowledgment of the completed correction and inspection arrival. The commander supplied both. The envoy authenticated them beside our original order.

We had made one part of the corridor work. No one had yet been allowed through.

“Now,” I said, keeping my voice level, “please give the pilot the inspection sequence before you ask the pilot to move again.”

The envoy entered the request herself. Above us, the escort’s inspection lights came on.


The inspection lights came on in sequence, washing the freighter’s hull in pale bands. They revealed every dent, patch, and frozen seam. Through the liaison console, the escort’s cameras fed us three angles at once: cargo spine, engine housing, and docking collar. The ship looked less like a weapon than a tired animal being examined under bright lamps.

“Inspection sequence transmitted,” the envoy said. “The pilot has acknowledged receipt.”

I read the order from the shared screen. Engines at idle. Weapons safed and externally verified. Cargo images first, then pressure-seal readings, then a paired boarding team only if the watch accepted the remote evidence. No movement beyond the outer point without a separate clearance.

“Add the final restriction,” I said. “No attitude adjustment during inspection unless the escort orders it.”

The envoy glanced at me. “That is already implied.”

“Implied conditions become accusations when something twitches.”

She entered the amendment rather than arguing. Her seal appeared beside mine, followed by the watch commander’s acknowledgment. I saved the record to the convoy file and sent a copy to Khepri’s Fall’s command room. The outpost had been waiting for the oxygen cartridges, but waiting did not mean we could let a frightened crew improvise inside a weapons envelope.

A sharp tone sounded from the inspection feed. One of the freighter’s external cargo cameras had lost focus. Its view blurred across the central panel, turning the containers into pale blocks. At the same instant, the escort marker flashed amber.

“Camera fault,” the pilot reported. “Request permission to cycle the external mount.”

“Denied,” the commander answered.

I looked at the pressure readings. The cargo spine remained sealed. The escort’s images showed no change in the ship’s attitude, but the Alliance officers were already watching the lost feed as if it were a hand reaching for a concealed switch.

“Can the camera be reset from the inspection console?” I asked.

“Negative. Manual access only.”

The commander’s channel opened. “Manual access would require movement.”

“Then hold the vessel where it is,” I said. “Use the escort’s thermal feed to verify the spine while the pilot keeps the camera untouched.”

“Thermal resolution is insufficient to confirm container seals.”

“Insufficient is not the same as hostile. We have two independent pressure readings and the external mass profile. Ask for a second angle from the escort before you ask the freighter to move.”

The envoy repeated my request through the authorized channel. Her voice remained even, procedural, but I could hear the strain in the pauses between translated phrases. The Alliance had built its blockade around a legend: humans approached under flags of aid, then opened hidden compartments and turned civilian proximity into slaughter. Every pause in their evidence became another page in that legend.

The escort’s second camera swept across the freighter. Its thermal outline showed the cargo spine intact, with no heat blooms behind the containers. The watch commander requested the convoy’s passive pressure telemetry and compared it against the escort’s instruments. I watched the figures populate line by line. They matched within the expected tolerance.

“Record the camera failure as equipment-related,” I said. “No corrective maneuver authorized.”

The commander answered after a long silence. “Recorded provisionally.”

“Provisional records still need a basis.”

The envoy’s gaze settled on me. “You are pressing him.”

“I am making sure the record says why the ship remains under watch.”

“And if he refuses?”

“Then the refusal goes in beside the evidence.”

She did not smile, but she authenticated the exchange.

The escort’s inspection beam shifted to the docking collar. Frost feathered the outer ring. A seal indicator showed green, then flickered yellow. My pulse kicked hard enough to make the console edge tremble beneath my hand.

“Pressure fluctuation at the collar,” the commander said.

“Pilot, report.”

“Thermal contraction in the outer gasket. Internal pressure stable. We can compensate with a quarter-degree rotation.”

“No,” I said.

The word escaped before authorization. The guard behind me lifted his rifle, and the envoy’s hand stopped above the channel control.

I forced myself to breathe. “Correction: no movement without watch approval. Transmit the internal and external pressure traces. The escort can compare them.”

The commander came back immediately. “You issued an unauthorized order.”

“I withdrew it before the pilot acted.”

“That distinction is familiar to officers who have already violated engagement limits.”

The reference to Orison struck cleanly. I kept my eyes on the telemetry instead of the guard.

“Yes,” I said. “That is why I withdrew it.”

For several seconds, no one spoke. Then the envoy opened the formal response channel. “Watch commander, the captain’s statement is not a maneuver authorization. The vessel remains stationary. Please assess the requested evidence.”

The commander accepted the clarification without apology. The freighter transmitted its traces. The collar’s pressure had shifted by less than the safety margin, and the escort’s instruments showed the same change. A maintenance note identified the gasket as one of the seals replaced before departure.

I attached the note to the inspection packet. The envoy checked the translation, then placed her seal beside mine. Jointly authenticated evidence accumulated in small, stubborn pieces: the acknowledged hold, the engine fault, the authorized correction, the camera failure, the stable pressure readings. None of it erased the myths haunting the blockade. It gave the myths less room to speak.

“Inspection may proceed to cargo verification,” the commander said. “The convoy remains stationary.”

Only then did I straighten. The liaison alcove’s side hatch opened onto the receiving-bay observation corridor, and the envoy gestured toward it. I took my records and crossed the threshold with her and the armed guard, the open hatch and the active inspection feed marking the transition from communications oversight to remote cargo verification. On the corridor display, the freighter waited at the outer inspection point, still barred from Khepri’s Fall.

At the corridor console, I requested the first container image. The pilot sent it. Oxygen cartridges filled the frame, serial plates clear, seals unbroken.

“Log the image,” I said. “Then request the next rack. No movement yet.”

The envoy authenticated the record. Above us, the blockade ships held their positions, and the outpost’s dwindling air remained the reason every careful second mattered.


The next rack image arrived distorted by static. I enlarged it without touching the freighter’s controls. Six cartridges appeared behind the blur, their serial plates partly hidden by a restraint bar. The inspection software marked two seals as unreadable, and the escort’s amber indicator brightened again.

“Do not classify the unreadable plates as a discrepancy yet,” I said. “Request the same rack from the escort’s forward camera.”

The envoy routed the request through the signed corridor order. Her hands moved quickly over the console, but she kept her body angled so I could see every command before it left the station. The armed guard remained behind us, rifle held across his chest. I felt the weapon’s presence without looking back. It was a reminder that cooperation had not become trust, and that my first mistake at Orison still occupied more space in the Alliance’s judgment than the evidence accumulating here.

The forward camera answered with a sharper image. The plates were not concealed; the restraint bar had been mounted across them at an awkward angle. The serials matched the cargo manifest, and the seal patterns matched the manufacturer’s record transmitted before approach.

“Cross-reference the manifest,” I said.

“Already doing so,” the envoy replied.

A tone sounded from the overhead display. The watch commander’s channel opened, carrying the translated voice over a background of clipped alerts. “The forward camera confirms the plates. Rack one is accepted. Continue.”

The display labeled his source as BLOCKADE CONTROL—INNER WATCH. It was the first clear location attached to his voice since the inspection began. I saved the designation with his acknowledgment. Somewhere beyond the corridor’s armored walls, he was watching Khepri’s Fall through the blockade’s command net, not speaking from the freighter or from any station aboard our ship. The distinction mattered. A commander who could see the whole weapons envelope might judge caution differently from a pilot trapped inside a failing camera feed.

“Rack two,” I requested.

The pilot transmitted another image. This one showed oxygen cartridges packed in three layers, the nearest row sealed and the rear rows obscured by frost. The temperature reading sat below the preferred range but above the cargo’s transport limit. I compared it with the escort’s thermal scan. The figures disagreed by a narrow margin.

“Hold acceptance,” the commander said.

I looked at the two readings. “The escort’s sensor is reading the container surface. The freighter’s probe is internal to the rack housing. Ask for the probe calibration record and a third thermal pass.”

The envoy sent both requests. “Commander, the evidence is incomplete, not contradictory.”

“Your captain has a history of treating that distinction as permission to act.”

The guard shifted. I heard the scrape of his boot against the deck.

I turned toward the channel display. “And I have a history of acting before the distinction is recorded. I am not doing that here. No movement has been requested. No movement will occur unless your watch authorizes it.”

Silence pressed against the corridor. Beyond the observation glass, the freighter’s navigation lights remained fixed against the black. The blockade cruisers held their positions around Khepri’s Fall, a ring of restrained force between a mining outpost and the oxygen it needed. If the commander called the discrepancy hostile, the convoy would remain outside. If he ordered a boarding too soon, one frightened gesture could turn inspection into the story the Alliance had inherited about humanity.

The calibration record arrived first. The probe had been certified before departure, but its last reading had been taken against an empty housing. I attached the document to the cargo packet and marked the limitation. The third thermal pass followed, sweeping slowly across the rack. Its reading fell between the other two, close enough to confirm the cartridges remained within transport tolerance.

“Record the variance and its basis,” I said. “Rack two accepted provisionally pending physical confirmation. The cargo stays aboard until the full remote sequence is complete.”

The commander did not immediately answer. Instead, a second overlay appeared on the display: the reliquary chamber’s isolation status, pulled from the outpost’s sealed systems. CHAMBER SEVEN—NO ACCESS. The mnemonic relic remained under paired observation below Khepri’s Fall, its secret untouched while we argued over oxygen seals. I felt an old pressure behind my eyes, a flicker of uncertainty about which memories belonged to me and which might have been placed there. I shut the overlay before it could become an excuse to doubt the readings in front of me.

The envoy noticed the change. “You want the chamber status excluded from the cargo record?”

“I want nothing from Chamber Seven used to interpret this inspection.”

She studied me for a moment, then entered the exclusion note. “Agreed. Evidence must remain attached to the event that produced it.”

Her seal appeared beside mine. The commander’s acknowledgment followed, less promptly but unmistakably. “Remote verification continues. No hostile classification. Send rack three.”

I released the next request. On the overhead display, the freighter’s third rack appeared: cartridges, seals, serials, all waiting to be compared. The convoy had not advanced, the escort had not tightened its weapons envelope, and Khepri’s Fall still had a corridor—narrow, conditional, but real.

I copied the commander’s location and acknowledgment into the inspection file, then leaned toward the console.

“Rack three,” I said. “Begin with the left restraint bar.”
