Story:

The first targeting signal crossed the courier’s hull without a sound.

On Mara Venn’s display, a pale line settled over the engine housing. A second marked the cockpit. Beyond the forward windows, Concordance warships held their positions against the stars, dark hulls angled to watch every route out of Kestrel Deep.

Her fingers tightened around the console.

For an instant she saw another ship through another window. Warning tones rising. Her own voice ordering the weapons officer to fire. She had believed the alien transport was preparing to attack, but she had not completed its identification. The inquiry had left no room for a kinder account: civilians had been aboard, civilians had died, and Captain Venn was responsible.

No hidden order had forced her hand. No missing evidence would make the decision right. She had mistaken fear for certainty and fired before she knew enough.

“Captain,” said Lieutenant Pell at communications. His voice was careful beneath the alert tones. “They’re holding their aim. No change in power readings.”

Mara kept her eyes on the display. The courier carried no weapons. The Concordance ships had announced an interception and established a firing solution; they had not attacked. There was a difference, even if it felt narrow from this side of the targeting lines.

“Transmit the diplomatic identification packet again,” she said. “Full sequence.”

“They already have it.”

“Then they can have it twice.”

Pell sent the packet. Mara placed both hands flat on the console, resisting the urge to reach for a control simply to feel she was doing something. The courier followed its assigned approach vector. The targeting signals did not shift.

Since the inquiry, she had carried its verdict into every command. She had accepted responsibility, as she should have. Still, she privately wanted one decisive success, something clear enough to outweigh her disgrace. She knew a successful mission could not return the civilians to life. That knowledge had never stopped her from hoping.

A new message appeared, authenticated under Admiral Sareth’s authority. The admiral permitted the courier to pass through the blockade and report aboard the flagship for negotiations. The route and escort instructions were exact: no deviation, no approach to any other vessel or station.

Mara read the terms, then acknowledged them without qualification.

“Adjusting to the assigned vector,” Pell said.

“Do it.”

The courier turned by fractions. One Concordance warship shifted to keep pace. The targeting signals stayed steady until the escort cleared the outer edge of the blockade. Then they winked out, one after another.

Mara realized her jaw hurt. She unclenched it and took a slow breath.

Kestrel Deep rotated beneath them, a rough, iron-colored world under the dim light of its distant star. The outpost lay in a highland basin on the nightward side, its pressure domes and refinery towers small against the surrounding land. The approach lanes should have been busy with transports. Now their beacons were gone.

The blockade had stopped ore exports and essential deliveries alike. Mara opened the crisis brief. Medical supplies were below reserve; food allotments had already been reduced; replacement filters were overdue. The report called the settlement’s reserves sufficient for a short interruption, provided nothing else went wrong. It did not say what “short” meant to the people counting their remaining supplies.

Near the top of the brief was Inspector Oren’s name. He had vanished after an interrupted distress transmission. There was a fragment of the message, an abrupt cutoff, and no confirmed location. Under the armistice, Oren was to inspect the outpost’s military installations. Without him, the Concordance could suspect the humans of obstructing the inspection. The outpost had no evidence that could settle the question.

Mara scrolled to the final notice. An enforcement squadron would arrive in thirty-six hours. If Oren remained missing, its orders were to seize the outpost.

Thirty-six hours looked ample in neat type. It was not. The blockade had already made ordinary life precarious; the squadron’s arrival would add a deadline to every shortage and suspicion. If no agreement was reached, the armed force would come with orders it was expected to carry out.

She closed the brief halfway, then reopened it. The tasks were linked: find Oren and negotiate an interim settlement that would delay the seizure while the search continued. She was assigned to do both. A problem with two clear objectives seemed, at first, like something she could master by acting quickly and well.

That was how she wanted to think of it.

A success here could prove she still belonged in command. She could find Oren, secure a settlement, and show that the mistake which had defined her record did not define every choice she made. She understood that the people of Kestrel Deep were not a test set for her redemption, and Oren was not a tool for repairing her reputation. But beneath that understanding, the old hope remained: one decisive act might make the shame easier to bear.

“Captain,” Pell said. “The flagship has assigned our berth. Four minutes to docking.”

Mara secured the crisis brief for the meeting. Through the forward glass, the flagship grew from a dark shape into an immense hull. Its docking bay was open, its lights restrained. The ship had permitted them through the blockade, but the exact route and escort had made the limits of that permission plain.

“Prepare the inspection records and the distress transmission,” she said. “We take both into the room.”

Pell acknowledged. The courier began its final approach.

Mara watched the flagship fill the window. She would recover Oren and negotiate a settlement; she would keep the outpost from being seized. Those were the immediate aims. She did not yet see how little authority she had over what happened next, or how many other people would have to trust one another for either aim to succeed.

For now, she believed that if she chose decisively enough, she could make the outcome right.

The flagship’s docking bay received the courier without ceremony. White guide lights ran beneath its landing struts; armored doors closed behind Mara with a sound she felt through her boots. Two Concordance officers waited beside the transfer lift, unarmed but watchful.

She followed them through broad passageways built for a crew accustomed to moving quickly. Narrow windows showed the blockade holding position around Kestrel Deep, warships dark against the stars. Ore exports had stopped, along with essential deliveries. In thirty-six hours, an enforcement squadron would arrive with orders to seize the outpost if Inspector Oren remained missing.

Mara had rehearsed her opening during the flight: lift the blockade, allow the settlement to function, and the search would proceed without risking an incident. The argument was practical. It was also unlikely to persuade anyone who believed the blockade itself was necessary.

Admiral Sareth stood at the far end of the conference room. Tavi En, the Concordance interpreter and historian assigned to accompany Mara, sat beside her with a translation slate. Both rose as Mara entered.

“Captain Venn,” Sareth said. “You may address the blockade and Inspector Oren. You may not conduct an unrestricted inspection of my fleet.”

“I understand the limits of my passage.”

“Then we can begin with the limits of your assignment.” Sareth indicated the chair opposite her. “I require Oren’s verifiable recovery and your compliance with the armistice’s inspection provisions. Kestrel Deep must make its facilities, manifests, and relevant personnel available.”

Mara sat. “The outpost can comply more effectively if the blockade is lifted immediately. Shortages are already affecting essential operations. Keeping warships on station only increases the chance of an incident.”

“The blockade remains,” Sareth said. “The question is whether your side will honor the armistice.”

Mara had expected resistance. She leaned forward, trying for a measured tone. “The fear of human resistance is an irrational inheritance from the Ash Fleet. The present settlement is not the Ash Fleet. Our ships are not preparing to attack you.”

A pause followed. Sareth’s expression remained composed, but her attention seemed to draw inward.

“Human warships destroyed inhabited human colonies during the Ash Fleet campaigns,” she said. “Not empty stations or military depots. Colonies, whose residents had no part in the conflict.”

Mara knew the history. She had meant to distinguish the present from it, not deny it. But calling the fear irrational had made the distinction sound like contempt for those who remembered.

Sareth continued. “My family lost people in a later human attack. They were not officers or combatants. I will not be told that caution is superstition because you find it inconvenient.”

Mara’s hand had tightened around the edge of the brief. She let go. “I’m sorry. I dismissed real losses. I shouldn’t have.”

The apology came out plain and inadequate. She resisted the urge to explain what she had intended; intention would not change what she had said.

Tavi glanced between them. “The record is not in dispute,” they said. “But the claim that every human would choose annihilation over compromise is not a historical conclusion. It is a political story built around the record.”

Sareth looked at them. “I did not say every human.”

“No. But some will draw that conclusion from what has been said.”

Mara stayed silent. Tavi had separated documented crimes from the sweeping claim used to make suspicion permanent. It was the distinction she had wanted to make, but her first words had damaged the chance of being heard. Tavi’s correction could not undo that.

“My request stands,” Mara said. “The outpost needs room to function. I need access to the distress transmission and to the place where Oren was last known to be.”

“You will have access to the original transmission,” Sareth said. “You may conduct a bounded search at the outpost, accompanied by a Concordance observer. You will send regular communications on your progress. Any evidence of treaty violations you find will be reported alongside evidence concerning Oren.”

Mara considered the condition. A discrepancy in the outpost’s records could become as important as the search itself. “You expect us to report anything, even if it complicates the settlement?”

“I expect you to report what you find. Oren’s recovery cannot conceal a breach. Nor will the search be used to manufacture one.”

Tavi closed the slate. “I will accompany Captain Venn and interpret the transmission. I can provide the relevant treaty and historical context as needed.”

Mara had asked for the blockade to be lifted and for unrestricted access. She had received neither. But the authorized search, regular communications, and original transmission were enough to begin. She could accept the limits or spend the remaining hours arguing for authority she was not going to get.

“I accept,” she said. “We’ll bring you what we find.”

Sareth held her gaze. “Bring us what is there, Captain. Not what would make your case easier.”

Mara stood. “Understood.”

The corridor seemed quieter on the way back. Tavi walked beside her without speaking until they reached the lift.

“You could have made that argument without calling her fear irrational,” they said.

“Yes.”

“It was not an obscure distinction.”

“No,” Mara said. “It wasn’t.”

Tavi studied her briefly, then faced the doors. Mara could not tell whether they were disappointed or simply taking note. She had no reason to expect a useful correction to become forgiveness.

At the docking bay, the courier waited beneath the Concordance officers’ watch. Mara carried the mission terms and Oren’s brief. Beyond the hull, the blockade remained in place; at Kestrel Deep, Oren was still missing, and the enforcement squadron was coming.

She had wanted more authority than Sareth would trust her with. Now she would have to work within the limits she had accepted, and make the findings public even if they complicated the settlement. Her own record gave Sareth additional reason for caution. There was no argument she could make that would erase that.

Tavi boarded after her. The hatch sealed, and the clamps released. Mara glanced once toward the flagship’s lit windows. She had not persuaded Sareth to trust her. At least, she had stopped trying to earn trust by denying what had made it difficult. It was not a breakthrough, only a beginning, and the difference mattered.

Kestrel Deep’s landing lights flickered beneath the crater rim. Beyond them, patched ventilation towers stood against the moon’s black horizon. One released a pale plume, then fell still.

Inside the landing chamber, the air tasted of mineral dust and overheated insulation.

Director Ilan Rusk waited beside the inner hatch. His utility coat was worn at the cuffs; his white hair had been cropped without much attention to symmetry. Workers passing through the chamber greeted him by name.

“Captain Vale. Analyst Tavi.” He glanced at the authorization on Mara’s wrist display. “Relevant records, interviews, and locations. Not unrestricted access.”

“And regular communications with our courier,” Mara said.

“Of course. Safety restrictions still apply.”

“They will need to be explained.”

Rusk studied her, then opened the hatch. “I would rather show you.”

He began at the infirmary.

Behind the dispensary glass, broad stretches of shelving stood empty. Someone had moved the remaining packets forward to make them easier to count. A nurse checked a requisition against three sealed ampoules, crossed something out, and started again.

“The shortages began before the blockade,” Rusk said. “Reduced production, reduced credit. Suppliers demanded guarantees our administration would not provide.”

Mara examined the delivery dates. The first missed shipment was nine weeks old.

“But replacement supplies are now waiting outside the exclusion zone,” she said.

“Antibiotics. Filter membranes. Surgical cartridges.” The nurse looked up. “Waiting does not keep them useful to us.”

“No,” Mara said. “It doesn’t.”

She recorded both facts: the longstanding shortage and the blockade preventing relief. Neither canceled the other.

In the air-processing bay, a vibration traveled through the soles of her boots. Two scrubbers labored beneath yellow warning lights; a third lay open, its casing surrounded by carefully sorted parts.

“Replacement membrane failed six weeks ago,” the technician told them. “Director got us a temporary assembly from an abandoned survey rig.”

“Temporary for how long?” Tavi asked.

“It was rated for ten days.”

Rusk looked at the running machinery. “We have had it for twenty-three.”

The technician touched his sleeve. “We’d have lost the lower dormitories without it.”

Rusk merely asked whether she had slept.

Mara watched the exchange. His authority here was not only a title. People trusted him because they remembered what he had done when help failed to arrive.

Outside the bay, a young worker stopped them.

“You’re her,” she said to Mara. “The captain who killed those civilians.”

Conversation thinned around them.

“Yes.”

“And now you work for the Concordance?”

“I am searching for their missing inspector under terms both sides accepted.”

“Whose side are you on?”

Mara felt the familiar urge to explain herself before anyone could finish judging her. She let it pass.

“I intend to find Oren and report what the evidence supports. Neither government deserves unquestioning trust.”

The worker’s expression did not soften. “That sounds convenient.”

“It would be more convenient to promise you an answer I haven’t earned.”

Rusk guided them onward without rebuking the woman. Mara was grateful for that, then wary of her gratitude.

In his office, he brought up the incident summary.

“Oren was warned away from unstable workings. He continued without an escort. Our last confirmed location places him near the western pump galleries.”

“What did your search find?”

“Nothing conclusive. We cannot send exhausted crews into unsupported shafts indefinitely.”

He stood beside a window overlooking the enclosed loading yard. Freight sledges sat idle beneath frost-whitened lamps.

“The Concordance sees human independence as a problem to be managed,” he said. “Anything we possess that might reduce our dependence becomes something they must inspect, regulate, or confiscate.”

“An inspector disappearing gives them a legitimate concern.”

“And warships give them the power to define every concern in their favor.”

Mara looked at the motionless sledges. She could not dismiss him. She had seen stronger powers turn practical necessities into permanent obligations. Resentment did not make his account false.

But neither did hardship make it true.

Tavi, seated at a records terminal, lifted their head.

“Where is maintenance relay M-17?”

“Old east workings,” Rusk said. “Unused gallery.”

“Your summary puts Oren west.”

“That is our last confirmed location.”

“The original distress burst carries M-17’s forwarding identifier.”

Rusk came closer. “The transmission was damaged.”

“Yes,” Tavi said. “That field survived.”

Mara joined them. They opened the externally received burst Sareth had supplied, keeping it separate from the station’s incident summary. Beneath the broken signal was a short relay header.

“Could it have been forwarded across the station?” Mara asked.

“Possibly. We need the routing records.”

Rusk authorized access after a pause.

For the next hour they compared the burst with ordinary maintenance traffic, not either government’s conclusions. M-17 served a local branch near the sealed gallery. Its recent test messages followed the same route as Oren’s distress signal.

“That narrows the origin,” Tavi said. “It doesn’t put him inside the gallery.”

“But it does require explaining,” Mara said.

They checked work orders for a reason anyone might have been there. A recent power request led to a transport entry; the transport entry referenced an infirmary requisition.

A sedative had been issued shortly after the distress burst.

Mara asked for the corresponding treatment record. There was no patient attached to the requisition. Its destination was isolation room four, listed as unoccupied.

“An emergency stock transfer,” Rusk said.

“Then why is the quantity recorded as administered?” Tavi asked.

The director’s jaw tightened. “Ask the medical officer.”

“We will,” Mara said. “At the room.”

“Isolation areas have additional restrictions.”

Until then, she had wanted him to have an explanation. She still did. That desire was precisely why she could not leave the entry unexamined.

She displayed the agreed search authority.

“A missing person, an unexplained sedative administration, and a room recorded as empty. This falls within our mandate.”

“Captain, suspicion is not proof.”

“No,” Mara said. “It is a reason to look.”

She turned toward the infirmary corridor, and after a moment Rusk followed.

The isolation corridor was colder than the rest of the infirmary. Condensation silvered an exposed pipe; behind the wall, a ventilation fan scraped once each revolution. Two workers waiting for treatment rose when they saw Rusk. One thanked him for finding her a lighter shift. He remembered her name.

At room four, Mara showed the duty medic their search authority.

“The census says empty,” the woman said.

“Then opening it should be simple.”

The medic hesitated, glanced at Rusk, and released the lock.

Inside, a man lay beneath a thermal sheet, an infusion pump glowing beside his head. His face matched Oren’s credential portrait. The bedside chart identified him as Casualty Nine: an unidentified worker recovered after an industrial accident.

There were dark bands around both wrists.

Tavi stopped just inside the doorway. Mara heard a breath catch, then the observer was photographing the chart and pump display.

“Don’t disconnect anything,” the medic said, moving past them. She checked Oren’s breathing and studied the infusion history. “He’s been kept under.”

“Can you wake him safely?”

“Not all at once.”

Mara sent the courier a preliminary recovery notice for immediate relay: a patient matching Oren had been located alive; direct authentication would follow. Then she asked Tavi to preserve the local chart, drug-administration history, and the order linking the patient to this room.

The medic reduced the infusion and attached fresh monitors.

“He came to me as an accident casualty,” she said. “I was told to maintain isolation.”

“By whom?”

“The instruction came through the emergency queue. I’ll retrieve it. Right now, let me work.”

Nearly two hours passed before Oren could sustain a conversation.

He gave his name, his inspection number, and the current date. His voice was rough, but his questions were precise: where were his belongings, who had accessed his inspection account, and had the distress transmission reached the squadron?

“It reached them,” Mara said.

His fingers loosened their grip on the sheet.

“Then someone knew.”

He remembered checking a distribution diagram at a pump junction. His next distinct recollection was this ceiling and medication entering the port beside his hand.

“Between those points?” Mara asked.

“Nothing.”

“Confused impressions?”

“No. The later waking and sleeping are confused. The interval before the injection I remember is simply absent.”

“Was Rusk there?”

Oren looked toward the director, standing beyond the open door.

“I don’t know.”

“The marks on your wrists—”

“Show that something restrained me. They don’t tell me who.” He swallowed painfully. “Please don’t put a suspect inside the part I cannot remember.”

Heat rose beneath Mara’s collar. She had let her question reach ahead of her evidence.

“You’re right.”

The medic’s examination documented three distinct findings: injuries consistent with restraint, substantial sedative exposure, and an abnormal pattern of neurological stress.

“That last finding is unusual,” she said, turning the scanner so Oren could see. “It isn’t what I’d expect from this drug alone. But I can’t identify the mechanism here.”

“Does it explain the gap?” Mara asked.

“It might be related. That isn’t the same as an explanation.”

Mara recorded the findings separately. None established who had confined him. None, alone, established how his memories had been lost.

Oren requested access to his inspection account. Once the medic was satisfied he could proceed, he recovered a routine checklist saved before the distress burst.

One unfinished item concerned an auxiliary power allocation. The amount was modest against the station’s total consumption, but it continued during periods when the equipment supposedly drawing it was idle. The feeder ran toward the sealed mining gallery.

“Could it be faulty metering?” Tavi asked.

“Yes. Or undocumented machinery. I hadn’t determined which.” Oren examined his own notation. “I was following the allocation, not investigating a weapon.”

It supported the question raised by the relay header. It did not tell them what had happened at the gallery.

When the medic certified Oren stable for transfer, Mara offered him the courier’s medical berth.

“There will be fewer interruptions,” she said. “And your treatment records won’t remain solely on the system we’re investigating.”

“I’ll go.”

Rusk objected until the medic explained the transfer precautions. Then he stepped aside. When a trolley wheel caught on the threshold, he bent to free it.

Mara watched him steady the frame. The same practical attention had kept exhausted workers loyal to him. She wished it could answer the questions waiting in the records.

The courier smelled of clean filters and warmed insulation. Once Oren was settled, Mara sent the confirmed recovery report and opened the secure channel to Sareth.

Oren answered a fresh authentication challenge himself.

For a moment the admiral said nothing.

“Inspector. Do you request evacuation?”

“I request continued medical assessment here. I can assist with the records from this berth, if the clinician permits it.”

“You are not required to remain.”

“I understand. It is my choice.”

Sareth inclined her head.

“Your recovery is acknowledged. The squadron’s automatic authority to seize the outpost on missing-inspector grounds is suspended.”

Mara glanced at the approach clock.

“The arrival schedule?”

“Unchanged. The blockade remains pending investigation of the inspector’s confinement and completion of the interrupted inspection. Any armed intervention must now rest on actual obstruction or threats. It cannot rest on a claim that he remains missing.”

“Put that distinction in the operational notice,” Mara said.

“It will be explicit.”

With Oren’s assent, Mara released a brief authenticated recovery bulletin to the station and squadron channels. It contained no unrelated medical information.

Then, while he rested, she returned to the isolation paperwork.

The bedside copy named only the duty service that had administered the sedative. The emergency queue showed who had received the instruction, but not who had originated it. Tavi requested the signed routing envelope retained by the station’s message archive.

Outside the berth, the courier’s fans ran smoothly. Mara had stopped noticing such sounds years ago; now the absence of Kestrel Deep’s grinding ventilation felt almost indecent.

The archived envelope arrived near the end of the fourth hour.

Tavi checked its seal against the routine administrative messages they had already preserved.

“This is the originating order,” the observer said. “Not the ward’s transcription.”

Mara read the instruction twice: isolate the casualty, restrict access, maintain sedation pending further direction.

It did not establish who had held Oren down, or what had happened in the missing interval.

But the order had originated under Director Ilan Rusk’s authority.

Mara laid the originating order beside the ward’s transcription. The sheets looked almost identical, but one carried Rusk’s authority seal; the other showed how the instruction had reached the medical staff. Neither explained what had happened during Oren’s missing hours. Together, they established who had ordered him isolated and sedated.

Beyond the berth, the courier’s fans ran with an even hush. Oren rested nearby, his face pale under the medical lights. Mara could feel the hours pressing into her shoulders, but Rusk’s voice on the station channel was alert.

“You can still make this useful,” he said. “The fragment is the only thing Sareth might believe. Let us transplant it into Oren, then arrange contact. She’ll see what happened.”

“What happened, or what you want her to believe happened?” Mara asked.

“If it stops a strike, the distinction is academic.”

“Not to Oren.” She glanced at the records. “The procedure could hurt him or damage what he does remember. And a borrowed fragment isn’t a complete account just because it feels vivid.”

Rusk’s reply came after a brief silence. “You think accuracy will protect anyone?”

“No. But fear doesn’t make falsifying the source acceptable.”

The relic had offered Mara a fragment, not a voice she could send across the void. It could not influence Sareth remotely. Any contact would have to be arranged between them, and Rusk’s proposal depended on coercing Oren into a dangerous procedure. Mara would do neither.

She opened the courier’s authenticated channel. Its amber status light marked the beginning of transmission. The courier could verify that the files were intact and show where they had come from; it could not decide what they proved. Mara assembled the originating order, the ward transcription, and the archived routing envelope, including their seals and custody records.

She added her own account of the borrowed fragment, clearly separating sensation from evidence. It had been brief, its point of view uncertain, and its sequence incomplete. She could not place every detail in time or claim that her fear had not shaped her interpretation. The records supported parts of what she understood, but not all of it.

She read the bundle once more, resisting the urge to remove qualifications that weakened its force. Then she released it to the station and squadron channels.

Oren opened his eyes when she asked if he wanted to speak.

“I can confirm who I am, my appointment here, and the records signed under my authorization,” he said. His voice was rough from the dry air. “I cannot remember the missing interval. I don’t know who restrained me or what they said. That order isn’t a memory.”

“Do you want to add anything?”

“Not yet.”

Mara transmitted his statement without embellishment. His identity and the limits of his knowledge would travel together.

Tavi waited at the berth entrance. “I’ll translate all of it,” they said. “Including what weakens your case.”

“Especially that.”

Mara opened another file: the record of her earlier engagement. The official language was precise and bloodless. Identification incomplete. Threat assessed. Civilians killed. She had read those lines enough times to know how quickly the mind searched them for an excuse.

“I killed civilians,” she said into the channel. “I was afraid, and the identification was incomplete. I acted anyway. What I do here won’t cancel that. I’ve been tempted to call a successful outcome payment for what I did. It isn’t.”

The workers watched through the assembly bay camera. Some faced the screen; others watched one another, waiting to see what the admission would require of them.

“I’m not innocent,” Mara continued. “I’m not asking you to treat me as a moral authority. Judge the records and what can be verified. Protect the people here. Don’t follow a legend just because it gives you a simple answer.”

Tavi translated without softening her words or turning the appeal into a command. The civilians she had killed remained in the statement. So did the evidence against Rusk. When Tavi finished, the bay was silent.

Then two guards stepped back from the access rail. A third stayed by the weapons locker, watching the screen. Rusk’s security was beginning to divide, but the mining distribution system was still armed. Its firing bank could be recharged from industrial power, while ventilation and medical equipment depended on circuits running through the same section.

“Security, stop her,” Rusk ordered.

One guard touched his headset but did not move. Another looked toward the workers’ camera.

“You disarm that system,” Rusk said, “and you invite occupation.”

Mara looked at the distribution diagram. The safe sequence depended on separating essential services first, then isolating the firing supply. A mistake could leave the station without air or medical power. No one could promise that Sareth would show restraint if Mara succeeded.

She pressed her hands to the console until their trembling eased. “I’m going to disable the firing supply and preserve life support,” she said. “The records are yours to judge. This is what I can do now.”

The courier acknowledged her statement and began transmitting it with the assembled evidence. Its light held steady as Mara turned toward the service route.

The service route began behind the ore-sorting drums, where the inspection schematics showed a passage beneath the distribution floor. Mara found the hatch bolted from within and worked its release with a wrench. When it opened, hot metal air rose from the passage, carrying the bitter smell of dust baked into old wiring.

She checked the workers waiting in the assembly bay. “No one past the yellow line. The switching area is hazardous. Clear it completely.”

Tavi translated. The workers moved away in uneasy groups, glancing at the cameras and at one another. Mara waited until the last had crossed the marked boundary. She would not keep them near the machinery to strengthen her position. Tavi confirmed the bay was clear, then relayed Mara’s entry to the station and to Sareth.

The courier’s lens blinked as it recorded the service route. Through its authenticated channel, the original records and their provenance were already moving outward, along with Mara’s qualified account of the borrowed fragment and the uncertainties in each source. Nothing in the relic could reach Sareth remotely. The courier could show what Mara did; it could not make the admiral believe her.

“Oren, I’m at the distribution access,” Mara said. “I have the schematic.”

“Read me the panel designation.”

She did. His voice came through the communications link, thin with interference but steady. He checked the designation against the maintenance procedure and had her confirm each circuit before she opened the cabinet. Its labels were faded except for newer red markings: INDUSTRIAL FEED, EMERGENCY BUS, FIRE BANK.

“Transfer essential services first,” Oren said. “Do not open the main feed. Confirm ventilation on the independent supply before isolating the firing circuit.”

The firing-bank indicator climbed as Rusk’s controller tested the charge. Mara fitted insulated gloves from the cabinet and checked the seals. Her right glove was nicked, so she replaced it. She kept her breathing slow until the tools stopped rattling in her hands.

Rusk’s voice came over the station speakers. “Security, stop her. Disarming that system invites occupation.”

Two guards moved toward the access rail. One stopped when a worker turned from the camera to face him. Another glanced toward the weapons locker but did not reach for it. They had heard Mara admit that fear and an incomplete identification had led her to kill civilians. They had heard her refuse to make that confession payment for the evidence against Rusk. Neither side had been given an innocent story to hide inside.

Rusk repeated his warning. “The defenses are the only thing keeping the Concordance from taking this station.”

Mara looked at the circuit diagram. “The station can judge the records. I’m going to disable the firing supply and preserve life support.”

She shifted the essential loads onto the emergency supply. The transfer switch resisted before seating with a hard clack. Three indicators changed: amber, then green, green. The medical circuit held. She watched the airflow gauge while Oren compared the readings with the procedure.

“Stable,” he said. “Keep watching it. If it falls below the threshold, stop.”

Tavi relayed the readings to Sareth without adding a conclusion. The fans continued their deep, uneven pulse through the walls. Mara moved to the local disconnect, where the maintenance schematic showed a mechanical override beside the actuator.

The actuator jammed halfway through its travel. A warning light flared, and the assembly clicked twice without moving. Heat pressed through the cabinet door. Mara smelled insulation beginning to cook.

“Back away,” Oren said. “It’s under load.”

“If I leave it, the bank stays connected.”

“Wait for the controller to discharge it.”

“It’s recharging.”

Mara’s voice had sharpened. She stopped, looked again at the indicators, and listened to the fans. Oren was not arguing against her; he was naming the risk. The mechanical override required her to reach beside the overheated assembly, but the firing circuit could not be left to software.

“Give me the local isolation sequence,” she said.

Oren recited it. Mara repeated each step before touching a control. The courier transmitted her words and the changing readings so Sareth could verify them against the station telemetry.

At the passage entrance, the guards had drawn closer. One raised a hand. “Rusk says this is your last chance.”

Mara met his eyes. “Stand clear. The bank is still live.”

The guard looked past her at the camera, then stepped aside. The others did not block the route. Rusk’s command remained audible, but fewer people seemed willing to carry it out.

Mara took the insulated hook and reached for the lever. The actuator housing snapped with a sharp report. Blue-white light filled the cabinet. She turned, but the flash caught her left forearm through the gap in her glove. Pain struck hard enough to steal her breath. Burned fabric smelled sharp and chemical.

“Mara?” Oren called.

“I’m here.” She could still hear the fans, and the airflow gauge held steady. “Continue.”

She pulled the lever through its last inch. The firing-bank indicator died. Then she locked the disconnect open with the physical bar and attached the grounding clamp across the stored-charge terminals. The charge meter descended in measured steps to zero.

Oren checked the readings against the procedure. Tavi relayed the controller state, ground reading, and life-support figures. The courier sent them all to Sareth. The mechanical lock held open; no software command could reconnect the firing supply.

Mara pressed her unburned hand against her forearm. The sleeve had split, exposing reddened skin. She kept her eyes on the dark firing indicator while the pain settled into a steady throb.

Sareth’s voice came through the courier. “I acknowledge Oren’s verified recovery and the authenticated records. Independent station readings confirm the firing system is isolated and life support remains stable. The crimes documented there remain under inspection. This action does not erase them.”

A brief pause followed.

“Effective immediately, I order a temporary ceasefire. Preparations for a strike are halted. Inspection protocols remain in force. No forced entry while station cooperation continues.”

The order ended. The warning tones fell silent one by one. Mara had no way to know how long the ceasefire would hold, or what Sareth would do if cooperation faltered. She had heard no promise of trust, only an order limited by evidence and conditions.

She listened to the ventilation fans and kept one hand on the physical lock until Oren confirmed that the charge was fully grounded.

At hour thirty-five, the charge meter held at zero.

Mara waited for Oren to read it aloud a second time before she released the locking bar. Her palm left a damp print on the metal. Above them, the ventilation duct shivered with the ordinary labor of keeping people alive.

Tavi’s voice came over the maintenance speaker. “The control-room crew have withdrawn Rusk’s command access. Two of them are coming down.”

“Keep the service controls staffed,” Mara said.

“They are.”

Footsteps sounded beyond the open door. Rusk entered between a station security officer and a woman wearing a coolant technician’s patched jacket. He was walking without assistance, but the officer carried his authorization key in an evidence sleeve.

“The crews want this finished,” Rusk said. His gaze moved from the grounding clamp to Mara’s burned arm. “I can finish it.”

The technician stopped beside the disconnect and inspected the bar. Only after she had checked the meter herself did she turn toward him.

“You told us the firing banks were protecting the station.”

“They were.”

“You put our air supply on the same wager.”

Rusk looked toward the security officer. The officer activated her recorder.

“Director Rusk, you are being held pending review of the recovered records and Inspector Oren’s statement. Time of custody: thirty-five hours, three minutes station incident time. You may make a statement. It will be preserved with this record.”

Mara watched the officer’s hands. They shook slightly as she fitted the restraint, checked its clearance, and asked the technician to witness the custody entry.

“You’re letting them arrest me?” Rusk asked.

“They’re documenting your custody.”

“And when the Concordance asks for someone to shoot?”

“No summary punishment,” Mara said.

Rusk’s mouth tightened. “Then give me immunity. I still know the station’s systems better than anyone here.”

For a moment she imagined pressing her injured arm against the cold doorframe until the pain became something simple. She had no strength left for his bargaining.

“You can provide information,” she said. “You can correct the record. Neither buys immunity.”

Oren stood beside the instrument panel, wrapped in a borrowed work coat. “And she cannot grant it.”

That steadied her more than agreement would have. She nodded.

“Record that too.”

The authenticated channel opened through the courier with three clear tones. The small display showed the outpost connection, the courier’s relay, and Sareth’s flagship acknowledgment. Beneath them ran the same time stamp.

“Custody record received,” Sareth said. “Submit the grounds.”

The officer read from the preliminary entry: Oren’s detention and obstruction of inspection, supported by the recovered access records and Oren’s account; the connection and attempted employment of the industrial firing systems, supported by command logs, controller readings, and the witnessed isolation.

“Allegations pending examination,” Oren added. “My testimony concerns what I experienced and what I personally observed. The system records require technical authentication.”

Mara saw Rusk lift his head at that qualification. She disliked the relief in his face, then disliked herself for wanting the qualification withheld.

Sareth acknowledged the distinction.

“The enforcement squadron continues its approach,” he said. “Your recovery invalidates the missing-inspector basis for forced seizure. It does not discharge the inspection demand.”

“Then put the inspection inside an agreement,” Mara said. “With relief deliveries first.”

“Define relief.”

“Medical stock held at the blockade. Dressings, analgesics, respiratory cartridges, the refrigerated treatments on the courier’s manifest. Release them for immediate transfer.”

“Escorted transfer,” Sareth said.

“Yes.”

The word cost her. She had demanded an open route before; now she could hear how readily an escort might become another barrier, another delay while somebody above them requested authorization.

“And essential traffic resumes,” she said.

“Conditional upon inspection and continued weapons isolation.”

“Life-support cargo, medical evacuation, and service personnel. Each movement logged. Decisions given within a stated interval, with reasons recorded for refusal.”

The courier’s operator read the categories back. Tavi supplied the station’s remaining service margins. There was enough reserve to negotiate, but not enough to pretend delay was harmless.

Sareth accepted priority clearance for the listed traffic after verification of each manifest.

Mara looked at the narrowed terms appearing on the screen. Somewhere beyond the station walls, freighters would still be waiting with their drives cold.

“This does not lift the blockade,” she said.

“Correct.”

“Put that limitation in the agreement. Along with the fact that acceptance here does not settle our objection to it.”

“Accepted.”

At thirty-five hours, fourteen minutes, they turned to the inspection team. Station technicians would accompany Concordance inspectors. Access would cover the firing installations, relevant control records, Oren’s confinement site, and the gallery. Routes and personnel would be logged; any expansion would require recorded reasons and joint acknowledgment.

“The relic stays where it is,” Oren said.

“The gallery cannot be exempt,” Sareth replied.

“It isn’t,” Mara said. “But inspection cannot become use. Contact-blocking containment, reciprocal seals, and no human or Concordance operation pending independent medical and technical review.”

A pause followed. Mara could hear Rusk breathing through his nose.

“Submit the containment specifications,” Sareth said. “Provisional assent depends on verification.”

“And the evidence?” Oren asked.

Parallel custody records, they agreed: original identifiers, witnessed transfers, matching authenticated copies. Any examination that might alter an original required notice and a preserved record of its condition beforehand.

Rusk shifted against the restraint. “Whose court?”

No one answered immediately.

“A review hearing in six hours,” Sareth said at last. “Joint witnesses. Counsel or an appointed adviser available to the detainee. Final jurisdiction reserved for lawful agreement between the competent authorities.”

Mara asked the courier to read it back. Only then did she assent.

At thirty-five hours, twenty minutes, the draft remained marked PROVISIONAL. The gallery safeguards and evidence inventories still had to be completed before the squadron arrived.

A medic waited beside the door with an open case.

Mara glanced at the relief-transfer field. No departure confirmation yet.

“Check the service readings again,” she told Tavi.

Then she moved aside so the technician could inspect the grounding clamp without having to reach past her wounded arm.

The grounding clamp seated with a dull click. Mara felt the vibration through the deck before she heard it, a small, ordinary sound beneath the ventilation fans. For most of the last hour, every mechanical noise had seemed to announce another failure. She waited until the technician checked the meter.

“Isolation holds,” he said.

“Put the reading in the joint log.”

Her burned forearm had begun to throb beneath its stiffened sleeve. The medic remained beside the door, watching her with the patient hostility of someone whose sensible advice had been repeatedly ignored.

On the wall display, the courier’s narrow communications window sat between the outpost schematic and Sareth’s image aboard the flagship.

“The gallery specifications,” Sareth said.

Tavi brought them up. A transparent maintenance enclosure would surround the relic’s plinth, its walls standing clear of every exposed surface. No access ports would remain open. The enclosure’s fasteners would carry separately numbered station and Concordance seals, each visible to the other side’s cameras.

Oren leaned toward the display.

“The underside,” he said. “There’s a maintenance opening beneath the plinth.”

Tavi stopped scrolling.

“It isn’t on this drawing.”

“It was there when I examined the mounting. I don’t know whether it’s still accessible.”

Mara watched Sareth’s expression sharpen.

“Then we check,” she said.

A station technician entered the gallery while the courier relayed his camera feed. The light beneath the plinth showed a recessed hatch, half concealed by a cable tray. Nobody spoke while he fitted a rigid cover over it without touching the relic or its mounting. He read out the cover’s identifier, then stepped back so the entire assembly remained visible.

Sareth requested a second angle. They provided it.

“Accepted for interim containment,” she said. “No operation, stimulation, sampling, or direct contact by either party.”

“Pending independent medical and technical review,” Mara added.

“Recorded.”

Oren closed his eyes briefly. Relief made him look older.

At thirty-five twenty-seven, the courier opened the evidence ledger.

Oren authenticated himself using his inspector credential and a challenge sent through the flagship’s personnel registry. His first response was too quiet for the recorder. Asked to repeat it, he looked toward the station security officer standing beside Rusk.

“Please keep him where I can see him.”

The officer moved Rusk’s chair into view. Rusk’s restraint scraped its metal arm.

Oren repeated the response. The registry accepted it.

His statement came slowly. He described where he had been confined, what he had heard, and which recovered records he recognized. When the courier’s clerk asked whether Rusk had personally authorized every alteration in those records, Oren hesitated.

“I can identify his voice in the exchange I witnessed,” he said. “I cannot identify who made entries while I was confined. I have not examined all the recovered material.”

Mara felt an unreasonable urge to help him, to supply the certainty everyone wanted. She pressed her uninjured hand against the console and stayed silent.

The clerk read the limitation back.

Original recordings and recovered storage units received identifiers in both custody ledgers. Their present holders, seal numbers, and condition were entered separately, then compared across the channel. Authenticated copies of Oren’s statement went to both sides; acknowledgment from one did not substitute for acknowledgment from the other.

Rusk watched the entries accumulate.

“You’re making this look settled.”

“No,” Mara said. “The charges entered against you go to the review hearing. So do your statement and any objections you make.”

“And afterward?”

“Jurisdiction remains reserved. I can’t decide it here.”

He looked away before she finished.

At thirty-five thirty-two, Sareth turned from their channel toward someone beyond the flagship camera. Another voice requested authentication. A squadron designation appeared beneath her image.

Mara’s stomach tightened. Until that moment, the approaching ships had been symbols on a plot. Now she could hear a bridge officer waiting for orders.

Sareth transmitted the agreement with its conditions attached.

“Inspector Oren is alive, identified, and available under the recorded arrangements. The missing-inspector seizure order is no longer applicable. Your task is to supervise the agreed inspection and medical relief operation.”

The acknowledgment came after a short delay.

“Revised instructions received. Inspection and relief supervision. Escorted access under joint logging.”

Mara let out a breath.

The first medical shuttle left the blockade holding position at thirty-five thirty-four. Its manifest filled a corner of the display: dressings, antibiotics, sterile fluids, replacement filters for the infirmary’s failing sterilizer. Names and quantities. Things she could understand.

Station security cleared the receiving passage. A Concordance escort officer and a station medic checked each cargo seal together over video. Beyond them, on the dock camera, people waited behind a painted line, several still wearing coolant masks around their necks.

Tavi touched Mara’s shoulder.

“Water pressure steady. Atmosphere circulation steady. Infirmary power on both feeds.”

“The firing systems?”

He showed her the joint readings. Physical disconnects remained open; control power was isolated. A Concordance technician requested a local meter check. The station technician performed it, holding the probes where both cameras could see.

No current.

At thirty-five thirty-eight, the shuttle’s inner hatch opened. The first medical cases crossed into station custody, their transfer countersigned by the escort officer. A nurse took the filters straight toward the infirmary.

Only then did Mara turn to the medic.

“All right.”

He cut away her sleeve. Cool air touched the burn, followed by the sharper sting of cleaning fluid. She gripped the edge of the chair and watched the display while he worked.

The agreement still carried an expiry time and conditions for suspension. Rusk’s hearing remained six hours from its appointment. In the gallery, two sets of seals held around something neither side yet understood.

At thirty-five forty, the dock reported the first delivery complete.

Mara lowered her eyes to the dressing being secured around her arm. Outside the room, a trolley rattled toward the infirmary, its wheels catching at every seam in the deck.

At thirty-five forty, the courier’s communications system chimed. The sound carried down the infirmary passage, where the first medical cases were rolling toward the ward.

“Enforcement squadron to Kestrel Deep,” said a voice from the speaker. “We are on final approach. Maintain the agreed access corridor. Confirm receipt.”

The reply went out under both signatures. On the dock monitor, nurses and station workers moved the cases past the painted line. Each seal was checked against the manifest before the trolley continued. Dressings, antibiotics, sterile fluids, replacement filters: ordinary things, urgently needed.

Mara sat with her bandaged arm held away from her side. The burn pulsed beneath the dressing. Tavi stood beside her, reviewing the joint inspection sequence.

“Firing installations first,” he said. “Then control records, Oren’s confinement site, and the gallery.”

“Both teams?”

“Station and Concordance. Every section opened by station security. Every record logged by both.”

The inspectors entered the service ring carrying their equipment in plain view. A station technician led them to the firing controls. The physical disconnects remained open. At the Concordance officer’s request, the technician tested the terminals again while both teams watched.

No current.

The reading went into two records. Mara stayed behind the security boundary. A clean measurement could not settle what had been done with the weapons, but it could establish that they were disabled now.

In the control room, the inspectors asked who had issued each command, which access key had authorized it, and what information was missing from the logs. A station operator answered while Tavi stood beside him. The Concordance recorder waited for the operator to finish before asking for clarification. No one was asked to rely on the other side’s memory.

At Oren’s room, the assigned medic asked whether he would agree to another exposure to the relic in the sealed gallery.

“No,” Oren said. He sat upright on the bed, hands still against the blanket. “The neurological risk is enough. I won’t do it again.”

“You can leave your earlier statement as it is,” the medic said. “You may correct it or add to it without another exposure.”

“I want the limit recorded, too.”

The inspector turned to him. “Your statement will say what you remember and what you don’t.”

Oren had given his name, inspection number, and the current date. He had described the ceiling above him and the medication brought to the port beside his hand. He had spoken of confusing waking with sleep, of the injection he remembered, and of the interval before it that he could not. He had named where he was held, the sounds he heard, and the records he had recovered. The allegations that had led to his detention remained unexamined; the inspectors kept them distinct from what he had witnessed.

“Mark that interval unknown,” he said. “Not hidden. Not reconstructed.”

The inspector entered the words, then read them back. Oren listened, eyes fixed on the slate, until the wording matched his meaning. He did not have to recover what was gone to be believed about what remained.

Rusk waited in custody under station guard, with a Concordance observer present. He was alive, unhurt, and still able to give his account. Asked whether he understood that his hearing remained scheduled, he said he did.

“I acted to defend Kestrel Deep,” he told the recorder. “That was the purpose.”

The recorder entered the answer without marking it as a finding. Rusk’s certainty did not turn his claim into a verdict; neither did his custody erase his right to make it.

Sareth stood beside Mara as the inspection continued. “You will keep your provisional duties,” he said. “Subject to review.”

“And the civilian deaths?”

“They remain your responsibility.”

Mara had no answer that could improve on the plainness of it. Sareth did not offer absolution, nor did he pretend that her decision to help now canceled the harm done before. “Your conduct will be examined alongside Rusk’s,” he said. “Future human conduct will be judged on its evidence, not presumed harmless because of you.”

“Understood.”

The words left a dry taste in her mouth. She had wanted trust to mean she could set down the past. Instead it meant she could be given work without being excused from what she had done.

Tavi returned from the control room with a slate. “The public accounts have started.”

One called Rusk a defender betrayed by his own people. Another named Mara the savior of the station. A third credited Concordance force alone with preventing catastrophe. Each made a clean story by leaving something out.

“We should publish a chronology,” Tavi said. “Sources, decisions, disputed points. An account people can correct without having to start from rumor.”

They gathered the records they could secure: command logs, access histories, custody entries, workers’ statements, blockade manifests, medical counts, and communications showing what each side knew and when. Rusk’s crime belonged in it, not merely his defense. So did the workers’ choices, made under pressure and not all in agreement; the blockade’s human costs; Sareth’s restraint; and Mara’s earlier failure to act while civilians still had time to escape.

Some records were damaged. Others had not yet been examined. They marked the gaps instead of smoothing them over.

Tavi opened the public draft. Its first summary called Mara the person who had brought the crisis to an end.

She read the sentence again. It was flattering, and not wholly false. That was what made it useful to a lie.

“Correct it,” she said.

Tavi waited.

“Say I helped end the immediate threat. Then say I had failed the people who died. Keep both.”

The revised line was less graceful and harder to turn into a rescue story. Mara approved it. Beyond the passage, trolley wheels caught at a seam in the deck, then rattled on toward the infirmary. The inspectors kept recording. Rusk waited for his hearing; Oren kept the boundary of his testimony.

Mara could not decide which legend would survive. She could refuse to purchase belonging with a lie.

At 35:40, the courier's communications system carried a burst of static through Kestrel Deep. Every speaker repeated the transmission with a slight delay, making the enforcement squadron seem to announce itself from several directions.

"Kestrel Deep, this is Concordance Enforcement Squadron Three. We have reached the outer blockade. We are proceeding under the agreed authority for inspection and medical relief. Maintain present access restrictions. Do not obstruct boarding personnel."

The message ended. Ventilation fans filled the silence. On the receiving platform, the first medical crates crossed into the station while workers and squadron guards counted the same containers on separate slates. No one called it a rescue. The word would give too much innocence to some people and too much power to others.

The inspection began at the firing installations. A joint team photographed disabled mechanisms, copied access histories, and compared surviving control records with blockade manifests. They would examine Oren's confinement site and the gallery where the relic had been concealed. Each movement was announced aloud and entered in both logs.

Mara watched a case of neurological stabilizers pass inward. Oren was alive, but the fact felt less like reassurance than a duty imposed on everyone who said it.

Rusk remained alive in accountable custody in an office near the infirmary. A guard stood at the door; his wrist monitor recorded his condition, and his access to station systems had been removed. He no longer controlled Kestrel Deep, though he still defended the purpose of his actions.

"I did what was necessary," he told Mara beside the custody recorder. "The relic could not reach Sareth remotely. If I had surrendered it, the station would have become an instrument of whatever belief he carried into it."

"You concealed it and manipulated access to the gallery."

"I prevented a greater danger."

"You also left people trapped under a blockade."

His expression hardened. "You are describing consequences as though they were intentions."

"No. I am refusing to let intentions erase them."

The recorder captured the exchange. Rusk's defense remained his own, but it could no longer change the doors, schedules, or decisions of the station.

In the infirmary, Oren lay beneath the ceiling he remembered. Sareth stood beside Mara while the medical officer explained that another relic exposure might recover fragments of memory, but could also deepen the neurological damage.

"No further exposure," Oren said.

The officer began to object. Oren lifted a hand.

"I will give the statement I can give. I will not be made into a complete witness by being injured again. The agreement does not require me to become a perfectly restored witness."

He asked where his belongings were, who had accessed his inspection account, whether his distress transmission had reached the squadron, and whether the missing interval would be marked unknown. Then he described the confinement site, the sounds he remembered, the recovered records, and the medication entering the port beside his hand.

"Before that, I remember nothing," he said. "My later waking and sleeping were confused. The absence is part of my testimony."

The recorder kept the silence after his words. Oren was not arguing against Mara. He was keeping possession of the boundary between what had happened and what he could honestly say had happened.

Outside, Sareth spoke to Mara in the narrow passage. "I will offer conditional professional trust," he said. "It is not absolution, and it is not a promise that future human conduct will be presumed harmless."

"I did not ask for that."

"You wanted belonging."

The words landed because they were true. Mara looked through the glass at Oren and at workers carrying supplies beyond him. "Yes," she said.

"Then your duties remain provisional and subject to review. There will be no instant promotion, and nothing changes your responsibility for the civilians who died."

"I understand."

Sareth's restraint was entered into the joint record alongside his warning. Neither was softened for public use.

Tavi returned from the control room with a slate. "The public accounts have started."
