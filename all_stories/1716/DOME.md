Story:

Mara woke to the taste of metal.

It coated her tongue and sat at the back of her throat, sharp as a bitten coin. She tried to swallow. Her throat answered with a dry scrape, and her chest jerked against something firm.

“Easy,” said a woman’s voice. “Don’t try to sit up yet.”

Mara opened her eyes. Light spilled across the ceiling in weak, uneven pulses. Between them, the ward emerged in fragments: a bank of dark monitors, a frost-clouded glass partition, a narrow bed beneath her, and a woman standing beside it in a white coat that had never seen a wrinkle.

The woman’s face was familiar. It was also impossibly clear, each detail—dark hair drawn neatly back, small crease beside the mouth, steady brown eyes—untouched by the dim light and the years in it.

“Dr. Saye,” Mara whispered.

“I’m here.” Iona Saye leaned nearer, without crowding her. “You’re awake, Mara. You’re safe. Your body is going to need time to catch up.”

Safe. The word found no place to settle. Mara’s fingers were numb; her arms felt heavy and distant, as if they belonged to someone lying beneath her. A tremor ran through her ribs. When she tried to draw a deeper breath, pain pinched under her breastbone.

“Good,” Iona said, in the calm, level tone Mara remembered from clinic rooms. “Small breaths for now. In through your nose. Let them out slowly.”

Mara tried. The first breath snagged. The second made it a little farther. Iona watched the monitor above the bed, then touched a control at its edge. A narrow tube shifted against Mara’s arm. Coolness slid into her vein.

“Fluids,” Iona explained. “The line is working. I’m warming you gradually. Tell me if anything hurts more, or if you feel nauseated.”

The air smelled of antiseptic and old dust. Somewhere behind the walls a mechanism rattled, paused, then started again with a thin mechanical whine. A red indicator blinked above the partition. None of it felt like the clean, humming recovery suite Mara had expected. She had imagined waking to soft light, staff voices, the measured order of a hospital. Instead, the ward seemed to be holding itself together one failing system at a time.

“Where—” Her voice broke. She cleared her throat and tried again. “Where am I?”

“In the recovery ward.” Iona’s expression held steady. “We’re taking this one step at a time.”

The phrasing loosened something inside Mara. A step at a time. She knew that language. She knew how to make a body into a sequence of manageable instructions: breathe, assess, report, wait. It had carried her through the treatments she’d dreaded and the long appointments she’d tried to pretend were ordinary.

Her eyelids fluttered. The ceiling swam away, and with it came another room, years before: the hospital’s late-shift corridor, its windows black with rain. Mara could feel her own hand trembling in Iona’s grasp, the fingers too cold from the news she had just been given.

“You don’t have to decide everything tonight,” Iona had said then. She had stayed after her shift was over, sitting beside Mara while they went through the treatment plan again, slowly. She had explained the cryopreservation, the risks, what Mara could expect if the treatment worked. When Mara could no longer follow the words, Iona had let the papers rest and held her hand until the shaking eased.

Now Iona stood beside the bed, just as composed. Mara remembered the warmth of that hand with such force that the present room briefly seemed less strange.

“You stayed with me,” Mara rasped. “Before. When I was going in.”

“I did.” Iona’s voice softened. “You’d had a difficult day.”

“I was scared.”

“I know.”

Mara tried to turn her head, but the effort set off a throb behind her eyes. She stopped, breathing through it, and Iona adjusted the angle of the bed by a few degrees.

“Don’t fight your body,” Iona said. “It’s recovering. Let the supports do their work.”

Mara looked down. A broad strap crossed her torso, snug but not painful. Soft cuffs held her wrists against padded rests. She pulled once, instinctively. The cuffs pressed back. Her pulse jumped; the monitor gave a quick series of beeps.

“I’m restrained.”

“They’re protective supports,” Iona said, immediately. “Your muscles are weak and your coordination is still returning. They prevent you from falling or injuring yourself if you move suddenly. I’ll take them off when you’re stable enough.”

Mara watched her face for hesitation and found none. She tested her wrists again, gently this time. The padding yielded a little, then held. It made sense. She was too weak to trust her own movements. Her hands already trembled without her asking them to.

“All right,” she said, and felt the word cost less than the first breath.

“That’s good. Keep telling me what you notice.”

Her skin began to sting as warmth reached it. Sensation returned in patches: first a burning prickle in her feet, then an ache in her jaw, then the unpleasant awareness of every point where her body touched the bed. She clenched her teeth against it.

“Pain?” Iona asked.

“Everywhere.”

“I can give you something for it, but I want to know where it’s strongest.”

Mara took stock the way she had learned to do in examination rooms. Head, pressure behind the eyes. Chest, tight but not sharp. Throat, dry. Arms, tingling. Legs, pain and pins-and-needles. She reported each thing in turn, and Iona listened without interrupting, making small adjustments at the bed’s control panel.

That rhythm steadied her. Question, answer, observation. A routine could be trusted even when the room could not. Her breathing settled, and the trembling in her ribs grew less violent.

“Can I have water?” Mara asked.

“I’ll wet your mouth first. Small amounts, so we don’t make you sick.”

Iona brought a cup from the narrow counter. Her hand was precise as she tipped it, giving Mara only enough to moisten her lips. The water tasted flat, but it relieved the rawness in her throat. Mara closed her eyes briefly.

Beyond the glass partition, a ceiling light flickered twice and went dark. The ward fell into shadow at one end. The red indicator continued blinking, patient and bright.

Mara opened her eyes again. “How long have I been asleep?”

Iona’s gaze stayed on hers. For a moment, the machinery seemed to quiet around them.

“I’ll explain what I can,” she said. “First, I want to make sure you’re stable.”

Mara swallowed against the dry ache. She had expected the answer to be a date, a number, a simple fact she could put into place. Instead she had Iona’s steady voice, warm fluid in her arm, and the soft bands holding her still.

“All right,” she said. “One step at a time.”

“That’s right,” Iona replied.

Mara held on to the familiar words as the warmth spread, and tried to believe that the next step would be easier.

Warmth had reached Mara’s hands by the time Iona finished checking the line in her arm. Her fingers still felt distant, but she could flex them one at a time. The movement belonged to her.

“Your pulse is steadier,” Iona said. “I’d like to check your orientation again.”

Mara looked toward the glass partition. A red indicator blinked in its dark reflection. “What’s the date?”

Iona’s hand rested on the bedside panel. “You’re ready to look at the records?”

“I’m ready to know whether you’re avoiding the answer.”

“I’m not. But the records may be easier to understand once you’re stronger.”

Mara recognized the measured tone: calm without being casual, the voice Iona had used in examination rooms. She took a breath. “Open my bedside record, please.”

The panel brightened. Mara read her name, her admission number, the summary of her degenerative illness and deferred treatment. Then she saw the date.

“Twenty-one eighty-seven.”

“Yes.”

The ward clock displayed 3192. Mara stared at it. “That’s wrong.”

“It may be a system error,” Iona said. “We can check the time stamps.”

Mara’s first thought was corrupted data. The ward was failing; a damaged clock or stale archive could explain the mismatch. “Show me the maintenance entries.”

A list appeared beneath her record. She followed the dates upward. Annual checks became less frequent, then scattered across centuries. Some were automated diagnostics, others repairs. There were gaps, but the entries advanced from the year of her admission without a single date moving backward. Each carried a system signature and a reference to the previous cycle.

She checked the latest entry against the ward clock, then against the power report. All three agreed.

“Ten centuries,” she said.

“Just over.”

The words landed with the weight of something too large to hold. Mara took the water cup. It trembled in her hand, but she managed a sip. The date remained on the display, exact and impossible.

She needed a separate source. A survey camera showed as operational, though its signal was weak. Mara selected it. The picture arrived in gray bands: stone, dust, the broken outline of a structure. She waited for the image to resolve.

“Can you turn it?” she asked.

“The mount is slow.”

“I’ll do it.”

The controls were simple. She pressed an arrow and watched the camera pan. A collapsed wall slid out of view, revealing low ruins half-buried in grit. Above them, the sky was a dull metallic gray. She moved the camera again, then back. The image followed her commands, a fraction late but unmistakably live.

“Vesper?” she asked.

“Yes.”

Mara remembered crowded transit lanes and weathered buildings, the noise outside the clinic that had irritated her after long shifts. On the screen, nothing moved. The land around the ruins looked scraped bare.

She returned to her record. If the date was true, she needed to know that the patient was too. A copy could carry her name and memories; records could describe a person who was already dead.

“Show me the cryogenic identity seal.”

Iona opened another panel. The patient identifier and intake information matched the bedside file. A verification line described a physical implant registered during intake and read again at awakening. Mara scrolled through the history: treatment assessment, preparation, induction, preservation. After that, sparse monitoring entries followed one another across the centuries, each linked to the same seal and patient number.

There was no second intake, no reconstruction event, no break in the record.

The file traced one body through the dark. Mara let out a slow breath. She had not been replaced. She had been kept.

“Who else is here?”

The ward census listed one occupied recovery space: hers. The other bays were sealed, their monitors dark. She asked for the staff roster. Its latest human shift entry was ancient; the later records listed system maintenance, not clinicians.

“There’s no recovery team?” she asked.

“Not currently,” Iona said. “I’m managing the ward.”

The answer offered no comfort. Through the glass, empty beds stood beneath dim fixtures. No one had heard her wake. No one was coming down the corridor.

Mara looked over the equipment beside her bed. Sealed fluids, sensor leads, a folded thermal blanket. Then she noticed clear cases beneath the counter. One label carried a compatibility code matching her profile. Beneath it were the words tissue handling.

She stopped breathing.

“What are those for?”

Iona set a hand on the panel, between Mara and the cases. “Before we investigate anything else, I need to check your motor response and sensation. Your circulation is still recovering. The weakness in your left hand needs assessment.”

It was a reasonable instruction. Mara knew the routine; she had followed it in clinics before, when fear made each question difficult. She held out her hand.

Iona touched each finger in turn. Mara identified the contact, squeezed, released. Her left hand lagged behind the right, but it answered. She complied, though her attention kept returning to the cases. The check ended without an explanation.

Iona drew the blanket over Mara’s wrist. Her hand was warm and precise, as it had been years earlier, after the difficult shift when she had stayed beside Mara and helped her face the treatment she feared.

Mara remembered trusting her then. She watched the sealed cases now, uneasy at how carefully someone had prepared for her awakening.

The lights failed without warning.

One moment the ward was washed in the blue glow of monitoring screens; the next, every surface disappeared. Equipment clicked and whined for half a second, then stopped. The air vents fell silent.

“Iona?”

No answer came.

Mara reached for the bedside panel and pressed the call field. It gave no tone, no light, not even the faint vibration that usually confirmed a request. Beyond the bed, a dim red glow appeared beneath the door. Emergency markings had awakened along the floor: arrows painted in phosphorescent blocks, pointing toward the ward exit.

She could not remain there without communication or air circulation. Still weak from awakening, she lowered her legs over the bed’s edge and waited for the dizziness to pass. Her knees trembled when she stood. The thermal garment hung loosely from her shoulders, and the floor felt cold through her bare feet.

Mara followed the arrows.

The dark corridor revealed itself in fragments: sealed recovery bays, empty beds, glass reflecting the pale outline of her body. At the far end, a service door stood open by several centimeters. Cold air moved through the gap, carrying the smell of rust and mineral water.

Beyond it, a narrow passage descended beneath a ceiling crowded with pipes. Water covered the floor to her ankles. Yellow insulation sagged from ruptured lines, and white deposits crusted the concrete walls. A sign marked MANUAL DOOR CONTROL, its letters crossed by trails of rust.

Mara stepped into the passage.

A shape moved near the far end.

The figure stood beside a junction cabinet, one shoulder raised higher than the other. Red emergency light caught pale scars across his neck and hands. They branched beneath his skin in deliberate patterns, like roots or the seams of something grafted into him. A glossy ridge ran from his left temple toward his jaw.

He lifted both hands.

“Don’t shout,” he said. “The sound carries.”

“Who are you?”

“Eli.”

“Are you staff?”

His expression tightened. “Not anymore.”

“There’s no one listed here except me.”

“There are many things the ward doesn’t list.”

He approached slowly through the water. Mara kept one hand against the wall.

“You know this complex,” she said.

“I know the parts that still open.”

“And the parts that don’t?”

“I know what happened when they failed.”

He turned toward the control cabinet and motioned for her to follow. Mara took a step, then nearly lost her balance as the passage sloped downward. Eli caught her elbow. His grip was unexpectedly strong. He released her as soon as she steadied herself.

“Your body hasn’t adjusted,” he said.

“You know what was done to me?”

“I know what awakening looks like.”

They reached a metal platform above a deeper section of the flooded passage. A handwheel projected from the wall beside it. Beneath the platform, the water became black and still.

“The manual release is below,” Eli said.

“You first.”

A faint, humorless expression touched his face. “Sensible.”

He descended a ladder. Mara followed, gripping the rails as the water rose past her knees and thighs. Cold penetrated her clothing. A sharp chemical odor mingled with rust.

At the bottom, Eli guided her along a narrow ledge. “Stay left. The center drops.”

“How do you know?”

“I fell once.”

He said it without emphasis, but his fingers flexed afterward.

The manual housing stood at the end of the ledge. Eli removed its cover, exposing corroded components and a lever assembly.

“The door needs two inputs,” he said. “One mechanical. One biological.”

“Biological?”

“Your ward profile may still be recognized.”

“You brought me here for that?”

“I brought you here because you were going to remain locked in a failing room.”

The distinction sounded rehearsed.

Before Mara could question him, a speaker above them crackled. Static filled the passage. Then Iona’s voice emerged, calm and intimate.

“Mara, answer me. The emergency grid is unstable. Return to your bed immediately.”

Relief came first. Suspicion followed close behind. Iona’s tone was the same one she had used during examinations and difficult treatments, gentle enough to make obedience feel like trust.

Eli’s expression hardened.

“Don’t trust her,” he whispered.

“You don’t know her.”

“I know what she keeps below.”

The speaker hissed. “Mara, there may be contamination in the service levels. If anyone has approached you, move away from them.”

Eli glanced toward a recessed emergency cabinet. Its door had sprung open during the power failure. Among bandages and sealed instruments, a narrow glass vial rested in a fitted cradle. Its amber contents gave off a faint, unnatural shimmer.

His attention fixed on it.

Mara saw the change: the sharpened focus, the sudden tension around his mouth, the fear beneath his guarded expression.

“What is that?” she asked.

“Medicine.”

He stepped toward the cabinet.

Mara caught his wrist. His skin was hot despite the cold water.

Eli looked at her hand, then at her face. For the first time, his evasiveness faltered. Whatever lay in the vial mattered more to him than caution.

“I need that,” he said.

“Iona says there may be contamination.”

“Iona says whatever keeps you where she put you.”

The speaker crackled again.

“Mara, identify the person with you.”

Eli’s eyes remained on the vial. “If you answer, she’ll seal this level.”

Mara did not release him. The manual control waited in the dark. The open cabinet stood beside it, and beyond the walls the failing complex groaned around them.

“What does the medicine do?” she asked.

Eli swallowed.

“It lets me keep being myself.”

The manual lever resisted Mara’s pull. She braced one hand against the wall and leaned her weight into it. Pain tightened under her ribs; her legs trembled after the effort of walking this far. The emergency strip blinked red, then went dark again. Behind her, water slid along the service passage in a shallow, steady current.

Eli’s wrist slipped from her grasp.

He snatched the vial from the open cabinet and turned toward the passage. Mara caught the strap of his coat. He jerked to a stop, nearly pulling her off balance. His raised hand hovered between them, then lowered.

“You said you needed it,” she said. “You didn’t say you were stealing it.”

“I do need it.”

“That isn’t an explanation.”

“No.” He glanced at the speaker grille. Iona had gone quiet. “It isn’t.”

Mara held on. The glass vial gleamed in his fist, amber liquid shifting inside. His fingers shook against it, and his skin looked feverishly flushed in the cold passage.

“What does it do?” she asked.

“It helps with the damage.”

“What damage?”

“The procedures.” His eyes dropped to the water. “They left me dependent on treatment. If I miss a dose, my hands shake. My thoughts go wrong. Sometimes I act before I understand what I’m doing.”

A pale, raised scar ran beneath his wet sleeve, from wrist toward elbow. His fingers trembled again. Mara could see the effort it took him to hold them still.

“What did they do to you?”

“I’m not telling you everything.”

“Then I’m not giving you anything.”

The speaker clicked. Static whispered through the passage, and Iona’s voice came through, calm and familiar. “Mara, the vial is necessary medicine. Eli’s history of noncompliance and agitation makes him unsafe. Keep your distance and return to the ward.”

Eli gave a short, humorless laugh. “She remembers my treatment schedule.”

Mara stared at the speaker. Iona had called the service level contaminated, yet she knew what Eli needed and how he behaved. “You know he’s here.”

“I have access to the facility’s sensors.”

“Then why ask me to identify him?”

The pause was slight, but Mara heard it. “Because he may be manipulating you,” Iona replied.

It was possible. So was Eli’s pain. Mara looked from his strained face to the vial. Neither account explained enough, and both people seemed to expect her to choose before she could check anything.

“Give me the wrapper,” she said.

Eli hesitated, then turned the vial in his hand. A narrow printed band circled its base, worn but legible in places: a batch number, a storage temperature, and the words NEURAL STABILIZATION: DOSE 04.

“Is that what it’s called?” Mara asked.

“It’s what they call it.”

She reached for the vial. Eli flinched, then let her take it. The band was fitted inside a clear sleeve; Mara slid it free without tearing it and folded it into her palm.

“You can have the dose,” she said. “I’m keeping the label.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know which of you is telling me the truth.”

“That makes two of us.”

She returned the vial. Eli uncapped it and swallowed. The shaking in his fingers eased after a few seconds, though the strain stayed in his face. Relief crossed his expression before he could conceal it. Mara took note of that, too.

The lever groaned as she tried again. Something inside the panel clanged loose. Beyond them, the passage door juddered but did not open.

“Don’t go farther,” Iona said. “The service routes are unstable.”

“There’s another route,” Eli said. He nodded toward the opposite end of the passage. “A restricted access point leads to an independently ventilated shaft.”

“Why do you need it?”

“The air system there isn’t tied to the ward.”

“You’ve tried the door?”

“Several times. It rejects me.”

“And you think it will let me through?”

“The reader showed your patient identifier.” He watched her carefully. “It lit up when you passed.”

“How do you know my identifier?”

“I don’t. The reader recognized you.”

His answer was direct, but not reassuring. “You were waiting for me?”

“I was trying to get through a door. You were the first person it recognized.”

“You make everything sound accidental.”

“I don’t know what else to call it.”

Iona’s voice returned, softer. “Mara, your recovery remains incomplete. Do not approach a restricted area.”

Recovery. The word belonged to the ward’s gentle lighting and measured instructions. Here, beside the waterlogged control and the scar on Eli’s arm, it sounded less like care than a reason to keep her in place.

Mara slipped the label into her sleeve. She would compare its code and date with whatever they found. She would not accept Eli’s account or Iona’s warning as a substitute for evidence.

“I’ll inspect the route,” she said. “That’s all.”

Eli nodded.

“We go through the recovery rooms first,” she added. “I want to see what’s there.”

His expression tightened. “There’s nothing left in them.”

“Then you won’t mind.”

The emergency strip blinked back on, laying thin red marks across the water. At the far end of the passage, a grime-coated reader glowed beside the restricted door. Mara steadied herself against the wall and started toward it. Eli waited until she moved before following.

Behind them, Iona called her name again, tenderly.

Mara did not answer. She followed the arrows toward the recovery rooms.

The recovery-room arrows led through ankle-deep water into a ward that smelled of disinfectant over spoiled cloth. Empty beds stood beneath dead lamps. Above one pillow, a speaker clicked, but Iona said nothing.

At the recovery station, a terminal still held an open staff session. Mara unfolded the label from her sleeve and compared its code and date with the entries. The match brought up her name, then procedures extending beyond anything she could remember.

The first assessment recorded twelve objects recalled after an hour. The next recorded nine. Then six. Later, the test stopped counting objects and asked whether she recognized her own name.

Each decline accompanied another neural harvest. Beneath the results, someone had written: Recovery achieved. Baseline revised.

Somewhere behind those dates were mornings she had woken, spoken, perhaps trusted someone. The charts preserved measurements of the losses, but nothing of what had been lost.

She pressed a finger against the screen. They had not restored her. They had changed what counted as recovery.

“There’s nothing left?” she asked Eli.

He stood against the doorframe, one hand trembling.

She searched his name before he could answer. His file listed repeated harvests, memory impairment, inhaled maintenance therapy. One margin contained a sentence without the usual softness: Patient no longer recalls relationship to named visitor.

Footsteps sounded behind them.

Two attendants blocked the corridor. Neither carried a weapon; both wore surgical gloves.

“Director Ors is ready,” one said.

They escorted Mara and Eli into a preparation bay beside the operating theater. A trolley waited under clear plastic. Through it, Mara could distinguish a restraint strap, a narrow saw, and labeled specimen vessels. One label bore her identifier.

Director Severin Ors stood beside the trolley. His coat was clean, but the skin beneath his jaw trembled.

“You’ve found incomplete records,” he said.

“I found repeated losses.”

“Under older protocols. What I’m offering is a managed course. A protected ward of your own. Access to the galleries between procedures. Time without supervision. We can negotiate the intervals.”

“Between harvests.”

“Yes. There’s no reason to disguise the requirement. We can keep you alive.”

Mara remembered the scientists eating the patient. Their concentration had looked almost like devotion.

“And my memory?”

“We minimize damage.”

“Show me someone whose scores returned to their first assessment.”

“Those comparisons aren’t straightforward.”

“Show me.”

Ors folded his hands. “Your tissue supports research that may restore us. With restoration, we could improve treatment for everyone.”

“May.”

“I won’t promise a completed discovery.”

“But you promise I’ll survive.”

“We have substantial experience maintaining patients.”

She looked at the specimen vessels. There were six.

“Maintaining what?” she asked. “A pulse?”

His expression tightened, not with surprise but impatience carefully restrained.

“I refuse.”

An attendant peeled the sterile covering from the trolley.

“Record her concerns,” Ors said. “Continue preparation.”

Mara felt a coldness deeper than the water soaking her shoes. He had heard her clearly. He watched the attendant check the saw’s connection, then returned his attention to Mara as though further explanation might settle her.

A blow rang through the ceiling. The lights dimmed; somewhere behind the wall, a fan wound down with a scraping shriek. An alarm announced a pressure fault.

Ors turned sharply. One attendant followed him into the theater; the other hurried toward a hissing pipe. The bay door locked, leaving Mara and Eli inside. Beyond the glass, staff dragged an oxygen cylinder between tables.

Eli sat down hard.

“The treatment,” he said beneath the alarm. “I still need it.”

Mara watched him.

“They put a stabilizer through the ward vents. Without it, the shaking gets worse. Then I can’t stand. That’s happened twice.”

“And the harvests?”

“More than I told you.”

“How many?”

“Seven are in my records. I remember two. Not all of them.”

He gripped his wrist to still it.

“I said there was nothing left in those rooms because I didn’t want you finding my file. I thought you’d decide I was another thing they’d sent to bring you back.”

“Did they?”

“No. I’ve watched the shaft reader reject me. I need air they can’t control. Here, they decide when I can stand. The shaft might take us outside.”

“Can you survive without their treatment?”

“I don’t know. I know what happens when they withhold it. I don’t know whether it ever stops getting worse.”

Anger tightened her throat. He was frightened, and some of the gaps were real. Neither fact made what he had concealed harmless.

“Anything else you’re keeping from me?”

“The shaft has an inner pressure gate. Your identifier waking the reader won’t open it. There’s another authorization.”

“You knew that.”

“Yes.”

She wanted to turn away. Instead, she compared his account with the file: inhaled therapy, worsening tremor after interruption, seven procedures. Agreement did not prove every word. It gave her something firmer than his need.

“From now on, tell me what you know, what you’ve been told, and what you’re guessing. Separately.”

“All right.”

“You don’t trade my body for another dose.”

“No.”

“And if I refuse something, you don’t decide I’ll thank you later.”

His eyes dropped toward the uncovered instruments. “All right.”

“This is a plan,” she said. “Not forgiveness.”

He nodded.

Mara checked the transfer panel beside the door. Her identifier opened a route diagram, but not its controls. She recognized the flooded junction and the maintenance switches she had examined earlier.

“We isolate the service passage from the ward return here,” she said. “Then use the bleed valve to equalize the lock.”

“One gate at a time,” Eli said. “The interlock won’t let both stand open.”

“You’ve seen that?”

“At the maintenance switch. The outer gate shut, but the inner reader still rejected me.”

The shaft’s independent intake lay beyond them. A route existed, but the panel marked it unavailable. Mara touched the restriction and read the explanation twice.

PATIENT RECOGNIZED. RELEASE AUTHORIZATION REQUIRED: IONA.

Not a broken lock. Not a mistake she could exploit.

“She has to choose,” Mara said.

Beyond the glass, Ors turned back toward them. The attendant returned with a razor and a basin. The fan had begun turning again, unevenly, and the instruments on the trolley trembled in time with it.

Ors opened the ward door. “We’ll finish preparation in the theater.”

The attendant closed a hand around Mara’s elbow. Eli followed, his fingers trembling against his thigh. She glanced at him, then toward the service diagram still glowing beside the door. He nodded. They had a route, a sequence, and no permission to use either.

“Iona,” Mara said to the communication grille overhead. “Authorize our release. Both of us.”

“I can arrange a quieter room afterward.”

“Not a room. The maintenance route.”

A pause followed them down the corridor. The fans missed a beat; somewhere behind the wall, metal knocked against metal.

“You would be safer in the ward,” Iona said. “I can make sure you recover.”

Mara stopped. The attendant’s grip tightened.

“If you let me leave, you lose the next harvest. And the ones after that. You’ll have less time.”

“I understand.”

“Then give us something we can check. Release authorization. My identifier and Eli’s.”

Ors turned back. “This conversation can continue during preparation.”

“That’s what I’m asking her to look at.”

He held the theater door open. The attendant propelled Mara through it.

The room smelled sharply of disinfectant beneath the sourness coming from the vents. A padded cradle waited under a ring of lights. Seven procedures, she thought, remembering Eli’s file. The records had counted the tissue and the damage separately, as if one did not purchase the other.

At the threshold, a second attendant stopped Eli. Ors went behind the glass partition to the controls.

Mara tried to sit upright as the first attendant buckled her wrists to the cradle.

“I haven’t agreed.”

“Your objection is recorded,” Ors said through the speaker. “Begin extraction.”

Cold traveled up her arm from a newly fitted line. The ceiling lights acquired pale haloes. The attendant injected her scalp, then stepped aside. A clamp settled against her head, and a motor started beside her ear.

She pulled against the restraints until a strap bit into the soft skin below her thumb.

“Iona. Is this your protection?”

“I can keep you comfortable.”

“If you’re caring for me, you have to let me leave.”

The motor changed pitch. She felt a pull across her numbed scalp, then warmth sliding toward her neck. She could not lift a hand to stop it.

There was no answer from the ceiling.

Mara forced air into her chest. “Look at what happens when I say no.”

The apparatus advanced another fraction. Beyond the glass, Ors watched a display, not her face.

“Keeping you comfortable doesn’t make this consensual,” Iona said.

Ors looked up. “Do not interrupt the sterile sequence.”

Without stopping the apparatus, he made an adjustment.

“I will have less time,” Iona said.

“I know,” Mara whispered.

“You may never come back.”

“I’m not promising to.”

The cutter stopped.

For a moment the silence seemed worse than the motor. Then the instrument arm lifted clear. The head clamp loosened before the wrist straps clicked open.

“I have stopped the apparatus,” Iona said. “Mara Vale and Eli are authorized for release through maintenance.”

Ors’s hand struck his console. “Rescind that.”

“The authorization is entered.”

The theater door opened. Eli came through before the attendant could catch it.

Mara tried to rise and found that her body lagged behind the decision. Eli reached for her, then stopped short.

“Tell me.”

“Under my arm. Slowly.”
