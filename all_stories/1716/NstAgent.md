## Chapter 1: Awakening

The first thing Elias Vance remembered was drowning.

Not in water. In a pressureless black that pressed against his teeth and filled the cavities behind his eyes. He tried to breathe and found his lungs packed with winter. Tried to scream and heard only a soft internal crackle, like ice flexing on a pond.

Then came the light.

It stabbed through his eyelids in red bands. Something hissed. Valves chattered overhead. A bell gave one weak note and died.

“Easy,” a voice said. “Do not fight the thaw.”

Elias opened his eyes.

A face leaned over him, narrow and hairless, its skin the gray-yellow of old wax. The eyes were too large and black at the centers, filmed with a fine reflective membrane that flickered sideways when the figure blinked. A delicate ridge of chitin ran from its brow to the back of its skull. It wore a stained medical coat fastened with metal clips, and beneath the coat something moved in slow, folded rhythms.

Elias tried to speak. His tongue was wood.

The creature placed two fingers against his throat. The fingers were jointed twice.

“Your larynx is functioning,” it said. “That is a promising beginning. Dr. Elias Vance?”

He swallowed through needles. “Where?”

“Below the city. Below what remains of the city.”

The chamber swam into focus around him. He was lying in a vertical capsule opened like a flower, its transparent lid folded back into the wall. Frost steamed from his skin. Clear nutrient lines entered his wrists and the hollow beneath his collarbone, pulsing with a slow amber fluid. The capsule’s interior was not smooth steel but a patchwork of polymer plates, dark and ridged, overlapping one another like healed grafts. Along the seams, the material puckered and contracted.

It looked less repaired than scarred.

The air smelled of formaldehyde, wet stone, and the sweet, fungal odor of a cellar where something had died and continued growing.

“Identify yourself,” Elias rasped.

The insectoid physician inclined its head. “I am Imago. Medical custodian of the lower preservation complex. You have been asleep for one thousand and three years, give or take the accuracy of our calendars.”

The statement entered Elias’s mind without meaning. He tried to fit it against his last memory: a launch facility, a consent form displayed on glass, his own hand signing beneath a paragraph about reversible hypothermia. There had been snow outside. His sister had been crying, though she had insisted she was not.

“One thousand…”

“Three years.”

“Impossible.”

“Historically, yes. Biologically, no.” Imago’s mandibles did not move when it spoke, but a small opening beneath its jaw pulsed with each word. “The Eternal Faculty maintained the vault through the Collapse. We preserved you.”

Elias turned his head. Pain flashed white down his spine.

There were twelve other capsules in the chamber. Their lids stood open. Inside each lay a nest of straps, tubing, and pale dust. In one, a skeleton had collapsed inward beneath a transparent sheet. In another, the bones had been threaded through with white fungal roots. Several skulls still wore the silver crowns of neural monitors.

“How many survived?”

Imago hesitated. That hesitation was the first human thing Elias saw in it.

“You did.”

The answer left the chamber colder than the thaw had.

A servitor rolled forward on six narrow wheels. It carried a basin and a bundle of gray clothing. Its upper surface was upholstered in something that resembled skin, though the stitching was too neat and the pores too uniform. Imago disconnected the lines from Elias’s arms. Blood welled, then stopped before it could run. A transparent membrane sealed each puncture.

“Your musculature will require support,” Imago said. “Do not attempt to stand.”

Elias attempted to stand.

His knees folded. Imago caught him with surprising strength. Up close, the doctor smelled of antiseptic, dust, and a faint metallic savor that reminded Elias of a butcher’s drain.

The floor tilted beneath him. He clung to the creature’s coat and saw that several dark stains had dried into its sleeve.

“Where is everyone?” he asked.

“Everyone is not a useful category anymore.”

Imago guided him into a wheeled frame. Straps crossed his chest. A mask delivered warm, bitter air into his lungs. “The Collapse altered the surface. War, famine, atmospheric failure, disease. The survivors adapted. Some adapted better than others.”

“The government?”

“Gone.”

“Civilization?”

Imago looked toward the dead capsules. “Under revision.”

They left the chamber through a round door whose edges were furred with pale mycelium. It withdrew from their approach, shrinking like fingers from a flame. Beyond lay a corridor grown through the bedrock. Fungal shelves climbed the walls in tiers, each holding glass vessels, surgical instruments, or labeled bundles of tissue. Dim lamps glowed beneath translucent membranes overhead. Their light was the color of old bruises.

Elias heard water moving inside the walls. Or blood.

He passed a painted sign: RECOVERY, in a language descended from English but warped by unfamiliar symbols. Beneath it, someone had scratched a small eye with legs.

“What is the Faculty?” he asked.

“Physicians. Researchers. Custodians of continuity.”

“Human?”

Imago’s reflective eyes fixed on him. “That depends on which definition you preserved.”

The corridor opened onto a junction. A gurney emerged from a side passage, pushed by two attendants in hooded masks. On it lay a woman, perhaps forty, her wrists restrained in padded bands. A clear line ran into her arm. Her head rolled toward Elias. Her eyes were open but unfocused, pupils pinpricks beneath a film of sedation.

She made a small sound.

“Please,” she whispered.

One attendant pressed a gloved hand over her mouth. The other checked the line and smiled at Imago.

“Guest of honor is ready for the elevator.”

Imago stopped.

Elias watched the woman’s chest rise and fall beneath a stained blanket. A patch had been shaved from her temple. Beneath it, something metallic glimmered in the skin.

“Where is she going?” he asked.

“Treatment.”

The attendant’s smile widened. “A very important treatment.”

Imago moved again, pushing Elias past before he could ask more. His hands tightened on the frame’s arms. He had no strength, no shoes, no knowledge of the corridors. Yet the physician’s silence had become an answer with its edges filed off.

At the end of the passage, a cracked viewport looked upward through layers of concrete. Elias saw the surface.

A city stood beneath a sickly green sky, though “stood” was generous. Towers had split open, their steel skeletons exposed like ribs. Bridges sagged between buildings webbed in black growth. Fires burned in windowless husks, too steady to be fires and too red to be lights. A pale dust moved through the avenues in long, deliberate currents.

No rescue beacon shone. No aircraft crossed the sky. The world above was not an answer waiting to be reached. It was a corpse with weather passing through it.

Elias felt the first true stir of fear—not the panic of waking, but something quieter, more intelligent. It moved through him like a diagnostic probe.

Why him?

Imago placed a hand on his shoulder. The claws did not pierce the fabric.

“Rest,” it said. “You have been returned to history.”

Elias looked back at the corridor, toward the elevator and the sedated woman disappearing behind its doors.

“No,” he whispered.

The word came out thin, but it belonged to him.

He had not been returned to history.

He had been selected from it.

## Chapter 2: The Eternal Faculty

Imago brought him through a door that had once been marked **STERILE TRANSFER**.

The letters remained, but something pale and fibrous had grown over them, crossing the steel in branching mats. When the door opened, a breath of cold air touched Vance’s face. It smelled of alcohol, wet stone, and the sweet mineral rot of meat kept too long in a sealed place.

Beyond lay a cathedral.

Its walls rose three stories beneath a ribbed vault of concrete and blackened conduit. Cabinets stood where saints might have stood, each one filled with preserved organs suspended in amber fluid. A heart the size of a child’s fist floated above a placard. A double-lobed lung drifted beside it, its bronchi threaded with silver wire. Human spines had been arranged in arches along the upper gallery, vertebrae linked by transparent tubing.

At the far end, beneath a cracked mosaic of a caduceus, three figures waited.

“Dr. Elias Vance,” said the tallest. “Welcome back to history.”

He wore a long coat of gray polymer, though the coat could not conceal the breadth of his shoulders or the dark plates that rose from his neck. His head was nearly human, except for the black chitin masking his jaw and the two narrow, glistening eyes set above his ordinary ones. A brass insignia, shaped like a closed hand, rested over his sternum.

“Provost Malkus,” Imago said.

The second figure inclined her head. She was thin and elegant, her skin translucent over a lattice of pale armor. Filaments like white hair descended from her scalp into a collar of data ports. Her hands ended in delicate hooked fingers, each tipped with a lens.

“Archivist Seraphine.”

The last was squat and broad, with a surgeon’s apron fastened over a carapace that shone wetly beneath the cathedral lamps. His face had been replaced by a smooth surgical mask. Three red lenses blinked where his eyes should have been.

“Surgeon Vey,” Imago said.

Vance gripped the rails of his wheeled frame. His knees trembled beneath the gray clothes they had given him. “You’re doctors.”

“We were,” said Vey.

Malkus gestured toward the specimens. “Titles are more durable than bodies. The Faculty preserves both.”

Vance looked again at the organs in their jars. “What happened here?”

“The Collapse,” Seraphine answered. Her voice had a faint clicking undertone, as if another speaker repeated her words from inside her throat. “The climate wars, the plagues, the wars against the plagues. The surface governments failed in sequence. The hospitals became fortresses. The fortresses became tombs.”

“And you survived underground.”

“We prepared to,” Malkus said. “We altered ourselves before the air turned toxic and the food chains became theoretical. Chitin to preserve the skin. Redundant organs. Fungal symbiosis. Efficient digestion.”

A dispenser beside him gave a soft mechanical sigh. Warm vapor issued from its nozzle. Something in it smelled like broth, iron, and the unmistakable animal sweetness of opened flesh.

Vance’s stomach tightened.

Malkus noticed. “Nutrient concentrate.”

“From what?”

“Whatever the station can process.”

The answer was delivered with such administrative calm that it frightened him more than a denial would have.

Seraphine approached, her lenses narrowing over him. “You are a remarkable restoration, Dr. Vance. The thawing protocols were primitive by our standards, yet your tissues accepted them. Your cellular senescence is minimal. Your neural architecture is unusually stable.”

“Why me?”

The Faculty exchanged glances. Imago did not.

“Because you are a legacy genome,” Seraphine said. “A surviving reference. A bridge to the human baseline.”

The phrase entered Vance’s mind and remained there, cold and weightless. Reference. Baseline. Not a man, but a specimen with a label waiting to be printed.

Malkus seemed to sense his unease. “Come. We will show you what your survival makes possible.”

They led him from the cathedral into a gallery of tissue banks. Cylinders climbed from floor to ceiling, their fluid glowing blue beneath inspection lights. Within them floated strips of muscle, glands, eyes, fetal limbs, and pale knots of brain tissue. Each bore a code. Some had dates centuries old. Others had been updated yesterday.

At the end of the gallery stood a room lined with black glass.

“Consciousness archives,” Seraphine said.

Vance saw no shelves, only recessed ports and bundles of cable descending like roots. In the glass, faint human faces appeared and vanished—an old woman opening her mouth, a child turning toward someone beyond the reflection, a man whose eyes had been replaced by static.

“Recorded memories?” Vance asked.

“Continuities,” said Vey. “The distinction is sentimental.”

“You keep people here?”

“We keep what can be kept.”

The station pulsed beneath his feet. Somewhere below, machinery thudded with the slow rhythm of a buried heart.

Imago guided him to a scanning platform. “Your examination must be completed.”

“I’ve already been examined.”

“Not by the Faculty.”

The platform opened a fan of blue light. Vance stood inside it while the machine mapped him from bone to blood. A transparent display unfolded in the air. His skeleton appeared, then his organs, then layers beneath his skin that should not have been there.

Thin scars crossed the base of his skull. Another ringed his abdomen. Tiny geometric marks clustered along his spine, too regular to be accidental.

Vance stared. “Those weren’t in my records.”

“Records decay,” said Seraphine.

“I would remember surgery.”

“Memory decays more gracefully.”

The scan deepened. For an instant, a second pattern flickered through his nervous system: silver threads, minute reservoirs, structures nested in the marrow. Then the display folded shut.

Vance turned on Imago. “What did you do to me?”

The insectoid doctor’s eyes held his. Behind the compound lenses, they looked almost human.

“You need rest,” Imago said.

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No.” A pause. “It is what you need.”

They returned him to a small recovery chamber with a narrow bed and a wall dispenser. Imago adjusted the lines in his arm and dimmed the lights.

“Sleep, Dr. Vance.”

Vance let his eyelids close.

He listened to Imago’s steps fade. He counted breaths, then heartbeats, then the minutes by the changing pressure in the pipes. The nutrient dispenser clicked awake outside his door. A cup filled. The smell came with it, warm and rich and deeply wrong.

Below the floor, voices began to chant.

Not words at first—only a rising murmur, layered and rhythmic. Then he caught fragments.

“Preserve the pattern. Feed the pattern. Return the flesh to—”

The rest dissolved into the machinery’s groan.

Vance opened his eyes in the dark.

The Faculty called themselves scientists. They catalogued organs, measured memories, and spoke of protocols. But science did not chant beneath a hospital. Science did not hide its answers behind words like legacy and baseline.

He lay perfectly still, pretending to sleep while the voices below grew louder.

For the first time since waking, Vance understood that the station was not merely preserving the past.

It was worshipping it.

## Chapter 3: The Ward of the Woken

Vance waited until the chanting stopped.

It ended without conclusion, the voices thinning into the pipes until only the station’s pulse remained. He counted another hundred beats, then sat up. Pain moved through him in small electrical waves. His muscles had been preserved, not strengthened; they belonged to a man who had slept through a millennium and awakened expecting a body that no longer existed.

The door opened when he touched it.

Beyond lay a corridor marked **RECOVERY WARD**. The letters had been painted over several times, but the old red cross showed through like a bruise. Vance took the wheeled support frame beside his bed and pushed himself forward.

The ward was divided by curtains and transparent partitions. Twelve beds stood beneath greenish lamps. Some held patients. Others held restraints, drainage bowls, and folded sheets stiff with old stains. Tubes entered the walls and emerged again with fluids moving through them. The station seemed to be feeding on everyone inside it.

A woman sat in a wheelchair near the last window.

She was perhaps thirty, though illness had drawn her face into an older shape. Her hair had been shaved on one side, exposing a metal stud at her temple. Both hands gripped the wheels. When Vance approached, she startled so violently the chair struck the wall.

“Don’t,” she whispered.

“I’m a patient.”

“That’s why.”

Her eyes flicked toward two masked attendants beside a cart of covered bowls.

“My name is Elias Vance.”

“Mira.” She swallowed. “Mira Vale.”

“Can you tell me where we are?”

“Below. That’s all anyone says. Below the city. Below the dead.”

A slot opened in the wall. An attendant placed a steaming cup inside it. The smell reached Vance: broth, salt, scorched fat, and the sweet undertone he recognized from the Faculty dispenser.

Mira turned away.

“What is it?”

She leaned close. “They serve people.”

Vance looked at the cup. A thin film trembled on its surface. “What?”

“The food. The meat. They say it’s waste. They say nothing is wasted here.” Her fingers tightened around the rims. “I saw them take my brother. He was dead already, they said. They put him in the furnace and gave me soup the next day.”

“Your brother was a patient?”

“He was alive when they took him.”

The ward contracted around them. Behind the curtains, someone began to cough. The cough became a wet rattle and stopped.

Mira stared at him. “Don’t let them make you grateful.”

A soft clicking sounded behind Vance.

Imago stood in the corridor, medical coat folded neatly over his narrow body. His compound eyes reflected the ward lights in dozens of small moons.

“Dr. Vance,” he said. “You should be in bed.”

“What is in the food?”

Imago glanced at the cup. “Nutrient slurry.”

“Made from what?”

“Organic waste. Diseased tissue. Nonviable remains.”

“People.”

The doctor’s mandibles shifted. “The station operates under a closed waste-recycling protocol. The dead consent to nourish the living.”

Mira made a sound of disgust.

“The dead don’t consent,” Vance said.

“Consent is difficult to define after death.”

“And before it?”

Imago did not answer.

That silence was the answer.

Vance looked down the ward. A man beneath the third curtain breathed through a mask. An attendant stood beside him with a tray and syringe. The man’s hand emerged from the sheets, fingers fluttering weakly. The attendant pressed it flat before drawing the needle.

Vance’s clinical mind tried to arrange the facts. Closed system. Biomass recovery. Thermal conversion. Nutrient stability. The language was clean. The practice was not. Somewhere between the two, a human being disappeared.

He turned toward the service corridor.

“Back to your room,” Imago said.

Vance pushed faster.

He had no plan beyond movement. The corridor narrowed around humming filtration units. A maintenance hatch stood ajar beneath a sign faded to three black triangles. He dragged himself from the frame, gripped the ladder, and lowered his feet into darkness.

“Dr. Vance.”

He did not look back.

The shaft smelled of wet metal and fungus. Pipes crowded his shoulders. He descended until the ward sounds faded, then crawled along a grated passage lit by red lamps. Something warm dripped onto his hand.

Below, machinery groaned.

He followed the sound to a chamber with a waist-high railing. A vat occupied most of the room. Its surface rolled beneath gray foam. Hooks hung from an overhead track. Around them lay strips of pale tissue, stripped clean of hair and skin, each piece stamped with a black identification code.

A conveyor carried another load through a hatch.

For one frozen instant Vance saw a human hand among the pieces. A wedding band circled one finger.

His stomach convulsed.

He vomited onto the grate, gripping the rail while warm vapor rose around him. The smell was no longer hidden by broth or spices: opened bodies, iron, disinfectant, and the sweet rot of a place that had made horror routine.

When he lifted his head, Imago stood at the entrance.

The doctor had followed him silently. No weapon showed in his hands. That made it worse.

“How long?” Vance rasped.

“Since you left the ward.”

“You watched me.”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you stop me?”

Imago looked toward the vat. The fine limbs at his sides had drawn tight against his coat.

“I wanted you to see it.”

“So you could call it medicine?”

“No.”

The word was quiet. Almost human.

Vance backed away, one hand pressed to his stomach. “Then what do you call it?”

Imago’s eyes held the red reflection of the lamps.

“I am sorry.”

There was no ritual in it. No qualification. His voice broke around the words as if they had edges.

Vance hated him for that more than he would have hated a lie.

Imago extended one hand. “Come back before the ward notices you’re gone.”

Vance stared at the claws, then at the face behind them. The creature had awakened him. It had lied by omission. It had followed him into the dark and allowed the truth to speak for itself.

He took the hand.

Not because he trusted Imago. Because his legs could no longer carry him.

As they climbed, Vance looked once over his shoulder at the vat. The black codes marched along the tissue like labels in a library. Nothing here was discarded. Nothing was mourned. Bodies became nourishment, memories became archives, and suffering became procedure.

He had dreamed of returning to the future as a physician, a witness, perhaps even a man with work left to do.

The past he wanted was not waiting beneath the city.

It had been rendered down, sterilized, and served warm.

## Chapter 4: The Records Beneath

Imago left him with water and an empty basin.

“Small sips,” he said. “I will return.”

Vance watched the door close. The apology had changed nothing: the lock still belonged to Imago, the water came through their pipes, and somewhere below, a man's wedding ring was turning in a vat.

He drank anyway. His body insisted on living without first establishing terms.

Beyond the partition, a servitor struggled with a jammed linen trolley. It had once been human. Now its back was bent beneath a shell of instruments, and its wrists ended in clamps. A flat access wafer hung from its coat on a retractable cord. Vance had watched it touch the wafer to the ward doors.

He pushed his frame into the corridor.

“The wheel,” he said. “There's cloth caught underneath.”

The servitor lowered itself with a sound like a folding umbrella. While it worried at the axle, Vance caught the dangling wafer. His fingers almost failed him. He pinched the cord against the trolley and pulled until its frayed end parted.

The servitor kept working.

He walked away slowly. Running would have been impossible; looking innocent was almost as difficult.

At the end of the ward, a sealed door bore the word RECORDS beneath a crust of pale fungus. The wafer woke a green light. Beyond it, stairs descended around a motionless lift.

He left the frame at the top and went down holding both rails.

The datacore occupied a low room where roots had entered through the ceiling and died in the warmth. Cabinets stood ankle-deep in black water. Most were dark. One still breathed through a grille, its fan ticking against something caught inside.

Vance eased himself onto a stool before its terminal. An old keyboard lay beneath a newer membrane, the letters visible where the covering had split. He knew that arrangement of keys. The recognition hurt more than the unfamiliar machines upstairs.

The wafer opened a maintenance index.

He typed his name.

For several seconds nothing happened. Then a single entry appeared.

PALINGENESIS: VANCE, ELIAS.

He remembered the word from some forgotten lecture: coming into being again. Rebirth.

Beneath it was a photograph of himself before freezing. He wore hospital blue and looked irritated at being photographed. There was a small nick on his chin from shaving. He remembered that morning. He had been late, and someone had laughed at him in a corridor flooded with sun.

The screen assigned that man a number.

He opened the file.

LEGACY LINEAGE: SELECTIVE BREEDING OUTCOME.

“No,” he said.

The room returned the word in the fan's broken rhythm.

A branching pedigree filled the display. At first he mistook it for a family history. Then he saw the approval marks beside certain pairings, the rejected branches, the clinical notes attached to births. The dates preceded his own birth by generations. His parents' names sat beside an authorization code.

He remembered his mother telling him he had been wanted.

Below the pedigree, a different chronology began at cryogenic admission. Preservation intervals. Compatibility assessments. Maintenance interventions. His thousand years of sleep reduced to columns, some entries corrupted, others sealed behind permissions the stolen wafer could not supply.

He pressed two fingers against the scar beneath his collar. The file would not tell him what had been done there.

A summary remained accessible.

SELECTION BASIS: GENOMIC CONTINUITY; NEURAL ARCHITECTURE; RETENTION CAPACITY.

DESIGNATED PURPOSE: VIABLE COGNITIVE SUBSTRATE.

He read the final phrase again. A substrate was something upon which something else could grow.

There was no mention of curing him.

He opened an adjacent register. Earlier subjects appeared in rows, each with an awakening date and a chain of completed procedures.

Revival. Reassurance. Nutritional restoration. Mapping.

The next column was headed SYNAPSIS CHAMBER.

He selected a woman whose photograph showed her smiling reluctantly at the camera. The report recorded distress upon revival, followed by successful rapport formation. She had asked whether her daughter might have descendants. An attendant had been instructed to preserve that possibility during acclimatization.

TRANSFER FOR EXTRACTION: COMPLETE.

SUBJECT SURVIVAL: NEGATIVE.

He opened another. A young man. Then an older woman. Then a patient whose photograph had decayed into colored squares.

Every accessible record ended the same way.

One contained a note in a different hand: Excess sedation compromises yield. Anticipatory engagement preferred.

Vance thought of Imago saying welcome. Of the careful tour, the promised rest, the water placed beside his bed. They wanted him awake. They wanted him interested in tomorrow.

He searched for Mira Vale. A restricted entry appeared, but he had no time to open it.

“You should not be standing without assistance.”

The voice came from behind him.

Vey occupied the doorway, long surgical arms folded close to his body. Water darkened the hem of his coat. Vance had heard neither the stairs nor the door.

He closed the file. The screen returned to his photograph.

“Substrate for what?”

Vey looked past him at the untroubled face on the display.

“You have exhausted yourself.”

“Those people died.”

“Yes.”

“You told me I was a guest.”

The Surgeon's mouthparts separated with a small, precise click.

“You were never a guest; you were a vintage.”

Vance gripped the keyboard and tried to stand. The stool scraped backward. His legs held for one instant, long enough for him to turn toward the stairs, before Vey caught his shoulder.

A needle hissed against his neck.

He struck at the arm. His hand landed without force, a patient's involuntary protest. Warmth spread beneath his jaw and became a weight inside his tongue.

“Imago,” he tried to say, though he did not know whether it was an accusation or a request.

Vey lowered him carefully to the floor, protecting his head from the cabinet edge.

That care was the worst of it.

Above him, the terminal still showed the man with the shaving cut. Vance tried to hold on to that small, useless wound, something he had done to himself on an ordinary morning.

But the face blurred. He was livestock, tended across centuries, brought gently into the light when the appetite was ready.

Even hope had been fed to him as a flavoring.

## Chapter 5: The Council of Hunger

He woke to the sound of someone sharpening a knife. For several breaths he could not place it. Then a figure above him turned its head, and he understood: not steel against stone, but mouthparts rubbing together.

A strap crossed his forehead. Others pinned his wrists, thighs, and ankles to a surgical slab. His gray shirt had been opened down the chest. Cold moisture gathered beneath his shoulder blades, and when he tried to swallow, something adhesive pulled at his throat.

He stopped moving.

The room rose around him in concentric tiers. Drainage channels divided the floor into wedges; above them, the Faculty stood behind a circular rail. Their shells caught the lamplight in patches of amber and diseased green. Some wore gowns with the sleeves pinned empty. Others had fitted instruments into the spaces where their hands had been. Their low conversation filled the theater with a dry, continuous buzzing.

Vey stood beside a trolley. On it lay a transparent hood threaded with filaments finer than hair.

Vance looked away too quickly. Vey noticed.

“Not yet,” the Surgeon said.

At the highest point of the circle, Malkus leaned forward. One side of his heavy carapace had been polished; the other bore a gray eruption like salt. Seraphine stood below him, her narrow fingers resting on a tablet.

“Dr. Vance. Your unauthorized excursion has made further reassurance inappropriate.”

Vance tested his right wrist. The strap tightened rather than yielding.

“What goes into the substrate?”

“Nothing,” said Malkus. “What remains usable is drawn out.”

The buzzing subsided.

“We call the procedure Sublimation.”

Vey lifted the hood. Its filaments drifted in the theater's damp air, each tipped with a tiny dark bead. Vance remembered the geometric points in his scan.

“The neural lace establishes continuity with your living architecture,” Seraphine said. “Your cognitive organization will support renewal of ours. Memory, associative flexibility, the capacity to retain a coherent self across alteration. Those qualities have become scarce.”

“You harvest the brain.”

“We harvest what the brain permits.”

“And I die.”

Seraphine's fingers paused over the tablet.

“Your independent activity ends.”

Vance pulled against every restraint at once. The slab rang beneath him. Pain spread across his chest, sharp enough to clear the last softness of the tranquilizer. A thin sound escaped him before he could stop it.

No one moved to comfort him.

Malkus raised a hand. Its outermost finger trembled, tapping against the rail. He caught it with his other hand and held it still.

“You have seen our adaptations. You have not understood their cost. Tissue can be renewed while the person inhabiting it diminishes. We lose sequences. Names. The meaning of instruments we ourselves designed.”

A Faculty member on the lower tier clicked three times, with terrible regularity. Nobody looked toward it.

“Our alterations preserved us through famine, radiation, burial,” Malkus continued. “They did not preserve us intact. We require an unmutated mind. An organizing inheritance uncontaminated by our successive transformations.”

“My mind isn't medicine.”

“It is precisely medicine.”

Seraphine stepped nearer. A pale membrane showed through a split beside her jaw. As she spoke it inflated, then sank.

“An ultimate medical sacrifice. One life sustaining the accumulated knowledge of a civilization. Transubstantiation, if you prefer the old language.”

“I don't.”

“You were a physician.”

“I still am.”

“Then consider the patients who will die if we fail.”

He saw Mira's hands gripping her wheelchair. The ring turning in the vat. His own photograph above a list of fatal procedures.

“Do those patients know what you feed them?”

A ripple passed around the rail. Hunger had a smell here: sweet broth beneath disinfectant, trapped in the folds of their gowns.

“Necessity does not become innocent because you rename it,” he said.

“Nor does refusal become ethical because it is frightened,” Seraphine answered.

At the foot of the slab, a smaller figure shifted. Vance had not seen Imago standing behind the instrument trolley. His coat was buttoned incorrectly. One long hand rested on its edge.

“There is no consent,” Imago said.

Malkus turned his head.

“The subject is distressed. His judgment—”

“No.” Imago's voice barely carried. “Without consent, this is murder.”

The word seemed to leave him physically depleted. His shoulders folded inward, yet he did not lower his eyes.

For one reckless instant, Vance believed someone would undo the straps.

“Your custodial attachment has become an impairment,” Malkus said. “You will remain available to the Surgeon. You will not address this council again.”

Imago's hand slipped from the trolley.

“Proceed at the next maintenance bell,” Malkus told Vey. “Allow the sedative to clear. We need his faculties intact.”

Vance stared at Imago. The creature did not step forward. Objection, then obedience: Vance could not live on the difference between them.

“Where will you keep me?” he asked.

Vey looked down, apparently surprised by the question.

“In preparation.”

“I'm cold.”

That was true. It was also something a patient might say.

While Vey fetched a covering, Vance watched the theater. Seraphine's tablet opened the inner door. An attendant waited for her to pass rather than using a credential of its own. A ceiling lamp dimmed whenever the pump beneath the slab engaged. The Faculty needed power, permissions, obedient hands. They needed him frightened but lucid, living but compliant.

His teeth chattered. He let them.

The stolen wafer was gone from his pocket; he could feel the flattened lining beneath his bound hip. No weapon. No ally he could trust. Very little strength.

Vey spread a sheet across him and checked the circulation in his fingers with exquisite care.

Vance concentrated on that touch until he could bear it without flinching. He had spent his life finding failures in living systems. A blocked passage. A dependency. Something small admitted where it did not belong.

The Faculty had made a place for him inside their defenses.

He could not survive by thinking like their patient.

Beneath the sheet, still shaking, he began to think like a disease.

## Chapter 6: Obolus's Testament

The cell had no corners.

Vey had called it preparation when the attendants unstrapped Vance in the theater and marched him here. They needed his mind clear before the next maintenance bell; apparently terror did not count as an impairment.

Its walls curved inward beneath a skin of sweating polymer, making Vance feel as though he had been placed inside a throat. A drain interrupted the floor beneath the slab. Every few minutes it gulped, and something warm moved through the pipes below.

He had stopped counting the maintenance bells. The sedative had withdrawn from his blood, leaving tremors in its place. His wrists were bruised where the restraints had bitten him. On the other side of the door, the Faculty kept watch through a black lens. He had tried silence, pleading, and the cold precision of questions. None had altered the red light above the lock.

The light went out.

Vance sat up.

A card whispered through the reader. The door opened only far enough for an old creature to enter sideways.

He wore the remnants of a Faculty coat, its collar fused to a carapace split down the breastbone. Fluid leaked from the fissure in slow, pearlescent threads and gathered at his abdomen before dripping onto the floor. His head was nearly human, except for the transparent plates over his cheeks and the dark, wet eyes clustered at his temples.

“Curator Obolus,” he said. His voice rasped as if each word crossed a bed of sand. “Do not stand. They will measure the effort.”

“Who sent you?”

“No one sends me anymore.” Obolus lowered himself against the wall. His shell clicked when he breathed. “That is one advantage of dying.”

Vance stared at the fluid. “You’re dying.”

“I have been dying since the third century of the project. The timetable became imprecise.”

He reached into his coat. Vance recoiled, expecting a needle, but Obolus produced a narrow access card and a glass vial no longer than a finger. The liquid within was cloudy amber. Dark threads drifted through it, contracting when the cell’s overhead light flickered.

“The card opens service passages,” Obolus said. “Not the public corridors. The old ones. The station has forgotten them, which makes them safer than doors watched by people.”

Vance did not take either object. “Why help me?”

Obolus’s clustered eyes focused one after another. “Because I remember the oath.”

“What oath?”

“First, do no harm.” A bitter click escaped him. “We said it before we learned to make harm efficient. Before we called a stomach a bioreactor and a mind a renewable resource.”

He extended the card. Vance took it. It was warm, almost alive.

“Malkus says you need an unmutated mind,” Obolus continued. “That is a lie useful enough to become doctrine. Your mind is not untouched. Neither is your body.”

The scan returned to Vance with sudden force: geometric shadows along his skull, the little structures beside his spine, Imago’s refusal to explain. “They modified me after I was frozen.”

“During. Between. Around.” Obolus’s leaking fingers trembled. “Palingenesis was never preservation. It was cultivation.”

He tapped the vial against his palm.

“Every generation adjusted you. Hormonal suppressors to slow decay. Neural conditioning to make waking less traumatic, and compliance more probable. Microchimeric grafts to teach your immune system what to tolerate. They fed engineered cells into you while you slept. Some died. Some remained. You carry descendants of the Faculty in your marrow.”

Vance’s mouth went dry. “I’m contaminated.”

“You are curated.”

“That is a prettier word for the same violation.”

“Yes.” Obolus looked almost relieved. “You understand quickly.”

The cell seemed to contract around Vance. He remembered his hands before the thaw: clean, capable, belonging to a physician who had chosen his work. But choice now appeared to him as another preserved artifact, a label pasted over a specimen jar. His parents’ names in the pedigree. The scars no one explained. The body that had survived when all the others had become bones.

He had wanted to be the last man from his world. They had not saved a man. They had maintained an instrument.

“What is in the vial?” he asked.

“An ancestral metamorphic strain. Wild stock. We abandoned it because it does not obey the thresholds.”

“Mutation.”

“Adaptation, if you survive. Dissolution, if you do not.”

Vance looked at the amber threads. They curled against the glass as though they could smell him.

“Why give me this?”

“Because the controlled strains recognize the Pattern’s commands. This one predates the commands. It may alter you beyond their ability to read or digest.” Obolus leaned forward, and a fresh line of fluid escaped his shell. “You asked why you were chosen. This is why. They believe they can reduce any life to a function. Prove them wrong.”

“You’re asking me to become one of them.”

“No.” His eyes tightened. “I am asking you to become something they cannot classify.”

The door’s black lens glimmered. A distant mechanism began to wind. Preparation, perhaps. Or merely another machine remembering its duty.

“Why not use it yourself?” Vance asked.

Obolus glanced down at his fractured carapace. “I am already an argument against uncontrolled change.”

He placed his palm flat on the floor. A dark smear spread beneath it.

“The Pattern,” Vance said. “What is it?”

For the first time, Obolus looked afraid. “A promise made from stolen minds. They joined memory to memory until they could no longer tell preservation from possession. It is beneath the station. It dreams through the walls.”

“And you helped build it.”

“I helped name the steps. Others supplied the hunger.”

The mechanism outside clicked closer. Vance closed his fingers around the vial. Its chill entered his hand.

Obolus pushed himself upright. He swayed, then steadied against the wall. “There is a maintenance shaft behind the old nutrient manifold. The card will open its lock. Do not go toward the chanting. Do not let them attach the lace.”

“You’re coming with me.”

A broken laugh shook his chest. “No. I have spent too long being carried by the project.”

He reached the door and paused without turning.

“Destroy the Pattern,” he whispered. “And when you can, let me die.”

The lock began to cycle.

Obolus slipped through the opening. The door sealed behind him, leaving Vance alone with the card, the vial, and the terrible possibility that freedom might require him to abandon the last human shape he recognized.

He pressed the glass to his sternum.

Under his skin, something answered.

## Chapter 7: The Extraction

The service card opened a door whose hinges had grown into the wall.

Vance pulled until the fungal crust broke. Beyond lay a ventilation passage scarcely wider than his shoulders. He entered sideways, holding the door to prevent its closing clang, then crawled into warm darkness.

The vial knocked against his ribs. Once he stopped and pressed his palm over it, absurdly afraid the sound would carry. Obolus had given him the means to become unrecognizable. For now he needed his hands to remain hands.

Below the passage, machinery hummed in uneven intervals. Each time the deeper note began, the duct lights dimmed. He remembered the surgical theater: the lamp faltering when the pump engaged. Whatever the Faculty claimed to have perfected, they still depended on failing wires.

He followed the sound downhill.

Through a grille he saw a chamber sunk beneath the theater. SYNAPSIS remained legible above its entrance. Cables hung from the ceiling like abandoned rigging, converging on a suspended ring. Underneath lay a black cradle.

Mira was in it.

For a moment he could not reconcile her with the woman in the wheelchair. Her head had been fixed between padded clamps. Needles stood along her throat and shoulders; finer threads disappeared beneath a translucent cap against her scalp. Her hands strained against straps without lifting.

Vey stood beside her. Seraphine watched from a platform above a transparent floor.

“Calibration requires recognition,” the Archivist said.

Vey bent toward the cradle. “Tell us who you are.”

“Mira Vale.” Her voice trembled. “You said this was a scan.”

“It is,” Vey said.

Beneath them something moved.

Vance had taken it for folded machinery. Now a swelling pressed against the glass: gray tissue threaded with amber light, convolutions piled into an immense irregular brain. Cables descended into it. Some had become indistinguishable from nerves. Fluid shifted between its lobes, and pale membranes tightened as though listening.

The Pattern.

Obolus’s stolen minds had a body.

“Tell us who you are,” Vey repeated.

“Mira Vale.”

From beneath the floor, faintly amplified, another voice answered with her.

Vance bit the inside of his cheek. Seraphine made a notation.

“The legacy subject’s conditioning remains unique,” she said. “Do not exhaust the receiving pathways on calibration.”

“Her transfer will finish before his preparation,” Vey replied.

They were not substituting her for him. She was a test before his procedure, another patient spent to make his death more efficient.

Vance eased the grille loose. Beyond it a maintenance ledge ran above a power cabinet. He lowered himself onto it, his knees shaking. Through the cabinet’s inspection window he could see an old emergency bridge, sealed behind cracked insulation. A service schematic marked it as a bypass for the chamber’s load regulator.

He looked down at Mira.

“Tell us who you are.”

Her mouth opened. No answer came.

He opened the cabinet with Obolus’s card and forced the bypass handle past its corroded stop.

The cabinet flashed. A brutal concussion threw him against the ledge rail.

Below, the suspended ring jerked sideways. Lights burst along its circumference. Vey stumbled back, shielding his eyes, and Seraphine’s tablet struck the platform. In the sudden darkness, the Pattern shone through the floor.

Vance dropped onto the stairs and ran down.

Backup lamps kindled red. A mechanical voice announced isolation, then recovery. Across the chamber a barrier was descending over the Pattern’s primary coupling, exposed by a ruptured panel. A heavy release lever stood inside the narrowing gap.

He could reach it. Disconnect the mass before its systems recovered. Perhaps finish what Obolus had asked.

Mira made a choking sound.

The ring had twisted her scalp leads taut. Blood ran behind her ear. Her chest rose, stopped, struggled to rise again.

Vance turned toward her.

He heard the barrier close behind him as he reached the cradle.

There was no time to undo everything carefully. He released the clamps, broke the strap catches, and pulled the needles from her shoulders. One had bent. He steadied his hand and eased it back along its entry path while the alarms counted down the recovery sequence.

On the cradle display a branching image flickered beside the words TRANSFER INCOMPLETE. Some branches were dark. Others glowed with the same amber light as the mass beneath him.

“Mira. Can you hear me?”

Her lips moved.

He freed the scalp connection last. The display went blank. Under the glass, a luminous region continued pulsing after her leads were disconnected.

Something of her remained down there.

He lifted her. Her eyes opened and fixed on his face.

“Elias.”

“Yes. Hold on to me.”

“Tell us who you are,” she said.

“Mira?”

“Tell us who you are.”

There was no question in it. He shifted her weight, trying not to hear the empty precision of her voice.

The entrance opened. Chitinous drones crowded through, jointed bodies scraping the frame. Behind them came Imago, carrying a restraint loop.

He saw Vance and moved at once to the service exit.

“Seal the upper access,” he told the drones. “Keep him away from the stairs.”

Vance stopped. He had imagined, foolishly, that the creature’s objection in the theater had changed something.

“Move.”

“Put her back,” Imago said. “The connection must be restored.”

“You know what it does.”

“I know what happens when an extraction is interrupted.”

He advanced, opening the loop. A drone slipped beside him and struck with a hooked restraint. Vance twisted to shield Mira. The hook opened his upper arm; warmth flooded his sleeve. He kicked against the drone’s narrow front limb and felt it buckle.

Imago reached for Mira’s head.

Her eyes turned toward him.

“Tell us who you are,” she said softly.

His hand stopped.

For a moment nothing moved except the restraint loop, trembling between his fingers. He looked at her bloodied scalp, then at her eyes.

“She knew her name,” Vance said.

Behind them Vey shouted for the doors to close.

Imago did not step aside. But he failed to finish reaching, and the gap between his arm and the wall was just wide enough.

Vance drove through it shoulder first. Pain tore along his side as a drone caught him. Cloth ripped. He stumbled into the passage, struck the release plate with his elbow, and kept moving as the hatch ground shut behind him.

Mira’s head rested against his neck. Her breath was warm and regular. He counted it because it was the only evidence he could bear to trust.

At the junction ahead, a sign pointed toward fungal circulation pumps. He turned beneath it, leaving drops of blood on the floor.

Imago had tried to take her back. Vance would not forget that.

Yet he had stopped when she spoke. Somewhere inside the obedient anatomy was a faculty the Pattern had not entirely consumed. Vance did not know whether it was shame, or memory, or merely fear of seeing his own future.

Carrying Mira into the wet darkness, he needed it to be more.

## Chapter 8: Mutation

The pump-room door closed against Mira’s trailing foot. Vance pulled her clear and shoved it shut again. This time the latch caught.

Beyond it, something scraped metal with slow, deliberate strokes.

He lowered her onto a shelf of dry fungal felt beside the circulation pumps. Four immense bladders swelled in the darkness, forcing cloudy fluid through translucent pipes. Their contractions stirred a smell of wet plaster and spoiled meat. Every third beat, the smallest bladder faltered.

There was no second door. An inspection grille high on the opposite wall offered an opening barely large enough for his shoulders, secured by bolts he had no tools to turn.

“Mira.”

Her head rolled toward his voice. Her eyes remained shut. He found a pulse beneath her jaw, steady enough to make him grateful and ashamed of how little gratitude required now.

“I need a minute.”

He pressed a strip torn from his shirt against her bleeding scalp. His injured arm shook. Blood from his side had soaked the waistband of his trousers; when he stood, the room narrowed to a white point.

Outside, a voice said, “The latch is seated. Bring the cutter.”

Not Imago. Not anyone who might hesitate.

Vance searched the pump-room cupboards. Sealing paste. Brittle gloves. A maintenance dressing packet, and beneath it an old medical injector in a cracked case. He held it under the green emergency lamp. Its indicator still shone.

Then he took out Obolus’s vial.

The amber threads gathered against the glass nearest his fingers.

Adaptation, if you survive.

He had spent his working life asking patients to accept uncertainty. He remembered sitting beside a woman after an unsuccessful operation, explaining that another intervention might help. She had watched his hands rather than his face. He understood that now. Hands were what carried the promises out.

His own were slippery with Mira’s blood.

He fitted the vial into the injector. For a moment he could not press it to his skin. He imagined giving it to Imago instead, bargaining with Vey, doing anything that would leave this last boundary intact.

A cutting wheel screamed against the door.

“No,” he said, without knowing which possibility he refused.

He drove the injector against his thigh.

At first there was only cold.

Then his teeth struck together so hard he thought they had broken.

He fell beside the shelf. The injector clattered away. Pain opened beneath his ribs and spread with a terrible deliberation, touching each old surgical scar as though checking an inventory. He tried to breathe quietly. The breath became a cry.

Something moved inside his injured arm.

He clamped his other hand over it, absurdly trying to hold his body in place. The wound’s edges rose. Black points pushed through the skin, not closing it but parting it further, then spreading into overlapping plates. Blood welled between them. A ridge grew across his wrist while he watched.

He knew the names of the tissues tearing. Naming them did nothing.

The cutting wheel stopped.

“Listen.”

Vance bit his sleeve. His spine arched against the floor. Pressure filled his skull, concentrated beside the eyes, until he was certain the bone must split. Two wet depressions opened at his temples. Air entered them and became something other than air.

The room struck him all at once.

Hot iron. Fear-salt. The sour trace of his blood. Mira’s breath, frail and distinct. Beneath these came an immense tangle of signals from the walls: thirst, blockage, pressure, injury. He gagged at their intimacy.

The pumps were calling for repair.

He could not have said how he knew. Their demand arrived with the certainty of a full bladder or a cramped muscle. Fine threads beneath the wall membrane tightened when he turned his head. A bitter scent rose from his own skin, and the nearest pump changed its rhythm.

A lattice, he thought. Chemical signals crossing through flesh and air. The Faculty had built themselves a nervous system to live inside.

Now it had another listener.

*Wound-close. Brood-still. Bitter-near.*

The words were his attempt to understand something that had no words. It arranged the room by need and proximity. Mira was warm, helpless, to be covered. The figures outside were competing bodies. Beyond them waited a pressure so vast that his new senses recoiled.

*Center. Feed. Return.*

“No.”

His voice rasped. He held a memory with all the strength he had: rain on the hospital windows, his own name on a locker, coffee gone cold beside a stack of charts.

Elias Vance. Physician.

He remembered Mira telling him about her brother. Obolus leaking onto the cell floor. Imago raising a restraint loop across the escape route.

The memories remained. So did the pain. He lay among them until he could move.

At the door, the cutter began again.

Vance grasped an abandoned valve handle to pull himself upright. It bent in his hand. He stared at the curved metal, then released it carefully.

His side no longer bled freely. A hard seam covered the wound, pulling with every breath. Relief frightened him almost as much as the injury had.

He crossed to the grille. His fingertips found purchase beneath its rim. The bolts held for one tug, then tore through the rotten frame with a noise that made Mira stir.

Behind the opening, a service duct sloped toward the deeper station.

From below came a pulse.

Not sound: an instruction carried through the living walls. For an instant he felt many hungers leaning in the same direction. Beneath them flickered something smaller, a rhythm he recognized from the amber glow under the Synapsis floor.

Mira? He could not tell. Wanting her there might be enough to make him hear her.

He had studied infection as an invasion of a body’s dependencies. Here was a body that had made everything dependent upon it. He could enter it now.

The thought brought no triumph.

He knelt beside Mira and tucked the card into his remaining pocket. Her eyes opened briefly, looking through him.

“I’ll find what they took,” he told her. “Whatever is left. I won’t leave you in there.”

He reached to lift her, then stopped.

His fingers had lengthened. The nails were thickening into dark hooks; the joints no longer closed quite as he expected. He tried to lay his palm flat against her cheek and could not.

Behind him, the door began to give.

Vance wrapped his ruined sleeve around the claws before he touched her.

## Chapter 9: The Hive Collapse

The duct opened into the station’s deeper body.

Vance carried Mira against his chest, one arm beneath her knees, the other crooked protectively around her shoulders. His claws had punctured the sleeve he had wrapped around them, but not her skin. That small success mattered. He held to it while the walls breathed.

The passage was not built so much as cultivated. Fungal membranes glazed the concrete. Veins of translucent tubing ran beneath them, twitching whenever Vance passed. His new senses tasted the facility through its chemical exhalations: alarm, pressure, a sharp electrical panic rising from below.

Behind him, the pump-room door burst inward.

He lowered Mira beside a maintenance manifold and turned. Two chitinous drones crowded the opening, their lamp-eyes sweeping the duct. Vance released the bitter scent he had learned to summon from his altered glands.

The drones stopped.

For one breath, the station recognized him as kin.

Then a command struck through the walls: seize, return, preserve.

Vance lunged before the command settled. His plated shoulder smashed the first drone into the frame. His claws found the seam beneath its jaw and tore. Pale fluid sprayed his face, hot and sweet. The second struck him across the ribs. He folded around the blow, heard something crack, and answered by driving both feet into its abdomen. It went backward through the broken door.

He did not wait to see whether it rose.

The manifold beside Mira bore three ancient symbols: WARD, NUTRIENT, DORMITORY. His fingers hovered over the valves. The pipes trembled with their contents, and his sensory pits read the flow as hunger.

He turned WARD first.

Somewhere above, locks disengaged.

He turned DORMITORY.

A distant chorus began—not the chanting of the Faculty, but human voices waking in terror. Metal doors slammed open. Feet struck flooring. Someone screamed for help, and then many people screamed.

Vance opened NUTRIENT last.

The vats below the ward convulsed. Their pressure surged into the failing grid. Across the station, lights flashed white and died. Pumps shrieked. A smell of hot wiring and rancid broth rolled through the duct.

The facility’s living architecture recoiled from him.

Good, he thought. Be afraid.

He lifted Mira again and followed the strongest signal downward. It led toward the Synapsis Chamber, but beneath that direction pulsed something else: an amber rhythm, broken and familiar.

Mira’s mind—or the memory of it—was still there.

The chamber doors stood open when he reached them. Emergency lights strobed over the circular floor. Seraphine stood on the platform above the Pattern, her many-jointed hands buried in a console. Malkus faced her, one arm linked to a crown of neural cables. Between them, the Pattern shone through its glass enclosure.

Vance had imagined an engine. He had imagined circuitry, a machine with clean borders.

The Pattern was a continent of brains.

Cerebral tissue had been layered and fused into a pale, glistening mass, every fold webbed with black lace. Faces surfaced beneath the membrane and sank again. A child’s mouth formed a word without sound. An old man opened eyes that had no sockets. Hundreds of voices moved through the chamber as one wet thought.

Mira’s voice was among them.

*Calibration subject. State your name.*

Vance nearly dropped her.

“No,” he whispered.

Malkus turned. The provost’s carapace was split along one side, revealing luminous tissue that repaired itself even as he watched. “You arrived ahead of schedule.”

“You put her in there.”

“We preserved her.” Seraphine’s fingers moved faster over the console. “Her identity is unstable, but useful. Yours will be cleaner.”

The neural-lace crown tightened around Malkus’s skull. His mouth opened in bliss or pain. “Come, Vance. The Faculty can still make you eternal.”

Vance set Mira behind a fallen equipment housing. “You call that living?”

The Pattern answered for them. A hundred mouths inhaled.

*Elias. Elias. Elias.*

Within the repetition, he felt Mira—not whole, not safe, but reaching. A fragment of terror. A memory of rain. Her brother’s name. Then the calibration prompt closed over her again.

Vance ran.

Seraphine sent the cables toward him like striking vines. He cut the first with one claw, ducked the second, and seized the third. Its current flooded his arm. Every stolen mind flashed through him: patients begging, technicians reciting protocols, children asking when their mothers would return. The Pattern tried to pour him into its common hunger.

He screamed and pulled.

The lace snapped.

Malkus crossed the platform with impossible speed. His hand struck Vance’s chest and drove him into the glass. Plates cracked. The provost’s ruined side knitted closed, muscle blooming beneath the carapace.

“You mistake damage for freedom,” Malkus hissed. “We endured a thousand years.”

“You consumed a thousand years.”

Malkus raised his arm. The claws at its end unfolded, aimed at Vance’s throat.

A smaller body slammed into him.

Imago struck from the side and was crushed beneath the provost’s elbow. His carapace broke with a sound like crockery. He fell across Vance, shielding him from the descending claws.

Malkus tore free. Imago remained pinned, fluid leaking in dark sheets.

His compound eyes found Vance’s.

“I was a doctor once too,” he murmured.

Then he pushed the access rod from his hand across the floor.

Vance understood. He snatched it and drove it into the Pattern’s central housing.

The amber node beneath the glass split open.

For an instant, Mira looked at him from everywhere.

*Don’t let me wake there,* she said—not in words, but in the shape of a remembered hand closing around his.

Vance plunged both claws into the opening.

The core resisted like a living heart. Voices tore at his memories. He felt his childhood peeled from him, his medical training, his name. He held the rain, the locker, the cold coffee. He held Mira’s face as it had been before the chamber.

Then he tore.

The Pattern’s scream had no source. It emerged from every pipe, every vat, every throat in the station. Fluid erupted through the floor. The glass enclosure burst outward, and the cerebral mass collapsed in on itself, its stolen faces dissolving into gray slurry.

Malkus staggered as the regenerative light inside him went dark.

Seraphine vanished beneath a falling section of platform.

Around Vance, the station hemorrhaged. Nutrient lines split. Fungal walls blackened. The lights died in rings, and something enormous below began to beat more slowly.

He crawled to Mira. She was alive, but limp, her lashes wet with blood or condensation. He gathered her up as the chamber tilted.

Behind him, Imago made no sound.

Vance carried Mira toward the emergency escape shaft while the immortal engine died screaming beneath them. He did not look back. If he looked back, he might hear every mind he had failed to save.

At the shaft mouth, a final pulse moved through the walls.

Not the Pattern’s command. Not hunger.

A faint, human rhythm answered from somewhere inside Mira.

Vance climbed toward it.

## Chapter 10: Emergence

The shaft ended in light.

Not clean light. Dawn seeped through the crack above as a gray, jaundiced radiance, filtered by dust and the smoke of a city that had been burning for centuries in slow, buried ways. Vance saw it between the plates of his forearms as he climbed: a narrow wound in the earth, widening toward the sky.

Mira hung against his chest, wrapped in his torn coat. Her weight had become an equation he solved with claws and leverage. One arm hooked beneath her knees; the other braced against the shaft wall. Every movement drove pain through his cracked chest plates. Beneath them, the old human ribs still rose and fell. The new tissues around them flexed independently, tasting the air.

Dust. Rust. Rain trapped in concrete. Fungal bloom. Mira's blood, faint and metallic.

And beneath everything, the aftersound.

The Pattern had not ended when its core tore. It had screamed through him—not with a voice, but with a billion collapsing recognitions. Faces had flashed behind his eyes. A child reciting a multiplication table. An old woman praying in a language he did not know. Someone laughing while a surgeon opened his skull. Mira, again and again, asking not to wake there.

Now the voices were quiet. They had not vanished. They lay in the chambers of his mind like drowned instruments, strings still trembling when he moved.

“Stay with me,” he told Mira.

Her head rolled against his shoulder. The dressing at her temple was black with blood. Her lashes did not stir.

He stopped beneath the final break in the shaft. Above him, a slab of roadway had split open, its underside webbed with roots and cables. Morning wind descended through it, cold enough to make his sensory pits contract.

For one irrational instant, Vance expected the old world to be waiting: an ambulance siren, a hospital roof, a nurse shouting for oxygen. He imagined stepping into fluorescent light with a chart in his hand and a name badge on his chest. Dr. Elias Vance. Internal medicine. Human being.

Then he climbed out.

The megacity sprawled beneath the dawn like a skeleton picked clean. Towers leaned against one another, their windows empty, their steel frames exposed. Elevated roads had fallen into tangled ravines. A transit spine rose from the rubble and stopped in midair, leading nowhere. Between the ruins, pale grasses and fungal fans crowded the streets. Far off, something moved through a collapsed plaza, too large and deliberate to be a dog.

The surface was not salvation. It was simply a larger room.

Vance lowered Mira onto a shelf of broken concrete. His hands shook as he examined her. The wounds from the extraction were ugly but closed. Her pulse was weak. He pressed two claws against her throat, then adjusted them until the sharpened tips did not pierce skin.

There.

A beat. A pause. Another beat.

Not the mechanical rhythm of a pump. Not the synchronized pulse that had answered from the Pattern. This was irregular, fragile, and entirely hers—or near enough to hers that he could not abandon it.

“Mira?”

Her mouth opened.

“Calibration…” she whispered.

The word passed through him like ice. Her eyes remained shut.

“No,” he said. “Not calibration. You’re outside. You’re alive.”

Her fingers twitched once, closing on nothing.

He searched the rubble for anything useful and found a medical bag half buried beneath a fallen sign. Its leather had split; the white emblem was stained brown. Inside were two empty ampoules, a cracked pressure cuff, gauze fused into a hard brick, and a pair of blunt surgical scissors.

He held the bag for a long time.

It was absurdly light. It smelled of mold and old antiseptic. Still, he slung it over one shoulder. The gesture belonged to the man he had been. A doctor carried instruments. A doctor assessed injuries. A doctor did not hear the dead whispering through his nerves or grow a second set of sensory organs beneath his skin.

His reflection trembled in a shard of black glass nearby.

The face was mostly his. The jaw had narrowed beneath dark plates. Fine apertures opened and closed along his temples. Chitin climbed his throat in overlapping ridges, and his eyes caught the dawn with a wet, many-pointed gleam. His hands ended in hooked black claws.

He touched the glass.

The creature touched it back.

“I’m still here,” he said, though he could not tell whether he meant his body, his memory, or the stubborn fragment that had chosen Mira over escape.

Below, from the opened shaft, came a deep collapse. The earth exhaled dust. Somewhere under it, the Faculty's station folded into itself, burying its vats, its theaters, its preserved dead. Imago was down there. Obolus, perhaps. The thought brought no certainty, only a pressure behind his eyes.

Vance lifted Mira again. She was frighteningly warm against him.

“We’ll find a way to be human again,” he whispered.

The promise sounded like a lie. His claws tightened, and he forced them to loosen. Humanity, he understood now, was not a baseline hidden in clean blood. It was a choice repeated while every instinct urged otherwise.

He turned toward the distant ruin where a tower had fallen across the old highway, making a jagged bridge above the fog. There might be shelter there. Water. Other survivors. Or another kind of hunger.

He began to walk.

With every step, the new lattice beneath his skin tasted the wind and mapped the living city. With every step, the dead murmured at the edge of hearing. Mira's pulse answered them—small, uneven, stubborn.

Vance carried the broken bag, the unconscious woman, and what remained of his name into the ruined morning.

He would never return to the man he remembered.

Behind him, the earth closed over the laboratory. Ahead, the city waited, vast and diseased. Between those two darknesses, something transformed and monstrous kept moving.

For now, that was enough.
