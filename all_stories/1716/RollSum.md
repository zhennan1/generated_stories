## Chapter 1: Awakening

The first thing Dr. Elias Vance remembered was the smell.

Formaldehyde, sharp enough to sting his throat, braided with the damp odor of mycelium. Beneath both lingered something sweet and fatty, like meat left in a sealed room.

He tried to breathe. His chest convulsed.

A membrane peeled from his mouth with a wet click. Cold vapor spilled around his face, and an alarm began to pulse overhead.

“Easy,” someone said. “The lungs remember before the mind does.”

A face leaned over him.

It was almost human. The eyes were set too far apart, black and polished, each surrounded by translucent tissue. Pale plates overlapped along the narrow jaw. The mouth opened in what might have been a smile.

Vance stared.

The face blinked sideways.

“Dr. Elias Vance. Welcome back.”

He attempted speech. His tongue felt swollen.

“Where—”

“Later. Your airway has been dormant for nine hundred and eighty-seven years.”

The number reached him without meaning.

Long, jointed fingers adjusted a valve beside his head. Another hand pressed a sensor against his throat.

“Do you know your name?”

He did. The knowledge was a small island in a rising black sea.

“Elias Vance.”

“Occupation?”

“Physician. Neurology.”

“Excellent. I am Imago. Your attending custodian.”

Vance lifted his head. Pain flared behind his eyes.

His capsule stood open in a cylindrical chamber washed with blue light. The lining beneath him contracted faintly. Gray polymer sealed the capsule’s seams in thick ridges resembling scar tissue. Nutrient lines entered his arms and abdomen; clear fluid moved through them in rhythmic surges.

Other capsules extended into the haze.

One held a skeleton beneath a crust of white mineral. Another contained a dark stain and a nest of filaments. The nearest viewing panel had fractured, exposing the bones inside. Tiny mushrooms crowned the skull.

“How long?” Vance whispered.

“Since your interment? One thousand and twelve years. Your airway was reconstructed somewhat later.”

“No.”

Imago tilted his head.

“There were provisions,” Vance said. “Power redundancies. The project was designed—”

“The project survived.”

Vance looked again at the neighboring capsules.

Imago disconnected his lines with careful efficiency. His yellowed coat had been altered around a ridged spine. Something folded beneath its shoulders shifted independently of his arms.

On the breast was an embroidered seal Vance recognized: Palingenesis.

His own institution. His own promise of a future beyond the disease that had been killing him.

“What happened?” he asked.

“To the world?”

“Yes.”

“The Collapse. Atmospheric failures. Resource wars. The surface civilizations ended in stages. Our predecessors endured below.”

“Our?”

“The Eternal Faculty. They preserved the facility and its sciences.”

A tremor passed through the floor. Beyond the chamber came a low vibration, like voices speaking through stone.

“Did they preserve anyone else?”

“Much was lost.”

Imago helped him sit and wrapped a robe around him. Its warmth made Vance realize how violently he was shivering.

“Why revive me?”

“Because you could be revived.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the answer I can give you.”

The creature’s voice was gentle. Vance found himself listening for gentleness again, needing it, and hated how quickly that need had formed.

Imago guided him through a pressure door.

The corridor was narrow and warm. Fungal shelves climbed the walls, their undersides veined with amber light. Overhead pipes wore split insulation stitched together with dark fibers. Water ticked beneath the floor.

Vance leaned heavily on his rescuer. Through the coat he could feel something hard beneath Imago’s skin.

“Your condition,” he said. “Is it environmental?”

“In part.”

“Treatable?”

Imago glanced at him.

“Once, that was how we understood it.”

They passed an alcove containing a statue of a physician. Its hands were intact, one holding a bowl, the other raised in reassurance. Its face had been replaced with a chitinous mask.

At the next junction, an attendant pushed a gurney toward a service elevator.

A man lay beneath a transparent thermal sheet. Perhaps thirty, with a shaved head and a bruise along his cheek. Tubes entered his nose and wrist. His open eyes drifted without focus.

Vance stopped.

“Is he conscious?”

“Marginally.”

“What happened to him?”

“Surface retrieval.”

The man moved his mouth. His fingers strained against a padded restraint, then relaxed with unnatural slowness.

“He’s heavily sedated.”

“He needs to remain calm.”

The attendant noticed them and bowed. His waxed mask creased at the cheeks.

“The guest of honor,” he said.

Vance could not tell which of them he meant.

The elevator opened. As the gurney rolled inside, the man’s eyes briefly steadied on Vance. His lips shaped a word.

The doors closed before Vance could understand it.

“Where are they taking him?”

“Below.”

“For treatment?”

Imago’s supporting hand tightened, almost imperceptibly.

“You need rest.”

“So does he. That wasn’t what I asked.”

For a moment, neither moved. Then Vance’s knees began to fail, and he had to accept Imago’s arm again.

They continued toward a cracked viewport set into the corridor wall. Beyond the glass lay the city.

Vance pulled away from Imago and stepped closer.

Tower blocks rose from a plain of ash and collapsed concrete, their upper floors opened to the sky. Broken bridges hung between them. A monorail curved past the window and ended in empty air.

He remembered that line. Or another like it: evening carriages bright with passengers, windows flashing gold above the streets.

Now pale growth furred the tracks. Nothing moved among the buildings. Above them, a sickly green light seeped through banks of bruised cloud.

“This is the surface?”

“Yes.”

He searched for a lit window, a road, smoke from a cooking fire. Anything that might make the view a place rather than a ruin.

“People live out there?”

“Some.”

“Then take me out.”

Imago said nothing.

Vance turned. The doctor stood between him and the corridor, hands folded into his sleeves.

“The surface is not salvation, Dr. Vance.”

Behind the walls, something pulsed. Once. Twice. He felt it through the soles of his feet, a slow vibration traveling through pipes and foundations.

Vance pressed a hand against his chest until he could distinguish his own heartbeat.

Imago had brought him back. Had cleared his airway, warmed him, held him upright. Without that jointed hand he could scarcely walk.

Beyond the glass, the dead city offered no one else to trust.

He looked back along the corridor toward the capsules, the ruined seals, the mushrooms growing through a skull. So many people had been waiting beside him.

Why had they chosen him?

## Chapter 2: The Eternal Faculty

Imago came for him when the wall clock declared morning.

The clock had no hands, only a green pulse beneath cracked glass. It brightened and dimmed with the nutrient line in Vance’s arm.

“Your audience is prepared,” Imago said.

Vance lowered his legs from the cot. His muscles trembled after a thousand years of stillness. “Audience?”

“The Faculty.”

“Who are they?”

“The reason you are alive.”

It was not an answer, but Imago treated answers as medicine: measured, delayed, and never given without purpose. Vance accepted the creature’s jointed hand and allowed himself to be pulled upright.

They went downward through damp corridors where fungus glowed in blue shelves along the walls. Pipes had been patched with gray polymer that resembled scar tissue. Beneath Vance’s feet, the floor pulsed faintly, as though something enormous moved below it.

Imago opened bronze doors.

Beyond them lay a cathedral.

The chamber had once been an auditorium, but its ceiling had vanished into darkness among cables and pipes. Alcoves held preserved organs, bones, and whole bodies suspended in cloudy cylinders. A child’s heart floated in one jar, its valves fixed open. A human spine had been arranged like a ladder.

Three figures waited beneath a cone of white light.

The first was enormous. Provost Malkus sat in a welded-metal chair, his body covered in overlapping black plates. His jaw had widened into two heavy mandibular ridges, and sensory hairs trembled at his temples. One arm was organic; the other was a polished framework of bone-colored composite.

Beside him stood Archivist Seraphine in a robe of translucent membranes. Her pearl-gray carapace shone beneath it. Her eyes were clustered and iridescent, each catching a different fragment of light.

Surgeon Vey wore an apron the color of old blood. He looked almost human except for his lidless, widely spaced eyes and fingers that ended in slender surgical hooks.

“Dr. Elias Vance,” Malkus said. A resonating grille in his throat made the name vibrate through Vance’s chest. “At last.”

“You know me.”

“We know everything worth preserving,” said Seraphine.

Imago guided Vance to a chair beneath the light. A scanning arch descended around him, its lenses flickering.

“The Collapse began with the atmosphere,” Malkus said. “Agricultural failure, resource wars, sealed cities, failed seals. The surface became uninhabitable.”

“You survived underground.”

“We improved underground.” Malkus lifted his plated hand. “The human form was not designed for the world that followed. We corrected it.”

“Voluntarily?”

“At first,” Seraphine said.

Warmth passed over Vance as the scanner mapped his body. A ghostly image formed on a nearby display.

“Adaptive chitin,” Vey said. “Improved oxygen exchange. Resistance to fungal toxins. Efficient nutrient conversion.”

“Mutation.”

“Evolution under supervision.”

The scan chimed. Malkus leaned forward.

“And you were preserved before the corrections became necessary. A legacy genome. Untainted architecture. A mind developed under stable conditions.”

Vance felt cold. “I was a physician.”

“You were more than that.”

“I was dying.”

“Many valuable things are.”

The image vanished. Imago’s hand settled against Vance’s shoulder.

“The tour will clarify,” he said.

It did not.

They entered the tissue banks through a sealed passage. Shelves of vessels rose into darkness, each labeled in obsolete alphabets. Some contained organs suspended in amber fluid. Others held pale ropes of tissue that flexed when Vance passed.

A dispenser released dark paste into a metal bowl. Seraphine took it.

Vance smelled iron beneath the preservatives—not the clean scent of blood on a surgical tray, but something warmer and fattier, unmistakably animal.

“What is that?”

“Maintenance nutrition.”

“For whom?”

“For those who maintain the station.”

She lifted the bowl toward her mouthparts. Malkus made a clicking sound that might have been laughter.

“Do not burden him with old distinctions,” he said. “He has only just returned to the world.”

The consciousness archives occupied a vaulted room below the tissue banks. Cylinders stood in rings, filled with black fluid and threaded by silver filaments. Faces appeared and vanished beneath the surface. An old woman opened her mouth in silent speech. A child’s eye rolled toward him. A man’s features dissolved into light.

“Memory preservation,” Seraphine said. “The continuity of the human project.”

“Are they alive?”

“An imprecise question.”

“Do they know where they are?”

“An unproductive one.”

Vance stopped. “You woke me because of my genome.”

“Because of what it may permit,” Malkus replied.

The answer settled over him more heavily than the damp air.

Vey approached with a portable scanner. “A routine examination.”

“I was scanned upstairs.”

“That was an inventory.”

Blue light passed over Vance’s abdomen, throat, and skull. A red mark appeared on the display.

“Old intervention sites,” Vey said.

“From before cryostasis?”

“Some.”

The image showed tiny scars along his spine, a precise ladder of points. Others marked the base of his skull, beneath his ribs, inside the walls of his throat. Vance knew the anatomy of surgical scars. He remembered none of these.

“What procedures were done to me?”

Vey folded the scanner shut.

“Your body has been cared for.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No,” said Malkus. “It is what you need to know.”

Imago stepped between them. “He needs rest.”

The Provost’s mandibles clicked. “Of course. The legacy genome must not be stressed.”

Vance heard the warning beneath the reassurance.

Imago led him back through the fungal corridors. At his room, Vance glanced over his shoulder. Beneath the station’s mechanical pulse, something had begun to chant.

The sound rose through the floor in a slow, many-voiced murmur. Not words exactly, but repeated syllables, measured like prayer.

Vance lay down when Imago instructed him to. He closed his eyes and regulated his breathing, as he had once taught frightened patients before surgery.

Imago remained beside the cot for several minutes.

Then the door sealed.

Vance opened his eyes.

The chanting continued below, patient and reverent. He listened until its rhythm entered his pulse and made the walls seem to breathe.

The Eternal Faculty called themselves scientists.

But science, Vance thought, did not require prayers for its machinery.

Science did not look at a man as if he were an inheritance.

And science certainly did not smell like hunger.

## Chapter 3: The Ward of the Woken

When the chanting stopped, Vance found that silence was worse.

He lay beneath the thin blanket, listening for footsteps. The room had no clock. Above the door, a membrane brightened from purple to a yellowish white, announcing a morning that had nothing to do with the sun.

A tray waited beside his bed.

The bowl held something pale and glossy. A spoon stood upright in it. He bent close enough to smell salt, warm fat, and a sweetness he could not identify.

He left it untouched.

The door opened when he pressed its panel. He had expected a lock, and the absence of one frightened him. Perhaps they knew how little distance his legs could carry him.

He followed a faded blue stripe along the corridor, steadying himself against the wall. Beneath newer symbols, familiar lettering survived: RECOVERY.

For an instant, the word comforted him.

Inside, six beds stood beneath sagging ventilation cloth. Three were occupied. An elderly man slept with his mouth open; a woman faced the wall; another patient lay hidden behind a screen that trembled with each mechanical breath.

Beside the far window sat a woman in a wheelchair, turning an empty spoon between her fingers.

She watched Vance approach. Her hair had been cropped close, and the skin around her wrists was bruised.

“Are you new?” she asked.

“I woke yesterday. Elias.”

“Mira.”

He glanced at the bowl on her lap. The same pale food had dried along its rim.

“Have you been here long?”

“I don’t know what they count.”

Her hands shook. He crouched beside her, his knees protesting.

“Are you cold?”

“Don’t do that.”

“What?”

“Talk like one of them.”

He withdrew the hand he had almost placed against her wrist.

“I’m a doctor,” he said, then heard how little reassurance that offered here.

Mira leaned toward him.

“The food comes from people.”

Beyond the screen, a pump sighed.

“What have you seen?” he asked.

“They took the man beside me. He used to knock on the bedrail when he wanted water.” She tapped her spoon twice against the bowl. “The attendant brought his blanket back. There was a label caught in it. His name, and a weight.”

“That could mean—”

“I saw the trolley downstairs.”

She looked past him.

Imago stood in the doorway, carrying a folded garment.

“Your strength is returning,” he said to Vance.

Mira lowered her eyes. The spoon stopped moving.

Vance rose too quickly and caught the wheelchair to keep from falling.

“What are you feeding us?”

Imago’s facial plates shifted.

“Recovery nutrition.”

“Where does it come from?”

After a pause, Imago set the garment on an empty bed.

“Come outside.”

Vance followed him only as far as the threshold.

“Tell me.”

“The station maintains a closed waste-recycling protocol,” Imago said quietly. “We cannot afford the losses your civilization accepted.”

“Human remains.”

“Among other sources.”

The corridor seemed suddenly too warm.

“Do they know?”

“The dead consent to nourish the living.”

“The dead don’t consent to anything.”

“Provision is made before death.”

Vance looked back at Mira. She sat rigid beneath the window, gripping her spoon.

“Ask her,” he said.

Imago did not move.

“Ask her whether she agrees.”

“It would distress her.”

The answer left Vance with nothing to misunderstand.

He stepped away before Imago could touch him.

***

He waited until a pair of masked attendants entered the ward, drawing Imago into a murmured exchange. Then he walked around the nearest corner without looking back.

At the corridor’s end, damp air breathed through a maintenance grille. Beyond it, a narrow shaft sloped upward. He thought of the ruined city, the poisonous clouds. Still, there might be another chamber, a stairwell, somewhere beyond their reach.

One corner of the grille had broken loose. He pulled until the remaining fastener tore from the softened wall.

Crawling reopened a patch of skin on his palm. His shoulders scraped overhead pipes. After several yards the shaft leveled, then descended toward a brighter opening.

He smelled the room before he saw it.

Not decay. Cooking.

Through the slats below him, a vat turned slowly beneath a bank of yellow lamps. Paddles lifted pale strips from a thick suspension and folded them under again. Alongside stood a steel table with drainage channels.

There were bones on it, cleaned but not clean enough.

Vance recognized a scapula. A section of ribs. Near the drain lay a hand with the skin removed, its tendons still gathered at the wrist.

He had held hands like that in anatomy rooms, showing students the structures that made a person capable of touch.

A paddle rose. Caught across its edge was a piece of skin bearing the blue curve of a tattoo.

Vance pushed backward.

His heel struck a pipe. The sound rang through the shaft, and he scrambled toward it, away from the light, striking his elbow hard enough to numb his fingers.

He barely reached the corridor before he vomited.

There was little in his stomach. He knelt beside the open grille, retching until his throat burned. A jointed hand entered his vision holding a cloth.

Imago.

Vance recoiled against the wall.

“How long?”

Imago kept the cloth extended.

“How long were you following me?”

“Since you left the ward.”

“You let me go in there.”

“I hoped you would turn back.”

Vance stared at the narrow opening. Imago could have stopped him at any point. He could also have dragged him out and returned him to bed.

“Do you eat it?”

Imago’s hand lowered.

“Yes.”

The admission was almost inaudible.

Vance wiped his mouth with his sleeve. His body wanted the offered cloth, water, a supporting arm. It wanted the creature who had lifted him from the cryogenic chamber to make this bearable.

“I am sorry,” Imago said.

His black eyes held Vance’s gaze. There was grief in them, unmistakable and useless.

Vance took the cloth without letting their hands touch.

He could no longer mistake care for safety. Whatever Imago felt, he had come down the corridor knowing what was in the vat.

Behind them, a trolley rattled toward the ward.

Vance pressed the cloth to his mouth and waited for it to pass.

## Chapter 4: The Records Beneath

The trolley passed without stopping.

Its wheels squealed at the ward entrance, then rolled toward the lift shaft. A white sheet covered whatever lay beneath it, tucked too neatly around the shape.

Mira watched Vance watching.

“Don’t,” she whispered.

“I’m not going anywhere.”

“That’s what people say before they go somewhere.”

A bell chimed beyond the ward. The attendants turned toward it as one. Imago, beside the respiratory patient’s screen, lifted his black eyes toward the ceiling.

Vance moved.

He had already mapped the ward: two doors, one monitored by attendants, the other leading to the old recovery corridor. The second door’s locking plate had been replaced with a loop of insulated wire. He crossed toward it with one hand against the wall, walking like a man still too weak to cause trouble.

No one stopped him.

That was the first warning.

The corridor was narrower than he remembered, its walls furred with pale fungus. Tubes emerged from the plaster and disappeared into it again, as if the station had grown veins. A low vibration passed through the floor. Beneath it, the chanting continued—muffled, rhythmic, almost like breathing.

At the end of the corridor stood a service terminal. A servitor in a stained apron sorted metal tags from one tray into another. Its head was enclosed in a porcelain mask with a vertical black slit. Cables ran from the mask to a box at its waist.

On the terminal, green text crawled beneath a header:

ACCESS: RECOVERY LOGISTICS  
NUTRIENT ALLOCATION  
MORTALITY WEIGHT

The servitor lifted a tag. Vance glimpsed a name stamped into the metal.

MIRA S—.

He stepped from the shadows.

The servitor turned. Its mask made no sound, but the slit widened.

Vance seized the tray and flung it against the wall. Tags scattered. The servitor lunged, articulated rods snapping toward his throat. He caught its wrist and drove his shoulder into its chest. They struck the terminal. Sparks burst from the panel.

The servitor made a sound like boiling water.

Vance tore the cable from its waist. The figure collapsed, twitching. He caught the access card before it hit the floor.

A door beneath the terminal unlocked with a heavy clunk.

He descended.

The stairwell spiraled through concrete and damp stone. The air grew colder and more chemical. Fungus gave way to dust, overheated circuitry, and something sweetly rotten.

At the bottom, the datacore opened around him.

It had once been a room-sized computer array. Now its towers sagged beneath mineral deposits and fungal webs. Cooling pipes leaked mist. A few monitors still glowed, their screens crowded with corrupted symbols.

A plaque remained bolted to the nearest console.

PALINGENESIS  
CONTINUITY THROUGH BIOLOGICAL PRESERVATION

Vance touched the letters.

Memory stirred: committee rooms, grant reviews, arguments about degenerative disease and the ethics of suspended life. Palingenesis had begun as a treatment platform, then expanded toward organ printing, cellular repair, and cryogenic preservation. Vance had worked on neural architecture—how to protect a mind while a body waited for a cure.

He remembered none of this clearly. He remembered even less about volunteering.

He inserted the access card.

The system recognized him.

WELCOME, DR. VANCE.  
LEGACY CANDIDATE 01.

He stared at the screen.

The personnel file opened in layers. The first image showed him decades younger, standing beside a cryogenic chamber. A woman in a laboratory coat rested one hand against the glass.

SUBJECT SELECTION: VANCE, ELIAS  
GENOMIC STABILITY: EXCEPTIONAL  
NEURAL PLASTICITY: EXCEPTIONAL  
COGNITIVE RESILIENCE: HIGH  
ADAPTATION COMPATIBILITY: PENDING

Beneath the original report, someone had added a later instruction.

MAINTAIN FOR LONG-TERM VIABILITY.

He searched the date.

The file predated the Collapse.

He had not been preserved because the ruined world needed someone to wake later. He had been selected before the world ended.

The records multiplied.

SUBJECT 04: AWAKENED. REASSURED. SCANNED. TRANSFERRED TO SYNAPSIS CHAMBER. NONVIABLE.

SUBJECT 07: AWAKENED. RESISTANT. SEDATED. TRANSFERRED. NONVIABLE.

SUBJECT 12: COGNITIVE YIELD ACCEPTABLE. FACULTY INTEGRATION FAILED.

SUBJECT 19: HARVEST COMPLETE.

Photographs appeared beside the entries: sleeping faces behind transparent lids, mouths slack beneath tubes, eyes open in terror. Each record ended with the same destination.

TRANSFERRED TO SYNAPSIS CHAMBER.

NONVIABLE.

Vance scrolled faster. His fingers slipped on the cracked glass.

A facility schematic replaced the reports. The ward sat above the datacore. Far below, concentric rings surrounded a chamber pulsing with light.

SYNAPSIS EXTRACTION  
COGNITIVE SUBSTRATE ACQUISITION  
FACULTY CONTINUITY PROTOCOL

Another file opened.

LEGACY CANDIDATE 01: PRESERVATION SUCCESSFUL.  
NEURAL ARCHITECTURE TO BE RETAINED INTACT.  
DO NOT DISCLOSE PURPOSE PRIOR TO STABILIZATION.  
PSYCHOLOGICAL COMPLIANCE IMPROVES YIELD.

The room seemed to tighten around him.

The food. The open door. Imago’s careful voice and the blankets placed over his shoulders. None of it had been kindness, not in the way he had understood kindness. He had been stabilized. Reassured. Prepared.

Hope was part of the procedure.

A click sounded behind him.

Vance turned.

Surgeon Vey stood at the foot of the stairs. In the blue terminal light he looked almost human, which made him worse. His eyes were lidless and widely spaced. Surgical hooks curved from each finger.

“You accessed restricted records,” Vey said.

Vance backed toward the console. “You bred me.”

“Maintained you.”

“You altered me.”

“Preserved necessary traits. Improved compatibility.”

“With what?”

Vey’s gaze shifted to the schematic. “You know.”

“No.”

The Surgeon advanced without hurry. “You were never a guest, Dr. Vance.”

The hooks opened and closed with tiny clicks.

“You were a vintage.”

Vance reached toward the terminal, searching for anything he could use. Vey moved first. A needle flashed from beneath his wrist and pierced the skin beneath Vance’s jaw.

Vance struck him across the face. Vey’s head turned, but the needle remained. Cold spread through Vance’s neck and chest.

“No.”

His knees failed.

Vey caught him before he struck the floor. Against the Surgeon’s coat, Vance smelled antiseptic, fungus, and the warm, sweet odor of the nutrient vats.

“Rest,” Vey murmured. “The harvest is easier when the subject accepts what he is.”

The monitors blurred. The letters on the screen dissolved into white streaks.

Vance tried to hold on to his name.

Elias Vance.

Doctor. Human. Patient.

But darkness rose through him, carrying the station’s many voices. Beneath them, something vast seemed to breathe.

He was not a patient.

He was livestock, taught to mistake the feeding hand for a rescuer. Every promise they had given him, every careful lie and spoonful of hope, had only been seasoning.

## Chapter 5: The Council of Hunger

Vance woke to thin, irregular applause.

The sound came from the dark beyond his vision, like beetles tapping their legs against glass. His skull throbbed. Something cold pressed beneath his tongue. His arms would not move.

He opened his eyes.

A round theater rose around him in tiers, its concrete walls ribbed with fungus and old machinery. Blue-green growth illuminated figures in black medical robes. Chitin gleamed at their shoulders and throats. Some had translucent membranes fluttering over their faces as they breathed.

Vance lay on a surgical slab at the center of the floor. Restraints crossed his wrists, elbows, ribs, thighs, and forehead. Clear tubing emerged from ports beneath his back. Above him hung a circular instrument, its needles folded inward like metallic petals.

Imago stood at the foot of the table.

“Where am I?” Vance asked.

His voice was dry and weak.

“Continuity Theater,” said a man from the highest tier.

Provost Malkus descended the steps. His torso was armored in overlapping plates, and auxiliary lungs pulsed beneath his ribs. His face remained almost recognizable, except for the mandibles framing his mouth and the pale eyes set along his temples.

“The Council of Hunger,” he said. “You are conscious at an optimal level. That is a kindness.”

Vance tested the restraints. They tightened in response.

“What are you going to do?”

“You have already read the answer,” said Surgeon Vey.

He emerged from behind the slab. The puncture beneath Vance’s jaw still burned.

“Do not distress him before the address,” said Archivist Seraphine.

She sat in a chair made from fused vertebrae. Silver filaments hung from her shaved scalp and disappeared into its upholstery. Beside her rested a cylinder of black fluid. Something pale moved within it.

Malkus came close. He smelled of antiseptic, damp soil, and old meat.

“Elias Vance,” he said. “Legacy Candidate Zero-One. Neurologist. Architect of suspended cognition. The most stable mind to emerge from the old world.”

“I didn’t architect this.”

“No,” Seraphine said. “You merely helped make it possible.”

Memory stirred: fluorescent rooms, a patient’s scalp beneath a ring of electrodes, his own voice explaining that consciousness might be patterned without being preserved. Then illness. A consent form. A blank interval.

Malkus raised one clawed hand. The clicking in the galleries stopped.

“For one thousand and twelve years, the Faculty maintained this station and its mission. We endured atmospheric collapse, famine, war, fungal invasion, and the failure of every surface refuge. We altered ourselves because nature had become an adversary. Chitin for protection. Auxiliary exchange organs for poisoned air. Efficient metabolisms for scarcity.”

A low chant began beneath the theater. It moved through the floor, too deep to be machinery. Vance felt it in his teeth.

“Our adaptations are failing,” Malkus continued. “Cellular repair loops have accumulated errors. Neural matrices decay. The archives preserve memory, but memory is not renewal. We require a cognitive substrate untouched by a millennium of correction.”

“An unmutated mind,” Vance said.

“An intact one,” Seraphine corrected. “You are not merely unmutated. You are curated. Hormonal modulation. Microchimeric reinforcement. Synaptic conditioning. Every alteration brought you closer to this moment.”

The words entered him with surgical precision. They had changed him while he slept, turning his body into an instrument and calling the process preservation.

“What moment?”

“The Sublimation.”

A monitor beside his head lit. An image unfolded above it: a silver mesh descending through a human brain and converging in a black, pulsing mass.

“We will lace your cortex,” Malkus said. “Your neural architecture will be transferred through the Faculty Continuity Protocol. The viable portions of your cognition will replenish our failing intelligences. Your memories and adaptive capacity will be distributed among us.”

“And my body?”

Seraphine’s expression softened.

“Your body will nourish the station.”

Vance pulled against the restraints until pain flashed behind his eyes.

“You call that medicine?”

“The ultimate medical sacrifice,” she said. “One life, freely preserved, sustaining a civilization.”

“I did not consent.”

“Consent is a primitive model,” Malkus replied. “You consented when you entered preservation.”

“I consented to treatment.”

“You consented to continuation.”

The distinction was clean and merciless.

At the edge of the theater, Imago shifted.

“Provost,” he said.

Malkus turned. “Yes?”

Imago’s hands tightened. “The procedure constitutes—”

“Constitutes what?”

Imago looked toward Vance. His compound eyes reflected the restraints in dozens of tiny lights.

“Constitutes murder,” he said.

The theater went still.

Vey hissed through his teeth. Seraphine’s fingers curled over her chair.

Malkus approached Imago until their faces were level.

“You object to continuity?”

“I object to—”

“You object to the use of a resource entrusted to us?”

Imago lowered his gaze.

“You have eaten from the same bodies you now claim to defend,” Malkus said. “You restrained the resistant, sedated the unstable, and prepared the dead. Do not discover ethics only because this specimen resembles you.”

Imago flinched.

For an instant, shame made him seem more human than any unaltered face Vance had seen in the station.

Malkus turned back to the slab.

“The Sublimation begins at second bell.”

“When is that?”

“Three hours.”

The word became an interval. A window.

Malkus lifted his hand. The apparatus above Vance unfolded, needles descending to test the air.

“Rest,” said Seraphine. “Fear consumes cognitive yield.”

The Faculty withdrew through the galleries. Vey followed. Imago remained until the doors had sealed.

“I am sorry,” he whispered.

Vance watched him. “Are you?”

Imago’s mouth opened, exposing small inner teeth. “I do not know what I am.”

Then he turned away, and the door closed behind him.

Vance lay beneath the instruments while the chant rose through the floor.

He had spent his life studying failure: the misfiring neuron, the infected cell, the body turning against itself. Disease was not evil. It was strategy without conscience, replication without permission. It entered through weakness, learned its host, and used every system designed to preserve life.

The Faculty believed he was a patient because they had placed him on a table.

They believed he was a resource because they had named him one.

They believed his mind would remain orderly until they opened it.

Vance stopped pulling against the restraints. Pain was data. Panic was metabolic waste. The slab had sensors. The room had cameras. Somewhere below, the Pattern—or whatever fed the chant—was listening.

He closed his eyes and inventoried the theater.

Six visible exits. Three active monitors. Two restraint circuits. One anesthetic line. Imago’s hesitation. Malkus’s impatience. Seraphine’s dependence on procedure. Vey’s confidence.

The apparatus descended another centimeter.

Vance breathed slowly through his nose.

If they intended to preserve him, he would become contamination. If they intended to copy him, he would become noise. If they intended to digest his mind, he would give them something that could not be digested.

For the first time since waking, fear became useful.

He began, silently, to design the disease.

## Chapter 6: Obolus's Testament

The cell had no door, only a translucent membrane stretched across the opening. It contracted with the station’s breathing, drawing in damp air that smelled of fungus, antiseptic, and the sweet iron reek of processing rooms.

Vance sat on the cot, wrists marked by restraints. Somewhere beyond the walls, machinery counted toward the second bell.

He had tried to reconstruct his life and found only fragments: a lecture hall, a woman’s hand on his shoulder, a consent form glowing on glass, the sensation of drowning in cold. Then darkness, edited into a single breath.

The membrane rippled.

An old creature entered carrying a metal case. Its carapace was split along the chest and abdomen, held together by black sutures and medical tape. Clear fluid leaked from the seams. Beneath the broken shell, pale fibers trembled with every breath.

“You are Elias Vance,” it said.

Its voice rasped like an ancient recording.

“I was.”

The creature tilted its head. Clouded eyes nestled beneath layered chitin; sensory filaments curled along its cheeks.

“Good. You still distinguish the past from the present.”

“Who are you?”

“Curator Obolus.”

“A coin placed in the mouth of the dead.”

“A suitable name for an institution that has forgotten the value of the living.”

Obolus lowered itself to the floor. Its shell cracked again.

Vance watched fluid gather beneath it. “You’re dying.”

“We are all dying. I am merely less successful at disguising it.”

The curator opened the case. Inside lay a black keycard and a narrow glass vial filled with amber liquid. A filament of something darker drifted within, curling like a drowned larva.

“What is that?”

“The keycard opens a maintenance route below the Recovery Ward. The vial is older than Malkus, perhaps older than the station’s first lies.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No. It is a warning.”

Obolus lifted the vial. “Before controlled mutation, there were wild adaptive strains. Unstable. Most killed their hosts. A few remade them.”

“You want me to inject myself with it?”

“I want you to understand why the Sublimation will work.”

Vance stood. “They said I was unmutated.”

“They said what made the harvest convenient.”

The old creature’s voice softened. “You have not been pure for centuries.”

The words struck harder than Vey’s needle.

“Explain.”

“During preservation, your endocrine system was adjusted to slow decay and improve cellular compliance. Your neural responses were conditioned for patterned interfaces. Microchimeric cells from other donors were seeded into your marrow and nervous system. Your immune system was softened, then trained again.”

“I was in cryostasis.”

“Cryostasis is not stillness here. You were opened, sampled, repaired, and sealed again. Sometimes decades passed between interventions. Sometimes centuries.”

Vance looked at his hands. They were still his: long fingers, blunt nails, the scar at the base of his thumb from a surgical blade slipping during residency.

“The scars.”

“Access points.”

“The dreams?”

“Neural conditioning.”

“The gaps in my memory?”

Obolus did not answer.

Cold spread through Vance, unrelated to the chamber. “Did I consent to this?”

“You consented to treatment.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No,” Obolus said. “It is not.”

The chant from below rose through the walls, a single syllable carried by many voices. Vance had thought it prayer. Now it sounded like a machine learning to speak.

“I built Palingenesis,” he said. “Or part of it.”

“You designed the suspension architecture.”

“For patients who could not be cured in their own time.”

“For patients who could be useful in ours.”

“I would never have agreed to that.”

“Would you have agreed to save a civilization?”

“I would have agreed to save people.”

Obolus gave a dry click. “That distinction was the first thing we lost.”

Vance stared at the vial. The dark filament inside moved against the glass, tracing slow loops as though it sensed him.

“I wrote the ethical standards for Palingenesis,” Obolus continued. “I argued that no patient could be reduced to future utility. I swore an oath to protect the vulnerable from those who called necessity a virtue.”

“And then?”

“The sky failed. Crops failed. The refuges failed. Children starved beneath clean-room lights while our colleagues calculated caloric yield from the dead.” Obolus touched the split in its chest. “We told ourselves survival would be temporary. Every compromise became a precedent. Every precedent became law.”

“You became the Faculty.”

“We became what the law was designed to prevent.”

Obolus pushed the vial across the cot. “The strain does not obey the Faculty’s controls. It adapts according to stress, environment, and whatever remains buried in the host. If you take it, you may become something they cannot digest.”

“Or it may kill me.”

“Yes.”

Vance almost laughed. “You offer terrible alternatives.”

“I offer an honest one.”

He picked up the vial. It was warm.

“Why tell me this?” he asked.

“Because your body is not merely altered. It was tuned. Hormonal cycles, neural conditioning, donor cells—each intervention prepared you for their harvest. You are a tool shaped to fit their machine.”

His reflection trembled in the amber liquid: a gaunt face, silver hair, eyes belonging to a man who had lost a thousand years without living them.

“Then what’s left of me?”

Obolus leaned forward, fluid dripping from its carapace.

“They changed your body. They buried your choices beneath procedure. But a tool does not ask whether it is a tool.”

The answer did not restore Vance’s humanity. It only gave his horror a direction.

“What do you want from me?”

“Destroy the Pattern.”

“The black mass.”

“The archive, the engine, the appetite. Every mind they consumed is in it, or near it, or screaming through it. The Faculty calls that continuity. It is only accumulation.”

“And you?”

“I am already in its shadow.”

Obolus reached for the keycard, then stopped. “Take it.”

Vance did.

At the membrane, the curator paused. A wet crack traveled through its shell.

“Why help me now?”

For the first time, Obolus looked directly at him.

“Because I remember the oath,” it whispered. “And because I am tired of surviving what I became.”

The membrane opened. Obolus disappeared into the fungal dark, leaving a thin trail of luminous fluid behind.

Vance stood alone with the keycard in one hand and the vial in the other.

The chant deepened. The station seemed to recognize his choice before he made it.

He closed his fingers around the strain.

“Destroy the Pattern,” Obolus had said.

Then, almost too softly to hear:

“Let me die.”

## Chapter 7: The Extraction

The cell opened without an alarm.

Vance held Obolus’s card against the plate beside the membrane. The translucent seal slackened, peeling away with a wet sound. Beyond it, the corridor was empty except for the luminous drops the Curator had left behind.

He followed them until they turned toward the lifts. Then he went the other way.

The vial knocked against his ribs inside his borrowed gown. He cupped it through the cloth, afraid of breaking it, more afraid of what would make him choose to open it.

Beneath the Recovery Ward, the keycard admitted him to a maintenance recess. A ventilation grille hung crookedly above a bank of dead monitors. He climbed the console and pulled himself into the opening.

The duct was warm and furred with pale growth. Every movement sent pain through his shoulders. Somewhere below, someone coughed, then began the same cough again, with precisely the same catch of breath.

A recording, he told himself.

He crawled faster.

The chant thickened as the duct descended. Through its metal floor he felt machinery gathering speed. At the next junction, blue light shone through a grille.

He lowered his face.

Mira lay in a cradle beneath a ring of surgical lamps.

Her wheelchair stood beside the wall, one footplate folded up. That ordinary little asymmetry almost undid him. Her wrists were secured, her head enclosed in a delicate silver framework. Filaments descended from it into needles along her scalp and neck. Beneath the transparent cradle, cables hung like roots.

Vey stood at her shoulder.

“Reference cognition remains coherent,” the Surgeon said.

Seraphine watched a display. “Increase acquisition. We require a stable calibration before Zero-One arrives.”

They were testing the apparatus on her.

Vance pressed his mouth against his wrist.

Mira’s lips moved. He could not hear her over the machinery, but he recognized the shape of a word.

Please.

Then light rose through the floor.

What he had taken for dark glass beneath the cradle became transparent, revealing something vast in the chamber below. Pale folds swelled against retaining ribs. Black fluid moved through channels between them. Silver threads converged in knots that brightened and dimmed, and with each pulse the mass contracted.

There were shapes embedded in it that resembled faces only until he tried to look directly at them.

The Pattern.

Obolus had called it an appetite. Vance had imagined a machine with a biological component. This thing filled the foundations.

Mira cried out.

Her voice came again a moment later from beneath the floor.

Vance pulled back from the grille.

A service panel occupied the duct wall. Its faded diagram meant little to him, but he recognized the warning strip marking an emergency power transfer. Beside it, through a second opening, he could see a recessed control station below.

He eased the grille inward and dropped behind a column of insulated pipes. Pain flared through his ankles. Neither Vey nor Seraphine turned.

The keycard woke the station.

A maintenance screen offered him a choice between isolating the chamber and transferring its load. He selected transfer. A warning bloomed across the display: the receiving system was unavailable.

He confirmed anyway.

For one breath, nothing happened.

Then the lamps burned white.

A crack snapped overhead. Seraphine staggered from her console as sparks scattered across it. The chant broke into separate voices, abruptly frightened.

Vance ran toward the cradle.

At its base, a red lever sat beneath a shield marked CENTRAL LACE ISOLATION. Through the glass below, the converging silver threads blazed. If that lever severed the feed, perhaps the surge would finish what he had begun.

He reached for it.

Mira’s body arched against the restraints. A needle shifted at her throat, drawing a thin red line.

“Help me,” she said.

He turned away from the lever.

Vey came around the cradle. “You will destroy the reference.”

Vance caught the wheelchair by its handles and shoved it into the Surgeon’s legs. Vey fell against a console. The impact rang through the chamber.

There was no time to understand the release controls. Vance tore open the wrist straps, then found the catch beneath the silver framework. It resisted. Mira stared past him while the filaments trembled.

“Look at me,” he said. “Mira. It’s Elias.”

“The food,” she whispered.

“I know.”

“The food.”

He wrenched the catch upward.

The framework opened. Needles withdrew unevenly; one remained caught until he pulled it free with fingers already slippery with blood. He pressed a fold of his gown against her neck and lifted her.

She was terribly light.

Her eyes opened fully.

There was no recognition in them.

“Mira?”

“The food,” she said, in the exact cadence he remembered from the ward.

Beneath their feet, her voice said, “Please don’t let them.”

He nearly dropped her.

Seraphine was shouting into a wall receiver. Doors unfolded along the far side of the chamber, admitting low, chitinous bodies. Their jointed limbs struck the floor in a rapid clatter. Drones, some bearing instruments, others only hooked forelegs.

Vance backed toward the service exit.

Imago stood in it.

For an instant relief weakened Vance’s knees.

Then Imago extended one long arm across the doorway.

“Containment breach,” he said to the approaching drones. “The candidate must remain intact.”

“Move.”

“Put her down, Elias.”

Behind him, Vey was getting up.

Vance shifted Mira against his chest. Her head rolled onto his shoulder, eyes fixed on Imago.

“The food,” she told him.

Imago’s arm lowered an inch.

“She is experiencing residual speech,” he said.

“Look at her.”

“I can stabilize—”

“Look at her.”

One of the drones struck. A hook raked Vance’s upper arm, opening skin from shoulder to elbow. He stumbled into the doorframe. Mira made no sound.

Imago caught the drone’s limb.

“Intact,” he said sharply.

Vey shouted his name.

For a moment Imago held the creature back, staring at Mira’s face. His mouthparts moved without speech.

Then he shifted aside.

Vance pushed past him.

He ran badly, one arm failing beneath Mira’s weight, blood warming his wrist. Behind him came Vey’s voice and the clatter of pursuit. He followed the service passage downward, taking turns wherever the air felt cooler, until a heavy pump-room door yielded beneath his shoulder.

He dragged it shut and dropped the locking bar.

Only then did he lower Mira onto a bed of dry packing material beside the pipes.

Her pulse beat beneath his fingers. Her breathing continued. He checked both twice because they were the only answers he had.

Beyond the door, claws scraped metal.

Vance pressed his bleeding arm against his body and remembered Imago’s hand holding back the drone. Not forgiveness. Not even trust.

But he had moved aside.

In that small, dreadful hesitation, something had survived.

## Chapter 8: Mutation

The pump room had been built to keep the station alive, and everything in it seemed to breathe.

Fungal mats pulsed along the walls. Pale caps opened over the pipes, releasing damp heat and the sour perfume of decay. Beneath the grating, a pump labored with the slow, uneven beat of an enormous heart.

Vance lowered Mira onto the floor between two conduits. Her head rolled toward him. The extraction needles had left pinpricks of blood along her throat, but her breathing remained steady. Her eyes stayed shut.

“Mira.”

Nothing.

Beyond the sealed door, metal scraped against metal. A drone clicked in the passage. Another answered.

“Dr. Vance,” Vey called through the door, his voice calm and amplified. “Your injuries require treatment. Open the door.”

Vance pressed his palm over the wound in his upper arm. Blood had soaked his sleeve. Pain was familiar, clean, and reassuring. The rest of him was not. His vision narrowed at the edges, and his heart raced in uneven bursts.

Mira’s mouth moved.

“The food.”

The words came in the same flat cadence as before, worn smooth by repetition. But beneath the floor, beneath the pumps and fungal roots, Vance could still hear her other voice.

Please don’t let them.

He reached inside his coat and drew out Obolus’s vial.

The black filament suspended in amber curled without any visible current. It looked less like a microorganism than a splinter of night. The label was handwritten and blurred with age.

Wild adaptive strain.

No dosage. No restrictions. Only the symbol Obolus had drawn beside it: a circle split by a jagged line.

Become something they cannot digest.

The door shuddered under a blow.

“Candidate Zero-One,” Vey said. “Your cognition is already compromised. Resistance will only damage the substrate.”

Vance thought of the records: the gentle voices, the food trays, the promise of rest. Everything had been arranged around the moment a living person became material.

He broke the seal.

The vial smelled of wet iron and opened graves.

For one second he considered pouring it down the drain. Then Mira made a small sound, almost a whimper, and the decision left him.

Vance drove the injector into his thigh and depressed the plunger.

The strain entered like ice.

He dropped the empty vial and caught himself against the wall. Something moved beneath his skin. His blood vessels darkened, branching upward from the injection site. Heat followed, boiling through his muscles.

His spine arched.

A crack sounded in his chest.

He fell beside Mira, fingers scraping at the floor. The fungal mat recoiled from his hand, then leaned toward him.

“No,” he said.

The word came out twice: once from his mouth, and once as a rasping answer from somewhere deeper.

His skin tightened across his forearms. Plates formed beneath it, pushing through in overlapping ridges. Chitin emerged ivory, then darkened to the color of old bone.

Vance screamed.

The sound struck the pipes and returned altered. Fungal bulbs contracted. Membranes trembled. Tiny pale insects poured from a valve and gathered in a spiral around him.

Pressure filled his skull.

Two pits opened beneath his cheekbones.

He felt the room through them.

Temperature became shape. Motion left trails. The drones beyond the door shone as clustered heat, their electrical hearts blinking in their torsos. The station unfolded into layers of living tissue, polymer, root, wire, and bone.

And threaded through everything was a chemical language.

Scent became grammar.

Fear was salt and copper. Pain was green bitterness. Obedience smelled of burned resin.

He smelled the Faculty through the door: Vey’s impatience, Seraphine’s cold attention, Malkus’s heavy command.

The new sense translated them into meanings that were not words.

Break. Open. Feed.

Vance pressed his hands to his head. “Stop.”

The walls answered with a pulse of intention.

Hatch. Hold. Consume. Become.

Insectoid grammar, he thought. Imperatives without the lies human language permitted.

Then the Pattern stirred below him.

Its pulse rose through the floor and entered his bones. Silver filaments flashed like nerves inside a body too large to comprehend. Fragments traveled with it: a child laughing; someone reciting a surgical oath; a woman begging for water; a man repeating his own name until it lost meaning.

Mira.

He knew her among the thousands.

Not whole. Not safe. A bright disturbance trapped inside the black current.

He reached for it.

The Pattern recoiled.

Every fungal mass in the room convulsed. The drones outside stopped scraping. For an instant, the entire facility held still.

Then the pulse changed.

It had noticed him.

Vance collapsed onto one elbow. His right hand had changed. The fingers were longer, the nails thickening into dark, translucent hooks. He flexed them, and the claws clicked against the floor.

He remembered those hands holding a scalpel.

He remembered designing the first suspension lattice, believing medicine could be made patient enough to outlast death.

He remembered his name.

Elias Vance.

The memories were incomplete, stained by other impressions, but they were his. He had been a doctor. He had not consented to this.

The conviction steadied him.

The door burst inward.

A drone thrust its head through the gap, mandibles opening. Vance saw the command current running through it. He smelled its directive.

Preserve the candidate.

It lunged.

Vance moved without deciding to move. His altered arm snapped forward. Claws punched through the drone’s throat assembly and pinned it against the frame. The machine convulsed, servos whining.

Hold, the new instinct whispered.

The drone’s heat faded beneath his grip.

He released it. The body collapsed. Behind it, the other drones hesitated.

Vance stared at his hand. Chitin had reached his wrist, ridged and glossy beneath the torn sleeve. Fine sensory hairs trembled along his knuckles, tasting the air.

Mira stirred.

Her eyelids fluttered, revealing only a dull gray sheen.

Vance lifted her carefully. Her head rested against his chest. Her pulse remained weak but regular.

“I’m here,” he said. “I’m still here.”

Her lips parted.

“The food.”

He looked down at her blank face and felt the Pattern pulsing below them, holding some hidden remainder of her in its vast archive.

He could not save what he could not reach. He could not prove that the voice beneath the floor had been hers.

But he had heard it.

“I’ll find you,” he said. “Whatever they left of you, I’ll find it.”

The lattice in his body answered with a tremor. Through the walls, the station opened pathways of scent and heat. The fungal growth beside the door bent away, exposing a narrow maintenance seam.

He understood the invitation.

Or the trap.

The Faculty was coming, and the thing inside him had begun to learn the shape of the place.

He gathered Mira against him. His left hand remained human enough to cradle her skull. His right curled into blackening claws.

When he stood, the pump room shuddered in recognition.

Above the door, something whispered through the pipes in a language made of hunger.

Vance carried Mira toward it.

## Chapter 9: The Hive Collapse

Vance carried Mira through the seam in the pump-room wall.

The opening closed behind them with a wet contraction of fungal tissue. Beyond it, the station narrowed into service passages ribbed with metal and pale growth. Heat moved through the walls. Electrical current glittered beneath the floor. Every surface seemed to breathe.

Mira’s head rested against his shoulder. Her pulse tapped faintly beneath her throat.

“The food,” she whispered.

“I know.”

He moved silently. His altered joints bent too far, then corrected themselves. Claws found seams in the walls and held him where a human body would have slipped. Behind them, drones battered the sealed room. Their commands reached him through the station’s living systems:

Preserve. Contain. Return.

Vance opened the vents in his altered body.

A bitter pheromone mist spread through the passage. The nearest fungal mats recoiled. A hatch unlocked. Farther behind him, the drones slowed, their limbs twitching as the orders became confused.

Not command, he thought. Interference.

He descended into a dormitory carved from an old subway tunnel. Rows of narrow beds filled the chamber. People lay beneath gray blankets, some awake, some too weak to move. Their faces turned upward as Vance dropped from the ceiling.

A woman screamed. A man raised a broken feeding cup.

“It’s all right,” Vance said.

His voice rasped through the new structures in his throat. It sounded almost human.

Mira stirred. “The food.”

The survivors stared at her, then at his armored face.

“You’re not one of them,” someone said.

“No,” Vance answered, though the word felt less certain than it once had. “But I know how to open doors.”

The dormitory controls were embedded in a column of living tissue. His blood and scent gave the system permission. Locks withdrew. Doors clanged open along the tunnel, revealing stairwells and storage rooms where more patients had been confined.

People began to crawl out.

Some could walk. Some had to be carried. Vance pointed them toward the upper routes, away from the Faculty’s elevators. He told them to follow the emergency lights. He did not tell them that the surface might be worse than the station.

A child clung to her mother’s coat and looked at his claws.

“Are you a doctor?”

Vance looked down at Mira.

“I was.”

He left the survivors moving upward and followed the taste of power through the walls. The nutrient vats lay beneath the dormitory, behind a pressure door. He cut through its lock.

Inside, tanks rose in rows, each filled with dark red slurry. Agitators turned beneath translucent lids. The air smelled of iron, salt, and preserved tissue.

Vance placed his claws against the main conduits.

The station’s current poured through him. For an instant he felt every pump, freezer, and neural relay drawing power from the buried grid. Then he changed the flow.

The vats ruptured.

Lids blew into the ceiling. Warm fluid cascaded across the floor and flooded the electrical channels. Lights burst overhead. Pumps shrieked. Far above, doors opened and slammed in frantic sequence as the station shuddered.

The command-grammar broke apart.

Feed became drown. Preserve became release. Hold became flee.

Vance turned toward the Synapsis Chamber.

He found it by following the Pattern’s pain.

The chamber doors stood open. Beneath its transparent floor, the Pattern writhed through black fluid and silver filaments. Faces surfaced and vanished. Voices crowded the air: surgical oaths, prayers, names repeated until they lost meaning.

Mira’s voice was among them.

Please don’t let them.

At the chamber’s center, Provost Malkus and Archivist Seraphine stood within a ring of machinery. Silver lace extended from the apparatus into a body suspended above the Pattern. Needles crowned its opened skull.

The identity being transferred was a composite of stolen memories, another mind for the Faculty to inhabit.

Seraphine turned. “You have damaged the process.”

Vance stepped into the light with Mira in his arms. “You damaged the people.”

Malkus smiled, his mouth opening in layered plates. “Person, body, memory—temporary arrangements.”

Silver lace snapped toward Vance.

He caught the first filament and tore it free. It burned through his palm. The body in the cradle convulsed, and voices erupted from below.

Vance seized another strand, then another. The neural lace wrapped his arms and chest, searching for the access points in his scarred flesh. He pulled until the silver mesh parted with a wet, singing sound.

Malkus attacked.

The Provost unfolded into a mass of limbs. A hooked arm struck Vance across the chamber and drove him into a console. Chitin cracked along his ribs. Malkus regenerated even as he moved, torn tissue crawling together beneath his carapace.

“You were made for this,” Malkus hissed. “Your consent remains in the original record.”

“My consent was not a blank check.”

Malkus raised both arms. Blades pushed through his flesh.

Something slammed into him from the side.

Imago.

The doctor’s body was crushed beneath the Provost’s weight, one arm twisted backward, his thorax split and leaking clear fluid. Still, he held Malkus’s killing limbs away from Vance.

Imago looked at him. One eye had burst. The other was strangely gentle.

“I was a doctor once too,” he murmured.

Malkus tore free and struck him through the abdomen.

Imago sagged but did not release him.

Vance rose.

He could feel the Pattern as a wound beneath the world. Mira’s fragment trembled within it. The consumed minds pressed against one another, preserved and starving.

At the center pulsed a node of silver tissue and black nerve—the oldest command, the engine’s heart.

The Pattern opened around him.

Vance drove both claws into the core.

Every voice screamed.

He pulled the node apart.

The Pattern convulsed. Silver filaments whipped through the chamber. Seraphine lifted from the floor and vanished into the black fluid, shouting that the archive must survive. Malkus staggered as his regenerative flesh collapsed faster than it could repair.

The station began to hemorrhage.

Black nutrient fluid burst from the walls. Fungal columns split. Pipes ruptured overhead. The floor buckled as the machinery lost its sustaining intelligence.

Vance found Mira and dragged her into his arms.

Behind him, Imago lay half-submerged in the spreading dark. Vance looked back once.

The doctor’s remaining eye followed him.

“Go,” Imago said.

The escape shaft yawned open at the end of the chamber. Vance climbed into it with Mira against his chest. Below, the Eternal Faculty’s engine screamed through every failing speaker, a thousand stolen minds crying out as one.

He pulled himself upward.

The shaft shook. Fluid rose beneath them.

Vance tightened his claws around Mira and climbed toward the thin promise of open air while the buried laboratory died below.

## Chapter 10: Emergence

The shaft narrowed above them.

Vance climbed sideways, Mira secured against his chest with torn conduit. Each movement dragged fire through his ribs. Beneath the pain came other sensations: groundwater seeping through stone, the tremor of fleeing bodies somewhere above, current flickering in a buried cable.

And beneath everything, the Pattern screamed.

It no longer needed speakers. Its death passed through his altered nerves, thousands of minds slipping from their chambers into silence. Most had become pressure without words. Others remained terribly distinct.

Water.

Mother.

Please, doctor.

He climbed.

Mira’s head rested against his collarbone. Blood had stiffened her hair. Her eyes remained closed, but every few minutes her lips moved.

“The food.”

He tightened his arm around her.

“I know, Mira.”

His voice resonated strangely inside the new cavities of his throat.

A tremor shook dust from the shaft. Far below, something immense collapsed. The emergency lights vanished in succession, leaving him suspended above a darkness his changed senses could still penetrate: roots contracting, fluids draining, doors struggling through their final commands.

Preserve.

Then, fainter:

Release.

He remembered Imago holding Malkus back, his abdomen split, his dying strength spent on a man he had helped prepare for slaughter.

Go.

Vance drove his claws into the concrete and climbed.

The marks disturbed him. These hands had once tied sutures, examined pupils, rested reassuringly on frightened shoulders. Now black talons found purchase where human fingers would have failed.

Above him, a thin gray radiance entered the shaft.

Dawn.

He braced his feet, pushed against a fractured slab, and forced a passage through the earth. Wind struck his face, smelling of ash and wet stone.

For several seconds he knelt without moving.

The megacity lay in broken layers. Towers leaned together above streets buried under their own facades. Bridges hung between buildings that no longer possessed foundations. Swollen silver growth covered vents, windows, the open mouths of overturned vehicles.

Beyond them, sunlight spread through a veil of cloud, turning the horizon copper.

He had seen this city from a cracked viewport. It had looked dead. Standing inside it, he could feel how much still lived here.

None of it promised mercy.

He lowered Mira onto fallen masonry. Plates along his ribs shifted with each breath. The sensory pits beneath his cheekbones registered her warmth, but he distrusted the effortless certainty of that knowledge.

He placed two claws gently against her throat.

A pulse. Weak, but regular.

“Stay with me.”

She did not answer.

For a moment he remembered examining another woman beside a sunlit window. He knew the room’s wallpaper, the smell of boiled linen, the ache in his knees.

He had never been in that room.

The memory dissolved, leaving someone else’s grief behind.

Vance pressed his forehead against the cold stone until it passed. The Pattern was dying, but fragments of its contents had lodged in him. He could not tell whether he had rescued anything or merely carried the wreckage out.

Mira’s lips parted.

He leaned closer.

“The food,” she whispered.

He waited for another word. There was none.

Searching the rubble for cloth, he found a medical bag wedged beneath a collapsed wall. Its leather was cracked; mold furred the clasp. He worked it free and opened it with awkward care.

Most of the instruments had rusted together. A glass vial broke beneath his touch. But one roll of bandages remained sealed, and a diagnostic hammer lay intact beneath the debris.

He lifted the hammer. Its curved metal returned a warped reflection: one recognizable eye, dark plates climbing his temple, pits opening beneath the cheekbone.

He turned it over.

A bag would not restore him. Yet when he eased its strap over his shoulder, he knew what to do next.

He wrapped Mira’s torn scalp, checked the dressing, and freed her from the conduit before lifting her again.

“We’ll find a way to be human again,” he whispered.

His claws clicked against each other.

The promise had escaped him before he could examine it. He could not give Mira back the mind the Faculty had taken. He could not even name everything moving beneath his own skin. A second rhythm stirred under his sternum; the fungal veins below the pavement offered themselves to senses he had never possessed.

He adjusted his grip so no sharp edge touched her.

Behind him, the ground subsided. Black vapor rose from the shaft, carrying a last thin confusion of voices. He thought he heard Imago. He thought he heard Mira.

Then the wind scattered them.

Beyond the broken avenues stood a low ruin whose upper floors remained intact. A thread of smoke lifted from its roof.

People, perhaps. Shelter. Another appetite.

Vance started toward it.

The medical bag struck his hip with every step. He would never again be the man who had entered the ice. Whatever waited ahead would see that before it heard his name.

Mira was warm against his chest.

He carried her into the ruined light, monstrous and afraid, keeping his claws turned away from her, holding what remained of hope.
