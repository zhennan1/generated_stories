## Chapter 1: The Salt and the Silence

The house smelled of closed windows and the lavender soap his mother used to keep beside the kitchen sink.

Theo stood in the doorway with his suitcase against his shin. At twenty-four, he had come home with fewer possessions than he had taken away: clothes, a laptop, three canvases turned to face the wall of the rented van. The canvases were still outside. He could not bear another trip yet.

Beyond the kitchen window, the garden had gone to seed. A strand of bindweed held the handle of his mother’s watering can.

He put his keys in the blue dish by the door. They made such an ordinary sound that he had to sit down.

For eleven months, he had paid someone to check the house. Open the windows. Run the taps. Make sure nothing had burst or fallen in. The woman had sent photographs of damp patches and a slipped roof tile, never of the coat still hanging in the hall.

His mother had died in February. Now it was January again, and he still sometimes reached for his phone when something happened.

Nothing much happened anymore.

The gallery had called his last paintings “unresolved.” His landlord had called about the rent. Eventually Theo stopped opening either kind of message. He packed the studio in an afternoon, leaving a pale rectangle on the floor where his easel had stood, and drove east until the roads narrowed and the sea appeared.

Morrow Point received him without ceremony. The same hardware shop had buckets outside. The same crooked noticeboard advertised piano lessons and missing cats. At the harbor, boats nudged their moorings with the soft, hollow knocking he remembered hearing through his bedroom window.

He had once thought leaving meant he would become somebody.

He carried the canvases inside after dark.

***

There was a chip in the kitchen windowsill, beneath the place where his mother had grown basil. Theo rested his thumb in it while the kettle boiled.

He remembered making it. Nine years old, swinging a terracotta pot by its rim despite being told not to. The pot had struck the sill and broken on the paving stones outside.

“Oh,” he had said, and then, seeing her face, “I didn’t mean to.”

His mother crouched beside the wreckage.

“Well, that’s usually how things get broken.”

He had waited for her to be cross. Instead, she gathered the larger pieces into her apron.

“Come here. Mind the sharp edges.”

They carried them into the garden, where she laid the fragments on the wooden bench. A curved piece still held the pale outline of a leaf that had grown against it.

“Look at that,” she said.

“It’s ruined.”

“It won’t hold a plant anymore.”

“That means ruined.”

She smiled a little. “Does it?”

She turned the fragment in her hand until the leaf shape caught the light. Then she gave it to him.

They spent the afternoon pressing pieces into the soil around the rosemary bed. His mother let him choose where each one went. When they finished, the broken rims made an uneven red border among the green.

For years afterward, he had looked for his piece whenever he came home.

Now the kettle clicked off. Theo poured water over a tea bag and forgot to drink it.

***

He slept on top of the bedcovers in his old room.

At half past four he woke convinced someone had called from downstairs. He lay still, listening.

A pipe ticked in the wall. Wind moved under the eaves.

He had done this in the city, too: woken with an answer already forming, some irritated little What? meant for a person who was no longer there.

He dressed without turning on the light.

Outside, the lane shone with overnight rain. A milk float hummed around the corner, its driver lifting two fingers from the wheel. Theo lifted his hand too late.

He walked downhill past the shuttered bakery and the café with its chairs stacked upside down on the tables. At the seafront, fog had erased the horizon. Water and sky occupied the same pale space.

He had missed the sea in the city. Now he found he had nothing to say to it.

The tide was out, leaving a broad strip of dark sand beneath the promenade. Theo took the steps down and walked toward the pier, his shoes sinking at each stride. Bladderwrack lay in glossy tangles. Somewhere overhead, a gull complained.

He thought about walking until he was tired enough to sleep properly. Beyond the pier, beyond the cottages at the edge of town, there were miles of shore where nobody would recognize him or ask what he was doing now.

Under the pier, the air smelled of wet timber and old bait.

He nearly passed the box.

It sat between two pilings above the tide line, one corner sagging into the sand. A strip of packing tape hung from its side. He noticed it because it moved when there was no wind beneath the boards.

Theo stopped.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then came a faint scraping from inside.

He crouched, careful of the low crossbeam, and lifted a flap.

Two green eyes looked up at him.

The kitten was so small that at first he could not make sense of its proportions: enormous ears, narrow face, a scrap of gray fur gathered into one corner. Beneath it lay a wet towel. Its whole body trembled.

“Hello,” he said.

It opened its mouth, but he heard nothing.

Theo looked toward the beach steps. No one was coming down. He looked inside the box for a note, though he did not know what a note could explain.

“How long have you been here?”

The kitten watched him.

He took off his coat and folded it on the sand. When he reached into the box, the kitten shrank back, then stayed still as his fingers closed gently around its body. It weighed almost nothing. He could feel the quick beating beneath its ribs.

“All right,” he whispered, though nothing was.

He tucked it into the coat against his chest and stood carefully.

On the way home, he kept one hand beneath the bundle and the other cupped over it, shielding the small face from the wind. At the steps he paused to check that it was breathing.

A tiny paw had caught in the wool of his sleeve.

***

He found a notebook in the kitchen drawer while looking for something else—a clean cloth, a number for a vet, instructions for what to do next.

His mother had bought it for him years ago. The first page was still blank.

With the kitten wrapped in a dry towel beside him, Theo wrote:

*January 18*

*Found a kitten under the pier this morning. Someone left her in a box.*

He stopped, listening to the faint rustle of the towel.

*I keep thinking we have both been left behind. I know that isn’t the same thing.*

*I brought her home.*

Beside his wrist, the kitten shifted toward the warmth of his hand. Theo put down the pen and let her.

## Chapter 2: Juniper

By seven, the kitten had stopped shivering.

Theo had made her a bed in a washing basket, folding an old towel around a warm-water bottle wrapped in one of his shirts. She ignored the hollow he had carefully shaped for her and lay instead against the side nearest his chair.

He called the veterinary practice as soon as it opened.

“Keep her warm,” the receptionist said. “And no cow’s milk. Have you got kitten food?”

“I’ve got sardines.”

There was a pause, kind rather than judgmental.

“The shop on Bell Street opens in ten minutes.”

He went with the kitten tucked inside his coat. Outside, Morrow Point was beginning its day without enthusiasm: shutters lifting, a van reversing behind the bakery, gulls disputing something on the roof of the post office. He had forgotten that a town could wake so quietly.

At the shop he bought wet kitten food, shallow dishes, litter, and a tray too large to carry comfortably. The woman behind the counter put everything into two bags.

“New arrival?”

“Found her under the pier.”

“Lucky you went down there.”

He looked at the gray head beneath his collar. “Yes.”

He wasn't sure which of them she meant.

At home, the kitten approached the food with solemn suspicion. Then she planted both front paws beside the saucer and ate so urgently that Theo sat down on the kitchen floor.

“Slowly,” he said, though he had eaten nothing himself.

She emerged with a smear of food on her nose. When he wiped it away with a damp cloth, she opened her mouth. No sound came out.

“Nothing to say?”

Her ears tilted toward him.

He felt a vibration beneath his fingers before he heard it: an improbable, rattling purr. It seemed too large for her, as though someone had hidden a small engine among her ribs.

Theo laughed. The sound startled them both.

***

When he was seven, a black-and-white cat had appeared outside the back door during a week of rain.

His mother called her Mabel before she had crossed the threshold.

“You can’t name somebody else’s cat,” Theo had protested.

“Then we’ll apologize when somebody claims her.”

They put notices in the shop and the library. His mother asked at every house along the lane. Nobody claimed Mabel, who spent the intervening days asleep on their clean washing.

“What if she belongs nowhere?” Theo asked.

His mother was kneeling beside the stove, warming a towel. She looked up at him.

“Everything abandoned just needs one person.”

“One person to do what?”

“To start with? Open the door.”

Mabel stayed for fourteen years.

Now Theo sat beside another small animal in the same kitchen, with the same uneven cupboard pressing against his shoulder. For a moment he could almost hear his mother opening the drawer for a spoon.

Then the refrigerator clicked off, and the house was quiet again.

The kitten climbed onto his ankle.

“All right,” he told her. “The door’s open.”

***

Dr. Okafor fitted them into a cancellation that afternoon. She weighed the kitten, listened to her chest, checked her mouth and ears, and ran practiced fingers through the thin gray fur.

“A little girl,” she said. “Underweight. We’ll help with that.”

Theo stood with both hands in his pockets so she wouldn't see him reaching to steady the kitten unnecessarily.

“Is it strange that she doesn’t meow?”

“Not necessarily. Some are quiet. Let’s get her settled and eating regularly, and keep an eye on her.”

There was no microchip. Theo left his number in case anyone called about a missing kitten, then carried her home with a feeding schedule and an appointment for a follow-up.

At the dunes he stopped to shift the bag of supplies digging into his wrist. Wind combed through the low juniper shrubs beside the path. They grew close to the earth, their branches bent inland, holding their green through winter.

The kitten poked her head out.

“Juniper,” he tried.

She blinked.

He said it again, more softly. A name he could imagine calling from the kitchen door.

By the time they reached the house, she had one.

***

Over the next few days, Theo learned the intervals of her hunger. He learned that she preferred the blue saucer to the white one, and that a dressing-gown cord could be stalked for twenty minutes but a purchased toy mouse was beneath consideration.

Her silence made him attentive. He began to recognize the little forward lean that meant she wanted lifting, the fixed stare directed at an empty dish.

On Thursday he took her into Anya’s café in the washing basket, now lined with the shirt she had claimed.

Anya came around the counter.

“Oh,” she said, bending down. “Look at those ears.”

“This is Juniper.”

“And does Juniper take coffee?”

“Apparently not. Medical advice.”

Anya smiled and brought water in a saucer. Beside Theo’s cup she set a little jug of cream.

“For you,” she said, when he glanced at it. “I remember how you take it.”

He had been drinking his coffee black because buying anything beyond necessities felt presumptuous. He poured in the cream and watched it loosen through the dark.

Anya left a toasted teacake beside him without asking questions.

Through the window he watched Ezra pass in his old navy jumper, then backtrack at the sight of the basket.

“Heard you’d come home,” the lighthouse keeper said.

Theo braced himself, but Ezra only offered Juniper a knuckle to smell.

“I’m mending nets down by the shed tomorrow. Could use another pair of hands.”

“I don’t know how.”

“Neither did I once.”

Theo looked down at Juniper, who had fallen asleep against his wrist.

“Maybe,” he said.

“Ten, if you fancy it.”

It was a small invitation, easy enough to refuse. He found himself grateful for that.

***

That night, he opened the notebook while Juniper slept in the crook of his elbow.

*January 22*

*She doesn’t meow. She just watches me like she already knows me.*

*Her name is Juniper. Today I went to the café and stayed long enough for my coffee to get cold.*

*Tomorrow there are nets.*

He paused. He had meant to write something about his mother, but couldn't find a sentence that didn't feel rehearsed. Instead he recorded how much Juniper had eaten.

When he stood to go to bed, she woke and stretched a paw across his wrist. Her claws caught lightly in his cuff.

“I’m coming,” he said.

He carried her upstairs. For the first time since returning, he set an alarm—not because he expected to sleep, but because someone would want breakfast.

## Chapter 3: A Small Unraveling

By the second week of February, Juniper had learned the house.

She knew which kitchen board complained beneath Theo’s heel and which corner of the sitting-room rug lifted high enough to conceal a bottle cap. She knew that the gulls beyond the window could not be caught, though she continued to crouch below the sill and consider them. At breakfast she sat beside her blue saucer, solemn as a guest who had arrived early.

No one had come forward to claim her.

Theo had gone to help Ezra with the nets, arriving late with Juniper tucked inside his coat. He had mostly watched, then held a length of mesh while Ezra worked. The following Friday he went again.

These were things he could tell himself when asked how he was doing.

The first time Juniper fell, he laughed.

She had aimed for the sitting-room chair, launched herself with enormous confidence, and landed against its upholstered side. Her claws caught briefly before she slid to the rug.

“Oh, very elegant.”

She turned toward him, ears upright. He lifted her onto the cushion, where she washed a paw with an air of deliberate indifference.

She was still small. The chair was high. There were any number of reasons.

Two days later, carrying a mug through the kitchen, he saw her hindquarters sway.

It was a small movement, scarcely more than a stumble. Her front paws continued toward her dish while her back feet seemed momentarily uncertain where the floor had gone. Then she steadied herself and ate.

Theo stood with the mug warming his fingers.

“Slippery boards,” he said.

He laid an old towel beneath her dishes.

For a while, the towel appeared to solve it. Or he arranged his attention so that it did. He watched her eat, watched her attack his sleeve, watched her sleep with one gray paw folded over her nose. He did not watch too closely when she crossed the room.

Then, one wet afternoon, she tried to climb onto his lap and missed.

He caught her before she struck the floor. Her little body twisted in his hands, impatient to continue what she had begun.

“All right,” he whispered. “All right.”

He called Dr. Okafor with Juniper pressed against his chest.

***

The waiting room smelled of disinfectant and damp wool. A terrier beneath the opposite chair trembled so vigorously that its lead tapped against the metal leg.

Theo kept his hand inside Juniper’s basket. She rested her chin across his fingers.

“She’s eating,” he told Dr. Okafor before the examination had properly begun. “She still plays. It’s only sometimes.”

“Show me what you’ve noticed.”

On the floor, Juniper took several determined steps toward Theo’s shoe. Her left back foot slipped sideways. She corrected herself, then sat.

Dr. Okafor watched without speaking.

Theo disliked that silence more than he would have disliked a frown.

She examined Juniper’s ears and eyes, listened to her chest, and gently tested how she placed her paws. Juniper endured this with offended dignity, turning her head repeatedly to locate Theo.

“There are a few possible causes,” Dr. Okafor said. “An infection may be affecting her balance. I’d like to start medication for that possibility, and take a blood sample.”

“An infection,” Theo repeated.

“It’s one possibility. We’ll need to see how she responds.”

“But it could clear up?”

“It could, yes.”

He held tightly to the word could.

Dr. Okafor explained the doses, wrote them down, then had him repeat the instructions. She asked him to prevent Juniper from climbing and to call promptly if the weakness worsened or she stopped eating.

He nodded at everything. Only outside, with the medicine in his pocket, did he realize he had forgotten to ask how long she might have been ill.

Rain gathered along the basket handle. He sheltered it beneath his coat.

***

His mother had received her diagnosis on a Tuesday.

He remembered because he had complained about missing a meeting. A small gallery, a possible group show: something that had seemed precarious enough to require his presence every minute.

In the hospital café afterward, she had unwrapped a shortbread biscuit and broken it carefully in two.

“We’ll fight it,” she said.

Her smile had been quiet. He had taken it for courage, and perhaps it was, but now he remembered how she had looked down before giving him the larger half.

He had begun talking immediately. Specialists, second opinions, things he had read without understanding. She had listened, brushing crumbs into her palm.

Outside the café window, a groundskeeper was planting yellow flowers in the rain.

Theo could remember those flowers more clearly than the doctor’s face.

***

At home, he read the label three times.

Juniper objected to the medicine. She backed away, shook her head, and left a shining bead of it on his wrist. He called the practice to check what to do, embarrassed by the relief of having a practical question.

The second attempt went better.

“There,” he told her. “We’re learning.”

He moved her basket beside the sofa and laid a folded blanket on the floor. The chair she liked was turned toward the wall. When she investigated this betrayal, he sat down beside her until she climbed onto his thigh instead.

That evening she ate most of her supper.

He washed the blue saucer and found himself planning the next morning: food first, then medicine; a call to the practice if he needed help. Ordinary arrangements. Things that might make her better.

He allowed himself to imagine her springing cleanly onto the chair.

Later, upstairs, she slept against his ribs. Each time he began to drift off, he woke enough to touch her side.

At two he reached for the notebook.

*February 12*

*The vet thinks it might be an infection. We have medicine. She ate tonight and tried to steal the string from my sleeve.*

His pen hovered, then moved again.

*I keep checking her breathing in the night. I don't know when I became someone who is afraid of love.*

Juniper stirred beneath his hand. He set the notebook aside and lay down carefully, leaving room for her to stretch.

## Chapter 4: What the X-Rays Couldn't Say

When Dr. Okafor called, Theo was washing Juniper’s blue saucer.

He had learned to recognize the practice number. For three weeks it had meant adjustments, appointments, another question about whether the medicine was helping. He dried his hand on his trousers and answered before the third ring.

“The specialist’s report has come back,” she said. “Could you bring Juniper in this afternoon?”

Behind him, Juniper was worrying the loose end of the towel beneath her dishes. She had caught it between her front paws and looked pleased with herself.

“Is it the infection?”

There was a pause, brief enough that afterward he wondered whether he had imagined it.

“I’d rather we talked in person.”

He stood holding the clean saucer until the water on his wrist went cold.

***

The X-rays were still on the screen in the consulting room.

He had found them reassuring when they were taken: Juniper rendered in pale, orderly shapes, each small bone apparently where it belonged. Dr. Okafor had explained that they could rule out certain injuries, not tell them everything. Blood tests had followed, then a referral, a sample sent away. Meanwhile, the medicine had done nothing.

Now Juniper sat on his folded coat on the examination table. Her hind feet slowly slid apart. Theo put a hand behind her before she could fall.

“The testing confirms a rare inherited storage disorder,” Dr. Okafor said. “Her body is missing an enzyme it needs. Material builds up inside cells, including the cells in her nervous system.”

He waited for the rest: the medicine, the difficult expense, the journey to a clinic somewhere inland.

“It’s progressive,” she said. “I’m very sorry. There isn’t a treatment that can stop it.”

“But there must be something.”

“We can help with symptoms as they arise. We can make changes so she’s safe and comfortable.”

“No. I mean for the disease.”

Dr. Okafor moved her chair closer. She did not reach for him.

“There’s no cure, Theo.”

Juniper lowered her head and began washing one paw. He watched her tongue catch the fur between her toes.

“The X-rays were all right.”

“They were. This isn’t something an X-ray can show.”

“How long?”

“It varies. Given her age and how her symptoms are progressing, I think we’re talking about months, rather than years.”

He looked at the calendar above the desk. A photograph of a sheepdog in bluebells. Someone had circled a date in March.

Months was not a length of time. It was February, March, April. It was the bag of kitten food he had bought because the larger size was cheaper.

“Is she hurting?”

“Not necessarily. The loss of coordination itself doesn’t mean she’s in pain. But her needs will change, and we’ll keep assessing her comfort. You won’t have to make those decisions alone.”

He nodded because she had stopped speaking.

She explained things he could not retain. He asked her to write them down. When she did, he stared at her ordinary handwriting and disliked her for being able to form it.

At the door he turned back.

“She was abandoned. Could being cold have caused it? Or not eating?”

“No,” Dr. Okafor said firmly. “You didn’t cause this. Neither did finding her too late.”

He had not asked that second question aloud.

***

At home he set Juniper on the blanket and opened his laptop.

Outside, rain blurred the garden. Water collected in the broken pots along the rosemary bed. He searched the name on Dr. Okafor’s sheet, then searched it with *treatment*, then *survival*, then *misdiagnosis*.

He found veterinary papers he could barely understand. He found pages that repeated the same sentences in different arrangements. He found a photograph of a kitten whose ears resembled Juniper’s and closed it so quickly the cursor jumped.

Experimental.

Promising.

Further research required.

Those words kept him upright.

He wrote an email to a university clinic, attaching the report. He searched for trials in other countries. He calculated the cost of a train, a flight, a consultation, though he had no idea whether anyone would see them. There were still three paintings upstairs. He could sell them cheaply. He could call the gallery. He could take any job.

At six, Juniper nudged his ankle.

“Just a minute.”

At seven, he remembered her supper.

She ate with her feet braced on either side of the bowl. Twice her body swayed, and twice she corrected herself. He crouched beside her, furious with the effort it cost her to do something she had done yesterday without his noticing.

Then he went back to the screen.

***

His mother’s last art project had been a bowl.

Not one she had made. A shallow white bowl from a charity shop, broken when her elbow caught it on the kitchen counter. By then she was tired easily. Theo had come home for the weekend and found the pieces arranged on newspaper beside a tube of gold-colored repair paste.

“You could buy another,” he said.

“I liked this one.”

He remembered being irritated by the slowness of it. She fitted two pieces together, rested, wiped a little excess from the seam. The kettle boiled itself quiet.

“Isn’t the idea to hide the cracks?”

“That depends what you want to look at.”

She held the joined pieces toward the window. Her hands trembled, though he pretended not to see.

“We don’t fix broken things,” she said. “We make them more honest.”

“That doesn’t make any sense. You’re literally fixing it.”

She laughed. “Then pass me the next bit and let me be inconsistent.”

He did. They spent the afternoon at the table, arguing about which fragment went where. Once, both certain, they tried to fit the same piece upside down.

The bowl stood on the kitchen shelf now. It could no longer hold soup. It held spare keys, a button, the little screws from something neither of them had remembered to mend.

***

Near midnight, the laptop battery warning appeared.

Theo had twenty-three tabs open. His eyes hurt. On one page, the word *fatal* sat halfway down a paragraph he had read six times.

He shut the lid.

Juniper was beside his foot, awake. He had not heard her approach. She lifted one paw toward his knee, missed, and tried again.

He slid from the sofa to the rug.

“All right,” he whispered.

She climbed onto his thigh, pulling with her front paws while he supported her back legs. Then she worked her way up his stomach until her small body lay against his chest.

Her purr began unevenly and grew louder.

He put both hands around her without quite holding on. Beneath his palms were warmth, ribs, the soft movement of breathing. She pushed her head under his chin, impatient for him to settle.

“I just found you,” he said.

She purred harder.

That was when he cried. Not carefully, as he had at the practice, but with his mouth open and his face pressed into the fur between her ears. Juniper shifted at the wetness, then tucked herself close again.

***

*February 27*

*I sent three emails. I don’t know whether anyone will answer.*

*She ate her supper. She got the towel again.*

*The vet said months. Juniper doesn’t know. That’s the mercy and the cruelty.*

He left the notebook open beside him. Juniper slept on his chest, and for a while he kept his hand still so he would not wake her.

## Chapter 5: The Gospel of Small Things

The first reply arrived while Juniper was pulling a loose thread from his sock.

Theo read it twice. The university clinic was sorry. They knew of no trial appropriate for her condition. They recommended that he continue working with his veterinarian to preserve her comfort.

Juniper tugged harder. Her back feet slid apart, and she stopped to gather them beneath her before attacking the thread again.

He looked from her to the screen.

All morning she had been awake. All morning he had been searching for a way to keep her alive while scarcely looking at her.

He thanked the person who had answered. Then he closed the browser, including the pages he had saved because their titles contained the word *possible*.

He did not delete the reports. He did not stop wanting a miracle. He put the laptop in a drawer and called Dr. Okafor to ask whether a little outing would tire Juniper.

“Let her set the pace,” she said. “Somewhere familiar. Keep her warm.”

He wrote those instructions down. They were things he could do.

***

At the café, Anya brought his coffee and a teaspoon of cream on a saucer.

“An occasional treat,” Theo said, repeating the vet’s warning.

“I can manage occasional.” She set it beside Juniper’s blanket. “Though I make no promises about you.”

Juniper drank with such concentration that cream collected on her chin. Theo wiped it away with his thumb. Beyond the window, two gulls disputed a paper bag, and a man in yellow waterproofs stood watching them as though he had money on the outcome.

Theo laughed.

Anya glanced up from the counter but said nothing. He was grateful for that.

Afterward he carried Juniper to the pier. Where the boards were dry, he lowered her onto the blanket and let her nose the air. She took three uncertain steps toward a coil of rope. He moved alongside her, one hand ready beneath her belly.

There was no destination. When she sat down, they stopped.

He had forgotten that a walk could be like this.

Over the next week they learned a small geography: the sheltered bench outside the café, the first broad platform of the pier, the patch of kitchen floor warmed by the afternoon. Some days the pier was too windy. Some days Juniper preferred his discarded jumper to anything he offered.

He tried to believe her when she seemed content.

***

On Friday, Ezra put a wooden netting needle in his hand.

“You’ve held that edge long enough.”

Juniper lay in an open basket by Theo’s boots, tucked beneath a towel. The shed smelled of tarred rope and damp wool. Outside, someone was scraping a boat hull in slow, determined strokes.

Ezra showed him how to draw the twine through, catch it, tighten the knot. Theo’s first loop sagged. His second was smaller than the surrounding mesh.

“Again,” Ezra said.

Theo glanced toward the basket.

“She’s asleep,” Ezra told him.

“I know.”

But he watched until he saw the towel move.

Ezra waited, the net spread between his hands.

“It feels stupid,” Theo said at last. “Going out for coffee. Doing this. As though I’ve forgotten what’s happening.”

“Have you?”

“No.”

“Then you haven’t.”

Theo pulled too sharply and tangled the twine.

Ezra leaned across to loosen it. His fingers were thickened at the joints, the nails edged with work.

“You don’t mend a net for the one fish. You mend it because the sea is still coming.”

Theo looked at him.

“Here,” Ezra said, returning the needle. “Leave yourself enough room.”

By the time Juniper woke, he had made six serviceable knots. Ezra inspected them without praise, which somehow pleased him more.

***

His mother had taught him to skip stones on the sheltered beach beyond the lighthouse.

He remembered the red cuffs of her trousers, rolled clear of the water, and her patient search for flat stones while he chose the biggest ones he could lift.

“Across,” she told him. “Not down.”

His stone plunged into the shallows.

“Very good sinking,” she said.

He scowled. She laughed, then apologized, though not convincingly.

When at last one bounced twice, he turned so quickly to see whether she had noticed that he missed its final leap.

“Again,” he demanded.

She placed another stone in his palm.

Now, standing at the kitchen sink, he found himself remembering how little they had accomplished that afternoon. A few stones thrown. Wet shoes. Sandwiches with sand in them.

He would have given anything to return to it.

Behind him, Juniper sneezed at a drifting speck of dust.

He dried his hands and went to sit with her.

***

The thunderstorm came after supper, sudden enough to shake the kitchen window.

Juniper flattened herself against the rug. At the next crack she scrambled toward him, hind legs failing to keep pace with her front ones.

Theo slid down from the sofa and gathered her into his lap.

“I’ve got you.”

She pushed her face into the fold of his elbow. Rain rattled the glass; the garden vanished behind it. He curved his body around hers without thinking, making the smallest shelter he could.

Her purr began against his wrist.

That undid him.

He cried with his cheek against her head, not quietly enough to conceal it, not trying to turn it into anything useful. Juniper stayed pressed beneath his hand. After a while she stretched one paw across his stomach and fell asleep.

He sat through the storm with her.

***

Two afternoons later, sunlight caught his watch and laid a bright oval on the wall.

Juniper raised her head.

Theo shifted his wrist. The light slid down toward the skirting board, and her ears tipped forward. She reached for it, missed, then tried to stand.

“Here,” he whispered.

He brought the light lower, close enough to touch without getting up.

Her paw landed squarely over it.

For a moment he kept perfectly still. Then she lifted her foot to see what she had caught, and the brightness slipped over her toes.

He laughed, and moved it back.

That night he opened the notebook with Juniper asleep beside his thigh.

*March 8*

*Today she caught the sun on the wall. It wasn’t much. It was everything.*

He put down the pen. Before turning out the lamp, he checked the forecast for tomorrow.

## Chapter 6: The Weight of Watching

The first time Juniper walked into the table leg, Theo blamed the light.

It was late afternoon, the kitchen washed in the dull silver that came before rain. She had been making her slow way toward him, hindquarters swaying, when her forehead touched the wood. She stopped, backed away, and lifted one paw into the empty air.

“Here,” he said.

Her ears turned. She came toward his voice.

He moved the chair out of her path and told himself he had left it at an unfamiliar angle. But the next morning she failed to notice the towel trailing beside her saucer. Usually she caught it between her paws while he prepared her breakfast. Now it brushed her whiskers before she struck.

He stood holding the unopened food.

“June?”

She looked toward him, or almost toward him. Her green eyes were clear.

***

Dr. Okafor examined them in a darkened room. Theo watched the small light move across Juniper’s face and tried to read the veterinarian’s expression.

“Her responses are reduced,” she said afterward. “That fits with the progression we discussed.”

“Can she still see me?”

“I think she can see some things. It may be difficult to know exactly what, and it may change.”

He stroked the narrow space between Juniper’s ears.

“Will she be frightened?”

“Keep things familiar. Don’t move her dishes or her bed. Speak before you touch her.” Dr. Okafor rested a hand on the examination table. “She’ll have other ways of finding you.”

At home he cut an old towel into strips and tied them around the table legs. He padded the low hearth with a folded blanket, secured loose corners of rugs, and walked through each room on his knees, looking for things he had never thought of as dangerous.

From that height, the house was crowded with edges.

Juniper sat in the kitchen doorway while he worked. When he spoke, she raised her head.

“I know,” he told her, pulling a knot tight. “Terrible decorating.”

That evening she tried to reach the litter tray and stopped halfway across the floor. He carried her the remaining distance, held her steady when her hind feet slid, then looked away to give her whatever privacy she could still have.

Afterward, he brought her back against his chest.

There were new ways to be useful. He hated needing to learn them.

***

The pencil was in the drawer with the takeaway menus.

He found it while looking for tape, a short, soft drawing pencil with tooth marks near the end. For several seconds he held it without remembering what he had wanted.

Juniper was asleep on his jumper. One forepaw curved over her nose; the other extended as if she had fallen asleep reaching for something. Her hind legs lay awkwardly to one side.

He fetched a sheet of paper from the printer.

The first line was too heavy. He turned the paper over.

He had not drawn since his mother died. In the city he had moved paint around unfinished canvases, working from old studies, calling it work until even that became impossible. Drawing had felt more intimate. There was nowhere for his hand to hide.

Now it shook.

He drew Juniper’s ear too large, rubbed it away, and started again. By the time she woke, he had made three attempts at her sleeping paw.

She stretched toward the scratching sound.

“It’s only me,” he said, and kept going.

***

When he was seven, he had drawn his mother with eleven fingers.

She had laughed so hard that tea spilled into her saucer.

“Am I a pianist?”

“You’re holding things.”

“What things?”

“Everything.”

She had studied the page again, her smile changing. Then she had asked whether she could keep it.

She kept all of them: purple boats, headless horses, a portrait of Mabel that resembled a burnt loaf. Years later he found a stack secured with ribbon in the linen cupboard. He had been embarrassed by the care she had taken.

“Mum, they’re rubbish.”

“They’re yours.”

“That isn’t the same as good.”

“No,” she had said, folding a corner back into place. “It isn’t.”

He understood her better now, sitting on the kitchen floor with a page he could not bear to throw away.

***

By the following week, the drawings had dates.

Juniper drinking, her chin wet. Juniper braced against his wrist. Juniper asleep in the shallow basket he carried from room to room.

He did not make her steadier on the page than she was. Sometimes this felt cruel. Sometimes it was the only honest thing he did all day.

At the café, Anya found him drawing the pale hairs inside Juniper’s ear.

“She’s resting?” she asked.

“For now.”

Anya sat opposite him. Outside, someone dragged a boat trolley over the cobbles, each wheel making its own complaint.

“When Peter was ill,” she said, “I used to watch him sleeping. Then I’d get cross if he woke, because I’d lost my chance to watch him safely.”

Theo lowered the pencil. He knew her husband had died, but she had never spoken about the illness.

“Did you stop?” he asked.

“Watching? No. I got very tired.”

He looked into the basket. Juniper’s face was turned toward his knee.

“I keep thinking I should be getting used to it.”

“To what?”

He could not answer.

Anya touched the edge of his drawing.

“The love doesn’t stop when they stop being here. It just changes addresses.”

He wanted to ask where it went. Instead he said, “I don’t want her somewhere else.”

“No,” Anya said. “Neither did I.”

She stayed until his coffee had cooled.

***

That night he drew Juniper awake.

He sat close enough for her whiskers to touch his sleeve. Whenever his pencil stopped, she turned toward him, waiting. He spoke about the rain, about Anya’s burnt first batch of teacakes, about nothing he needed her to understand.

Her eyes were still green. He left small spaces in the graphite for the lamplight.

Before bed, he opened the notebook.

*March 19*

*I drew her eyes while she still has them.*

He stared at the sentence, then added:

*That sounds wrong. She has them. She is here.*

Juniper stirred beside him. He put down the pen and offered his hand. Her nose found his fingers first, then her cheek settled into his palm.

## Chapter 7: The Almost-Not

The seizure began at 2:17 in the morning.

Theo knew because the mantel clock clicked over the minute, and because he had already been awake, listening to Juniper breathe in the basket beside his bed. His hand rested on the rim without touching her. He had learned not to disturb her when she was settled. Sleep had become something to guard.

Then her body made a sound like a cupboard door striking its frame.

Theo sat up.

Juniper’s legs went rigid. Her head jerked back, her green eyes wide and blind. One paw scraped at the towel. A thin, broken noise came from her throat.

“No.”

She convulsed.

The basket rocked against the floorboards. Theo reached for her, then stopped, remembering Dr. Okafor’s instructions: clear the space, don’t restrain her, keep her from hurting herself. His hands hovered while Juniper’s little body shuddered.

“Juniper. I’m here.”

He slid the basket away from the chair and pulled a blanket around it. Her claws opened and closed. Her mouth worked soundlessly.

He thought, *This is dying.*

The thought was whole and certain. He saw the box beneath the pier, her enormous eyes fixed on him; the blue saucer; the towel; the patch of sunlight she had chased across the wall. He saw her cheek in his palm only hours before.

“Juniper,” he whispered. “Please.”

The seizure lasted perhaps forty seconds.

It was long enough to take him apart.

Then her body slackened. Her head fell sideways. For one terrible moment she did not breathe.

“Come back,” Theo said.

Nothing.

“Come back, Juniper.”

A shiver passed through her. Her nostrils fluttered. She drew in a rattling breath and let it out against the towel.

Theo lifted her only when the trembling stopped. She lay limp in his hands, her head against his wrist. He held her to his chest and counted.

One. Two. Three.

Her heartbeat fluttered beneath his palm.

“I’ve got you,” he said. “I’ve got you.”

After several minutes, Juniper stirred. Her paws moved weakly against his shirt. Her eyes opened, unfocused, then turned toward his voice.

Theo pressed his mouth to her skull.

“Don’t do that again,” he whispered. “Please don’t do that again.”

Her purr began beneath his hand, faint and uneven, but unmistakable.

He stayed on the floor until dawn.

At six, with gulls arguing beyond the window, he carried Juniper to the phone and called Dr. Okafor. He described the rigid limbs, the shaking, the silence afterward, and her slow return.

“Is she responsive now?” the veterinarian asked.

“She knows my voice.”

“That’s good. Offer her something soft, but don’t force her. Keep her somewhere quiet. If she has another seizure, or doesn’t recover properly, bring her in.”

Theo closed his eyes.

“Did she suffer?”

There was a brief, careful pause.

“During the seizure, she wouldn’t have understood it as we do,” Dr. Okafor said. “Afterward, she may be confused and tired. I’m sorry.”

He looked down. Juniper had tucked her face beneath his chin.

“Is this the beginning?”

“I can’t tell you from one seizure.”

“But it could be.”

“Yes.”

The word remained between them after the call ended.

By late morning Juniper had eaten three licks of warmed food and slept through a delivery van outside. Theo watched her chest rise and fall. Each breath seemed to require his permission.

Then he thought of the clinic.

She could stay there. Dr. Okafor had trained hands, clean blankets, medicines, someone who would notice if her breathing changed. Theo could bring her in and ask for a little time. One day. Two.

He could sleep without listening.

The relief of imagining it was so sharp that he stood.

Juniper shifted in the basket.

Theo turned away.

After his mother died, he had left Morrow Point within a week.

He remembered the departure in pieces: cups in the sink, rosemary browned at the edges, the blue suitcase splitting along its zipper. He had not painted her. He had not painted anything. He packed his studio into boxes and drove north without telling anyone where he was going.

People had called it distance, as if distance were a medicine.

He had needed not to be in the house where she had grown ill, or the garden where she sat beneath the pear tree, or the kitchen where she divided the last biscuit and gave him the larger half. He had needed not to remember the moment she stopped answering him.

The guilt came later. It had no single shape: leaving the hospital to buy coffee, feeling irritated when she asked him to repeat himself, wanting one ordinary afternoon back and realizing that ordinary afternoons were what he had abandoned.

He called it survival because that sounded kinder.

Now, beside Juniper’s basket, he saw how easily leaving could wear the same disguise.

He could tell himself the clinic was safer. He could say love did not require witnessing every decline. He could make his absence sound like care.

Juniper lifted her head. Her gaze passed over him, then found him when he said her name.

Theo knelt.

“I’m here.”

She stretched one paw toward him. He placed two fingers beneath it, and her claws closed weakly around them.

That evening, he opened the notebook his mother had given him. His hand trembled as he wrote:

*I don’t know if I’m strong enough to lose another one. But I don’t know if I’m strong enough to leave either.*

He read it once, then closed the notebook.

Juniper slept beside his knee, her paw still resting against his fingers.

Outside, the tide moved beneath the dark pier, withdrawing and returning. Theo stayed where he was, listening for her breath.

## Chapter 8: The Net and the Needle

Theo left the house before Juniper woke.

In her basket beside the radiator, she slept with her chin on the folded towel. He watched her ribs rise and fall, then checked the food and water he had placed within reach.

He had promised to stay. He was only going out.

At the café, Anya glanced behind him.

“Where’s your shadow?”

“Sleeping.”

“Is she all right?”

“She had a bad night. I thought I’d let her rest.”

Anya set a mug on the counter. “And you?”

“I’m trying.”

He took his tea to the window. Outside, a fisherman coiled rope beside his boat. Theo watched him, grateful for a task that could be finished.

On his phone, a university clinic had finally replied. No suitable trial. No treatment they could recommend beyond the care Dr. Okafor had described.

He read the message twice, though nothing in it changed.

He went home to help Juniper eat and carry her to the litter tray. When she lifted her head toward his voice, he busied himself rinsing the saucer. Once she was settled, he found errands: bread, washing powder, a replacement bulb. He stayed out longer than any of them required.

By afternoon he was at the lighthouse, where Ezra sat repairing a net.

“Got an hour?” Ezra asked.

Theo sat beside him and accepted the netting needle.

Over, under, pull through. He had learned the motion, but today he kept losing his place. On the third mistake, Ezra put down his work.

“How is she?”

Theo stared at the loose cord. “I keep thinking about leaving her at the clinic.”

“For treatment?”

“For someone else to listen to her breathe.”

Ezra nodded without answering immediately.

“My dog was called Bramble,” he said at last. “Brown dog, one white ear. Thought every visitor had come to see him.”

Theo loosened his grip on the needle.

“When he got sick, I carried him upstairs every night. Then I worried the carrying hurt him. So I slept downstairs and worried the floor was too cold.” Ezra rubbed a knot with his thumb. “I wanted there to be a right thing I could do that would keep him alive.”

“Was there?”

“No. There were things that kept him comfortable. And there was staying.”

The wind tugged a loose strand across Ezra’s knee.

“I thought staying would kill me,” he said. “It didn’t. Hurt terribly, though.”

Theo looked toward the water.

Ezra picked up his needle. “We don’t get to choose the tide. We only get to choose how we stand in it.”

On his way home, Theo stopped at the cemetery gate.

He had not been back since the funeral. In February, when the first anniversary came, he had washed every floor in the house rather than come here. Now he followed the gravel path past the leaning yew to his mother’s stone.

Grass had grown against its base. He crouched and pulled it away.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

There was no one nearby, but he spoke quietly.

“I’m sorry I left. I was angry with you. You were dying, and I wanted you to ask me about something else. Anything else.”

He remembered the hospital room: the cup with its bent straw, the curtains that never quite closed. His mother had slept most of that last evening. He had sat beside her, exhausted, looking at the door.

Then she had opened her eyes.

“Don’t be afraid to break.”

They were the last words she said to him. He had nodded, unable to answer. Afterward, he had packed his car and driven north as though distance could prove he was still intact.

“I did break,” he told the stone. “I just didn’t want anyone to see.”

He sat back on his heels.

“I know you didn’t want to leave.”

For so long he had held her absence like an offense she might return to explain. Sitting there, he could remember how hard she had tried to stay.

“I forgive you,” he whispered, then cried because she had done nothing wrong.

When he stood, he brushed the dirt from his knees. He had been frightened. He had left. Neither thing could be undone, but he did not have to keep doing it.

At home, Juniper was awake in her basket.

“Hello, little shrub.”

He checked her water, then went to sit on the rug with his coat still on. He could not manage another useful task.

A towel rustled behind him.

Juniper had pulled herself over the basket’s low edge. Her hind legs dragged as she worked toward him.

Theo reached out, then stopped moving so she could find his hand. Her nose touched his fingers. He lifted her onto his lap, where she turned once, awkwardly, and settled against his stomach.

“You shouldn’t have to come looking,” he said.

Her purr began faintly beneath his palm. He bent over her, his resistance giving way without anything left to defend.

“I’m here.”

He stayed on the floor until the room darkened. Once, Juniper woke and lifted her head. He stroked her cheek, and she slept again.

Later he called Dr. Okafor. They talked about the seizure, her appetite, what comfort looked like now. He asked about taking her out in the basket.

“A short outing, if she’s settled,” she said. “Let her decide how much.”

He pictured the sheltered boards of the pier, the sea beneath them. Perhaps Anya’s café first. He would bring towels and come home whenever Juniper needed.

That night he opened his diary with her asleep beside his knee.

*I spent today finding reasons not to be in the room. She came looking for me anyway.*

He rested his hand against her back.

*I’m not leaving. Not before she does.*

Below that, he wrote:

*Tomorrow, if she is able, the pier. A last good day—not because I know it will be the last, but because she is here now.*

Juniper stirred. Theo put down the pen and touched his cheek to her head.

“Tomorrow,” he promised.

The promise did not reach far enough to save her.

It reached far enough to meet the morning.

## Chapter 9: The Last Good Day

In the morning, Theo packed too much.

Three towels, water, food, a spare shirt, the number for Dr. Okafor written on paper although it was already in his phone. He stood over the basket wondering what else a person could carry against disaster.

Juniper lay in the kitchen light, her chin resting on the rolled edge of a blanket.

“We can stay here,” he told her.

At his voice, she lifted her head. Her ears turned toward him, and when he slid his fingers beneath her jaw, she pressed down with the small, familiar weight of herself.

He helped her eat, then took her to the litter tray. There was nothing ceremonial about it. He wiped one damp paw and changed the towel beneath her. Only afterward did he settle her in the basket.

“We’ll go slowly.”

Outside, Morrow Point smelled of wet stone and someone’s toast. He carried the basket level against his stomach. At the corner, he stopped to check her, and she moved her nose toward the air.

***

Anya opened the café door before he reached it.

“The window table?”

“Somewhere quiet.”

She led him to the back, away from the grinder, and turned off the small radio beside the till.

Juniper rested across his lap while Anya brought his coffee and the blue saucer she kept behind the counter. A little cream shone in its center.

“Just a taste,” Theo said.

He dipped a fingertip and held it beneath Juniper’s nose. Her tongue touched him once, then again.

“There she is,” Anya murmured.

Theo had been afraid of pity, of having to explain the basket and the way Juniper’s legs lay. Instead, Anya sat opposite him and told him that a gull had stolen half a scone directly from a customer’s hand.

“Apple and cinnamon. I hope it gave him indigestion.”

Theo laughed. Juniper’s ear flicked at the sound.

For several minutes, he drank his coffee while it was hot.

When they left, Anya tucked a warm piece of bread into his coat pocket.

“For you,” she said firmly.

***

He took the lower lighthouse path, where the wall sheltered them from the wind. In summer, the grass there filled with insects; now it held last year’s pale stalks and a few small, stubborn flowers.

Ezra was scraping paint from the gate.

He set down the scraper when he saw them, but did not ask how much time remained.

“Good air today,” he said.

Theo sat on the bench beside the wall and lifted Juniper against his chest. Her head trembled briefly before finding the fold of his elbow.

Ezra stood with them, looking out to sea.

A fishing boat crossed the bright water beyond the harbor mouth. Somewhere below, a rope struck a mast with an irregular, hollow note.

“She used to watch everything,” Theo said.

“Perhaps she’s listening now.”

He lowered his mouth toward her ear.

“That’s Ezra pretending he isn’t cold. And that noise is a boat. There’s a gull on the wall, but you wouldn’t like him. He looks dishonest.”

Her purr began so faintly that he felt it before he heard it.

He stayed until she shifted restlessly, then tucked her back into the basket.

***

At the beach, he chose the steps with the gentlest descent. He carried her to the dry sand beneath the sea wall, well clear of the tide, and spread a towel across his knees.

They had sat here before, when she could still dig furiously at the cuff of his trousers. He remembered her astonishment at sand: the way it yielded beneath her paws, then refused to be caught.

Today she lay against his wrist while the breeze lifted the fur between her ears.

When he was seven, he had found a starfish in a rock pool on this beach.

“For me,” he had said, delighted.

His mother crouched beside him, her skirt gathered above the water.

“Let’s have a look.”

It had been orange, with one arm shorter than the others. He remembered wanting to carry it home in his bucket, give it a name, show it his bedroom.

“Will it be lonely here?”

His mother pointed into the pool. A shrimp flickered beneath a shelf of weed.

“There’s quite a lot going on.”

He had accepted that reluctantly. Before leaving, he put a shell beside the pool, a present in exchange for the thing he had believed the sea had given him.

Now Theo touched Juniper’s forehead with one finger.

“I found you just over there,” he said. “Under the pier. Terrible accommodation. No windows.”

Her nose moved against his sleeve.

“You were smaller than my shoe. I didn’t know what to do with you.”

He paused.

“I went home and boiled the kettle. As if you wanted tea.”

The laugh caught in his throat, but he let it come.

He took one photograph. In it, his hand looked enormous beside her face, and her eyes were half closed. He nearly deleted it, searching automatically for a better one. Then he put the phone away.

With the sketchbook balanced against the basket, he drew her ear, her bent forepaw, the shallow curve where her cheek rested on his arm. Halfway through, she shifted. He left the unfinished line.

***

They were home before the afternoon cooled.

Juniper ate a little warmed food and slept while Theo washed the saucer. He unpacked the unused towels. He ate Anya’s bread standing at the sink, then sat beside the basket until Juniper woke.

There had been no miracle. By evening she needed both his hands to help her stand.

He found he could still be grateful for the cream on his fingertip, for the foolish gull, for the warmth of her through his coat.

In his diary, he wrote:

*If love could heal her, she would live forever.*

Below it, after a while:

*She liked the air today.*

He made their bed on the sitting-room floor and opened the window a little. The room grew cool, so he drew the blanket over his legs and left Juniper’s face uncovered beside his hand.

Beyond the houses, the tide moved over the stones.

“I’m here,” he said.

Her whiskers brushed his wrist. He listened until the sound of the water and her breathing no longer seemed to compete, and slept.

## Chapter 10: The Pier at Dawn

Three mornings after their outing, Juniper turned her face away from breakfast.

Theo warmed it again. He tried the little spoon, then his fingertip. She swallowed once and let her chin sink onto the towel. When he helped her toward the litter tray, she could no longer hold her head steady.

He called Dr. Okafor.

At the house that afternoon, she examined Juniper on the blanket where they slept. Theo sat beside her with his hands between his knees.

“Is there anything else?” he asked.

“There are things we could do to prolong this,” she said. “I don’t think they would give her back anything she enjoys.”

He looked at Juniper’s paw. A single claw had caught in the weave. He freed it carefully.

“I thought I’d know.”

“Sometimes knowing feels exactly like wishing you didn’t.”

They talked for a long time. About swallowing, exhaustion, the small movements that no longer brought her where she wanted to go. About what the injections would do. Theo asked whether they could be outside, somewhere quiet.

“The pier,” he said. “If it’s sheltered enough.”

Dr. Okafor checked the forecast before she answered.

That night he did not write in the diary. He lay on the floor with his hand beside Juniper’s face. Whenever she stirred, he told her where he was.

***

At dawn the town seemed to have drawn a sheet over itself.

Theo carried her beneath his coat, wrapped in the soft towel from her basket. The fog beaded on his eyelashes. He walked slowly past Anya’s dark café, past the boarded window of the summer ice-cream shop. Somewhere a delivery van reversed, its ordinary warning strangely gentle at this hour.

Under the pier, the place where the cardboard box had stood was empty. Water moved between the pilings.

“Here,” he whispered. “This is where.”

He had expected the sight to undo him. Instead he remembered how irritated he had been by the damp inside his shoes that first morning. How he had nearly turned back before hearing the scrape inside the box.

He climbed to the sheltered bench at the landward end of the pier and sat with Juniper across his lap. Her eyes were half open. He tucked the towel around her back, leaving her face free.

Dr. Okafor arrived carrying a small bag. She sat beside him rather than standing over them.

“May I?”

He nodded.

She listened to Juniper’s chest, touched her gently, then explained again what would happen.

“The first medicine will help her relax and sleep. You can keep holding her.”

“All the time?”

“All the time.”

While the sedative took effect, Theo rubbed the place between Juniper’s ears. He felt her body ease against his arm. There was a faint vibration beneath his fingers; he could not tell whether it was a purr or only her breathing.

“You don’t have to do anything,” he told her. “Not even that.”

The vet waited until Juniper was deeply asleep before placing a small catheter in her foreleg. Theo watched her hands. He was grateful that someone knew how to do this, and hated that it was something anyone needed to know.

“Are you ready?”

“No,” he said.

Dr. Okafor waited.

He bent and touched his mouth to the soft fur above Juniper’s eye.

“But I’m here.”

As the final medicine entered, a memory came without warning: his mother sitting beside his childhood bed after a nightmare, her palm warm between his shoulder blades.

You’re all right. I’ve got you.

Then another: Juniper, newly home, asleep in the hollow of his elbow while he sat afraid to move. His mother had never seen her. There should have been no way for those moments to belong together, yet for an instant he felt the same quiet in both—the permission to rest because someone else was keeping watch.

Juniper breathed out.

He waited for the next breath.

Dr. Okafor’s fingers rested lightly against her chest, then her stethoscope. The sea knocked softly beneath the boards.

“She’s gone, Theo.”

He bowed over her.

“Thank you,” he said into her fur. “Thank you. Thank you.”

There was no courage in how he cried. His nose ran; his shoulders shook. He kept one hand cupped beneath her head.

Dr. Okafor stayed until he was able to lift his face. When he was ready, she helped him wrap Juniper in the towel and took her into her care.

Walking home with his coat empty was the hardest part.

***

For a week, he left the basket where it was.

He washed the food dishes but could not put them away. Twice he woke to the certainty that she needed lifting. Once he laughed at something on the radio and stopped so suddenly that the laugh hurt.

Anya brought soup and stayed to eat some. Ezra came to fix the loose garden gate, although Theo had not asked him. Neither tried to make the house less empty by talking.

The last university reply arrived. Theo read its careful regret, thanked the sender, and closed the computer.

When Dr. Okafor called, he went to collect the small container himself.

***

The next clear morning, fog lay low along the water.

Theo returned to the pier. He waited on the steps below it until the incoming tide reached the lowest one. Beyond the harbor mouth, sunlight began to loosen the gray.

He opened the container close to the surface, out of the wind.

Some of the ashes rested briefly on the water. Then the tide took them beneath its small folds.

He had thought there might be something he ought to say. Her name, perhaps. A promise. But he had spoken to her every day of her life with him, and at the end he had stayed. He watched until there was nothing left to distinguish from the sea.

On the walk back, he stopped at the café.

Anya set down his coffee.

“Toast?”

“Yes,” he said. “Please.”

He ate by the window while the town opened around him: keys turning in locks, a dog resisting its lead, Ezra crossing the street with a newspaper tucked under his arm.

At home, Theo moved the basket beside his desk. He was not ready to lose sight of it.

Then he carried a canvas into the kitchen, where the light was best.

He mixed gray, then wiped it away and began again with blue. There had been so much blue that morning, hidden inside what he had first mistaken for colorlessness.

The first marks were clumsy. He let them remain. By noon there was the suggestion of a pier, a strip of water, an uncertain brightness beyond it. He washed his brush, made himself lunch, and came back.

That evening he opened the diary.

For a while he looked at the words from their beach day. Below them, on a clean page, he wrote:

*She was never mine to keep. She was mine to love. That was the whole assignment.*
