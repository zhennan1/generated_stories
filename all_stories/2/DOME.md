After the storm, the harbor seemed to be holding its breath. Rain dripped from the iron railings, and shallow pools trembled beneath the gray morning light. Rowan Ellis came down the steps with their coat collar turned up and their hands in their pockets. The wind had driven waves against the seawall all night; now it worried at loose signs and carried the sharp smell of salt and wet stone through the empty streets.

Rowan was halfway to the bakery when they heard a thin, rasping cry.

The sound came again beneath the harbor steps. A kitten crouched between a mooring rope and a rain-swollen crate, tucked into the narrow strip of shelter above the tide. His ears looked too large for his head, and a crooked white blaze ran down his nose. When Rowan crouched, he flattened himself against the stone, bright eyes fixed on them.

“Hello,” Rowan said.

The kitten hissed. Rowan offered the end of a sleeve instead of a hand. He stared at the wool, whiskers trembling, then struck at the cuff with one small paw and caught the knit with his claws.

“All right,” Rowan murmured. “You’ve made your position clear.”

A gull cried above them. The kitten flinched, claws digging into the sleeve. Rowan felt the tug of hesitation: rent due in a week, bills on the kitchen table, work hours that never seemed to stretch as far as the month. But the kitten was shivering. When he tried to stand, one hind leg slipped on the wet stone.

Rowan wrapped him in their scarf and lifted him against their chest. He protested in a furious, threadbare voice, but his small body shook beneath the damp fur. Rowan turned away from the water and hurried toward the veterinary practice.

The waiting room smelled of disinfectant, and the clock above the desk ticked steadily while the kitten watched every movement. Dr. Leena Shah brought them into an examination room. She weighed him, checked his eyes and mouth, listened to his heart, and ran careful fingers over his ribs and legs. He endured the examination stiffly, catching at her sleeve when she touched his belly.

Dr. Shah waited for him to let go. “He’s cold and very hungry,” she said, “but I can’t find an immediate emergency. I don’t see an obvious injury, and his breathing sounds clear. Keep him warm, offer small amounts of food and water, and watch him closely over the next few days. Call me if he becomes lethargic, stops eating, or seems uncomfortable.”

Rowan repeated the instructions quietly, fixing them in memory. The kitten had no collar or microchip. Dr. Shah gave Rowan a tin of food and her card, then suggested asking around in case he had slipped away from someone during the storm.

“I will,” Rowan said.

At the flat, Rowan set a cardboard box by the radiator and lined it with an old towel. They put water within reach and watched the kitten step out of the scarf to investigate. He ate two bites, paused, then ate several more. The room was quiet except for the radiator’s uneven clank and rain tapping at the window.

Rowan moved the box closer to the warmth and draped a blanket over half of it to make a den. The kitten glanced inside, then at Rowan.

“I’m going to make tea,” Rowan said. “You can decide whether that meets your standards.”

They crossed to the kitchenette. The kettle clicked on. When Rowan returned, the kitten had left the box. His small, damp prints led to the chair beside the radiator, where he had curled into the hollow Rowan had just vacated. His chin rested on one paw; his eyes were already closed.

Rowan stood with the mug warming their hands. A laugh rose in their throat, quiet and unexpected. They let it out as a breath. One ear twitched, but the kitten stayed asleep.

That afternoon, Rowan began looking for an owner. At the harbor bakery, they asked the woman behind the counter, who shook her head and promised to keep an eye out. Rowan checked the noticeboard by the post office and the cards in the grocer’s window. There were notices for a missing dog and a set of lost keys, but nothing about a kitten. At the veterinary practice, Rowan left their name and number in case someone called.

For the next couple of days, Rowan listened for the phone. It did not ring.

The kitten ate, slept, and watched the flat from the warm chair. He ignored the cardboard box except when it held food. At night, he chose the folded coat Rowan had left by the radiator. Whenever Rowan stood up, he waited a little, then took the place they had warmed.

Rowan told themself it was temporary while laying a clean towel over the chair. They would find his owner, they said, while adding food to the grocery list and moving the bills into a neater pile. It was a practical arrangement, though the kitten’s small presence had already begun to alter the silence of the flat.

On the third morning, Rowan sat at the kitchen table with the veterinary card beside their cup. The kitten slept in the warm dip of their chair, oversized ears slack, crooked blaze bright against his dark fur. Outside, a few gulls picked through the tideline, and the bakery had opened its blue door to the smell of fresh bread.

Rowan glanced at the quiet phone. No one had claimed him. No one had asked where he was.

They had not named him. That could wait, Rowan thought, reaching for their tea. In the chair, the kitten stirred, pressed his nose deeper into the place Rowan had left warm, and slept on.

The name came on the fifth morning, when the kitten hauled a tea towel off the oven rail and tried to drag it beneath the table.

“Leave that alone, you little—Pip.”

Rowan stopped. The kitten looked up, one paw firmly planted on his prize. There had been no list of names, no decision to make him less temporary. But the small, brisk sound suited him.

“Pip,” Rowan repeated.

He bit the towel and pulled.

The bakery had pinned a notice beside the counter, and Rowan had checked again at the veterinary practice. Still nobody called. They kept their phone beside them at work, its volume turned up beneath the bookshop’s discreet rustling and creaking. Each time it rang, their stomach tightened. Each time, it was someone wanting a book ordered or asking whether they had renewed the electricity tariff.

By the end of the second week, the notice at the bakery had curled at one corner.

That evening, Rowan spread the bills across the kitchen table. Pip crouched on the opposite chair, watching the end of their pencil.

Rent first. Electricity. Groceries. Then food, litter, veterinary care.

Rowan checked the tenancy agreement again, although they already knew it allowed a cat. They crossed out a planned purchase of winter boots; the old pair could be repaired. There was enough for the ordinary expenses, with a little set aside each month. The uncertainty lay in everything that might happen unexpectedly.

Pip reached across the table and put a paw over the pencil.

“This is about you,” Rowan told him.

He pressed harder.

At the follow-up appointment, Dr. Leena Shah weighed him, listened to his chest, and examined his eyes and mouth. Rowan stood close enough to catch him if he attempted to step off the table.

“He’s gained weight,” she said. “That’s encouraging. Eating well?”

“Very well. He’s started supervising mine.”

“And the litter tray?”

“Normal, as far as I can tell.”

Leena nodded. There was still no microchip to identify him, and nobody had contacted the practice about a missing kitten. She outlined his vaccinations and the care he would need as he grew. Rowan asked her to write down the costs.

“Keep watching him,” she added. “If he stops eating, becomes unusually quiet, or his breathing changes, call us. Don’t wait just because he seemed well today.”

At reception, Rowan gave his name.

“Pip,” they said. Then, when asked to confirm the owner’s details, they gave their own without explaining that the arrangement was temporary.

Outside, they stood a moment with the carrier against their knee. Through the grille, Pip was washing a foot.

“Well,” Rowan said. “Home, then.”

The harbor was entering its quieter season. Pleasure boats disappeared from their summer moorings, leaving pale ropes tapping against empty pontoons. At the bookshop, long stretches passed without the bell above the door sounding. Rowan straightened shelves already straight and watched wind chase a paper receipt along the pavement.

They went home at lunchtime whenever the rota allowed. At first, they entered listening for trouble. Usually they found Pip asleep in a place he had rejected while Rowan was there to offer it: the towel-lined box, a folded jumper, the sunny corner beside the window.

He inspected them on arrival, then inspected his bowl.

His nights were less orderly.

One morning, before the sky had lightened, Rowan woke to a small paw touching their lower lip.

“No.”

The paw withdrew. A moment later, Pip climbed onto their chest and began attacking the loose edge of the pillowcase.

“It isn’t breakfast yet.”

Pip waited until Rowan closed their eyes, then pounced again.

They fed him at the usual time rather than the hour he proposed, but made their own breakfast with the exhausted concentration of someone handling machinery. While they turned to pour water into the teapot, a chair scraped softly behind them.

Rowan looked back.

Pip stood on the table with a piece of egg in his mouth.

They stared at each other.

“That,” Rowan said, removing the plate, “was mine.”

He swallowed.

For the rest of breakfast, Rowan ate standing up. Pip sat below, apparently hopeful that this new arrangement might improve his chances.

That evening, a scrap torn from an envelope became an enemy worth pursuing beneath every chair. Pip stalked it with his belly almost touching the floor. He waited, ears tilted forward, while Rowan nudged it with one finger. Then he sprang sideways, missed entirely, and hurried behind a table leg.

Rowan laughed before they could stop themself.

Pip emerged, seized the paper, and carried it away with such determination that Rowan let him keep it.

Later, they sat in the armchair with a book. Pip lingered near the radiator. Rowan patted the cushion beside them once.

He watched their hand.

“All right,” they said, and returned to reading.

For several minutes there was only the faint ticking of the radiator and the occasional sound of a car on the wet road outside. Rowan read the same paragraph twice, resisting the urge to look up.

A small weight landed beside their thigh.

They kept still.

Pip turned once, kneaded the worn cushion, and lowered himself into the narrow space between Rowan and the chair arm. His shoulder rested against their side. When Rowan shifted their wrist to turn a page, he lifted his head, considered the movement, and settled again.

The trust of it made Rowan unexpectedly cautious. They lowered the book without reaching for him.

A cheap notebook lay on the side table. Rowan had bought it to keep track of meals and anything Dr. Shah might need to know. With Pip asleep beside them, they opened it and began.

Thursday.

I gave him breakfast at seven. He ate everything. Normal litter tray. No coughing or sneezing. Slept while I was at work, I think. Stole some egg. Must move the kitchen chair away from the table.

They paused, listening to the faint, uneven beginnings of a purr.

Played with paper this evening. He came and sat beside me without being asked.

The pen rested against the page. Then they added:

He was much warmer than I expected.

Rowan considered drawing a line through the last sentence. It was hardly useful information for the vet.

Instead, they closed the notebook carefully. Outside, the last visitors were leaving the harbor, their voices thinning along the quay. Pip stretched one foreleg across Rowan’s wrist, and Rowan left their hand where it was.

By Friday morning, Rowan had learned to put the kettle on after filling Pip’s bowl, not before.

If they reached for the kettle first, he followed them across the kitchen, offering a series of short, disbelieving cries. Once breakfast was down, he ate with sufficient concentration to suggest that he had never complained about anything in his life.

“You could give me thirty seconds,” Rowan said.

Pip continued eating.

Outside, rain silvered the harbor railings. The fishing boats shifted against their ropes, and a delivery van rattled over the uneven road below. Rowan drank their tea standing up, watching Pip investigate a crumb beneath the cupboard.

Before leaving for the bookshop, they checked his water and litter tray. They straightened the towel in the box by the window, although he had ignored it since discovering the armchair.

“There’s an excellent bed here,” they told him.

Pip sat on their folded jumper and began washing one forepaw.

The bookshop closed at four during the quieter months. Rowan had once found the hour inconveniently early: too late to begin anything substantial, too early to call the day finished. Now, when they turned the sign and checked the back door, they knew what they would do next.

They walked home by the grocer’s, bought milk and a tin of tomatoes, and resisted adding a second errand across town. The cupboard could wait for new shelf paper. Pip would be listening to the stairwell.

Or perhaps he wouldn’t. Rowan reminded themself of this as they unlocked the flat.

He was waiting halfway down the passage.

“Well,” they said, trying not to sound too pleased. “Hello.”

He brushed their trouser leg, then went directly to the kitchen.

“Of course.”

The days began to acquire small, dependable hinges. Breakfast, the window opened briefly while Rowan cleaned the tray, the scrape of the key in the lock. After work came food, washing-up, and a period during which Pip supervised whatever Rowan attempted from a position that made it less convenient.

He disliked the cushion placed beside the radiator but approved of the narrow strip of bare floor in front of it. He accepted the towel-lined box when sunlight reached its corner and abandoned it as soon as the light moved. The jumper remained popular, especially after Rowan had worn it.

On Saturday evening, Rowan stood from the armchair to fetch their glasses.

When they returned, Pip occupied the cushion.

He had arranged himself diagonally, with the crooked white blaze of his face pointing toward the lamp.

“I was sitting there.”

His ears moved, acknowledging the sound without conceding its meaning.

Rowan fetched a dining chair.

From that less comfortable position, they noticed the string beside the armchair’s rear leg: a short length of thick cotton cord, untied from a parcel earlier in the week.

On Tuesday, before Pip had first settled beside them in the chair, Rowan had dropped that string while clearing the table.

They remembered the afternoon precisely. Rain had tapped at the kitchen window. A damp tea towel hung over the oven handle, and Rowan had been wondering whether the flat smelled permanently of cat food.

The string had slipped from their fingers.

Pip, crossing toward his water bowl, stopped.

At first Rowan thought they had startled him. Then his oversized ears tipped forward. He lowered himself until his belly almost touched the floor, eyes fixed on the loose end.

Rowan picked it up and drew it slowly behind a chair leg.

Pip crept forward. One paw rose and remained suspended.

“Very fearsome,” Rowan whispered.

He sprang too soon, caught nothing, and landed with both front feet on the place the string had just occupied. For a moment he stared at the floor. Then he wheeled around and pursued it beneath the table, hindquarters skidding.

Rowan laughed aloud.

The sound surprised them enough to interrupt the game. It seemed unusually large in the quiet flat.

Pip looked up, impatient.

“Sorry,” Rowan said, and moved the string again.

Now, on Saturday evening, the cord lay where they had put it after another game.

Rowan reached behind the chair and retrieved it. Pip lifted his head.

“Oh, you’re awake for this.”

They trailed the end across the rug. He watched it travel, descended from the stolen cushion, and crouched behind the table leg with his ears pitched forward.

Rowan waited. They knew better now than to spoil the approach by moving too quickly.

His whiskers came first, then one careful paw.

“There you are.”

He pounced, trapped the string, and looked briefly astonished when it stopped resisting.

Rowan let him hold it before drawing it away again. Afterward, they wound it loosely and put it in the side-table drawer, where he could not swallow it while they were out.

There had been a time, only days earlier, when everything brought into the flat for Pip had seemed provisional: bowls that could be washed and put away, a box that could be flattened. But Rowan knew which drawer held his toy now, and how slowly to move it behind the chair.

On Sunday they put the favored jumper back beside the window instead of into the laundry basket.

“It’s mostly clean,” they said.

Pip pressed his nose into a sleeve.

When Rowan read, they sat slightly off-center, leaving the space between their hip and the chair arm unoccupied. Sometimes he filled it. Sometimes he chose the floor, close enough that Rowan could hear him washing, beyond reach of their hand.

Once, when they stroked him, he ducked neatly away.

“All right.”

He remained nearby.

The notebook stayed on the side table. Rowan still recorded what he ate and whether the litter tray looked normal. Those were the reasons they had bought it.

But other observations began slipping between them.

Watched a gull for eleven minutes.

Dislikes the blue dish unless hungry.

Rowan paused over that one, then added: Perhaps dislikes being watched while deciding.

On Sunday night they got up for a glass of water. Behind them came the soft thump of paws landing on upholstery.

When they returned, Pip was turning once on the cushion.

Rowan opened the notebook and wrote beneath the evening’s meal:

Stole my chair before the cushion had cooled.

They read it back, left it uncrossed-out, and pulled the dining chair closer to the lamp.

On Monday, Pip let the string pass him twice before reaching for it.

Rowan drew it behind the chair leg, where it usually became irresistible. His ears tilted forward. He crouched, gathered himself, then sat up and began washing one paw.

“Not convincing today?”

They tried once more. Pip caught the string, held it briefly, and walked away.

The flat was warm. He had spent the afternoon watching pigeons. Rowan wound the string and put it safely in the drawer.

At supper, a little food remained around the edge of his dish. Rowan scraped it together, then stopped. He was entitled to be less hungry at one meal.

On Tuesday he played again, but paused between pounces. On Wednesday he left more supper.

By Thursday, Rowan had run out of separate explanations.

They opened the notebook beneath the lamp. Between notes about deliveries and Pip’s dislike of the blue dish were three unfinished meals and two abbreviated games. Individually, each entry looked harmless. Read together, they were harder to dismiss.

Rowan telephoned the practice before they could turn another page.

***

On Friday, after the bookshop’s early closing, they carried Pip through streets smelling of wet leaves.

He complained at the first corner, then settled. Through the carrier grille, his crooked white blaze looked unusually bright against the dim interior.

Dr. Shah listened while Rowan described what had changed. She asked when they had first noticed it, whether he was drinking differently, whether there had been vomiting or changes in the litter tray.

“No. Nothing dramatic.”

“It doesn’t have to be dramatic to be worth investigating.”

She weighed Pip, examined his mouth, and felt carefully along his body. When she listened to his chest, she shifted the stethoscope and listened again.

Rowan watched her face.

“There’s an extra heart sound I want to investigate,” she said. “It doesn’t tell us the cause by itself. I’d recommend blood tests and an ultrasound of his heart.”

“Could he just be tired?”

“He could have a quieter day for many ordinary reasons. But with the pattern you’ve noticed, I wouldn’t be comfortable assuming that.”

Rowan nodded. A useful answer, even though it was not the one they had wanted.

***

The scan was arranged for Monday. The results would follow.

Meanwhile, there were bowls to wash, orders to unpack, and a customer who wanted a book whose title contained either *winter* or *water*.

At home, Pip stole Rowan’s chair while they were putting the kettle on. He lay along the cushion with one forepaw extended, occupying more space than seemed physically reasonable.

“You’ve got a perfectly good jumper.”

He closed his eyes.

Later, Rowan shut the bathroom door. Pip objected with two sharp cries and pushed a paw beneath it.

“I’m coming.”

When they emerged, he walked away as though access had been the entire point.

These were the things Rowan knew about him: the particular complaints, the opportunism, the oversized ears turning towards a cupboard door. Beside them, the longer naps seemed difficult to understand. Sometimes Rowan wondered whether they had exaggerated everything. Then Pip stopped halfway through a game, and they remembered the appointment.

***

At Wednesday’s consultation, Dr. Shah had the scan report beside her.

“The findings show restrictive cardiomyopathy,” she said. “It’s a rare form of heart disease. Part of the heart has become stiff, so it can’t relax and fill as it should.”

Rowan looked down at Pip. He was investigating a fold in the towel inside his carrier.

“Can that be repaired?”

“No. There isn’t a cure.”

The room stayed ordinary. A printer started beyond the door. Someone laughed briefly in the corridor.

Dr. Shah waited before continuing.

“As it progresses, it will limit his energy. Treatment can reduce some risks and help keep him comfortable, but it can’t reverse the disease.”

“How long?”

“I can’t give you a reliable timescale for Pip. The scan tells us what’s happening, but not exactly how quickly it will progress.”

Rowan pressed their thumb against the carrier handle.

Dr. Shah explained the initial medication, including one to reduce the risk of blood clots because a chamber of his heart was enlarged. She wrote down the dose and arranged a review for the following week.

She showed Rowan how to count his breathing while he slept and marked the point at which they should call. Increased effort, open-mouthed breathing, collapse, or sudden weakness meant urgent help. A continuing drop in appetite also warranted a call.

Rowan asked whether the medicine needed food. What if he missed a dose? Should they stop playing with him?

“Let him set the pace. Offer things he enjoys, but don’t encourage him to keep going when he stops.”

“He jumped onto my chair yesterday.”

Dr. Shah nodded.

“And he still plays sometimes. Properly plays.”

“That can happen. Enjoyable moments can coexist with this illness. They don’t mean the scan is wrong, and the diagnosis doesn’t mean he can’t enjoy them.”

Rowan looked at the written instructions again.

There were several questions they could ask about a tablet. They asked those.

***

Back at the flat, Pip ate a little, washed his face, and inspected the window.

Rowan sat beneath the lamp with the practice’s papers on their knees. Outside, a bus sighed at the stop. The room had cooled while they were out; they turned the heating up one notch.

Their notebook was still in the coat pocket where they had put it for the appointment.

When they returned from fetching it, Pip was stepping onto the cushion they had left. He turned once and lowered himself carefully.

Rowan pulled over the dining chair.

11 October. I took him for the examination. Blood tests arranged.

14 October. I took him for the heart scan.

16 October. I was told the diagnosis. Restrictive cardiomyopathy. No cure.

I have the doses written on the practice sheet. I need to count his breathing when he is asleep, let him stop playing when he wants, and call about the changes Dr. Shah marked. Review next Wednesday.

I asked about missed tablets. I forgot to ask what to do if he spits one out.

I keep rearranging the facts as though the right order might make them negotiable.

Rowan put down the pen.

Beside them, Pip tucked his nose beneath his tail. One ear remained turned towards the small sounds of Rowan moving, while the rest of him settled into the warmth they had left behind.

Story:

For the next several days, Rowan followed the instructions on the practice sheet. They gave Pip his prescribed care at the appointed times, counted his breaths while he slept, and stopped offering play when he turned away. They noted each change in the notebook, even the ones that seemed too small to deserve a line.

At first there were still ordinary things to write down. Pip stole Rowan’s chair after they stood to make tea. He ate a little from his breakfast bowl. One afternoon, he settled near the radiator, his oversized ears angled towards the click of the heating. Rowan tried to let these moments be what they were: good moments, not promises.

Then the bowl remained fuller. Pip sniffed his food and took only a few bites, even when Rowan warmed it slightly and set it beside his cushion. He began moving as though each change of place required a decision. The chair was still his, but he did not always climb onto it. When Rowan brought his cushion nearer the radiator, he tried to settle there, turned once, and stood again. He shifted onto the rug, then away from it, unable to find a position that lasted.

The flat seemed to have developed too many small obstacles. The low table stood where it always had; now Rowan found themself moving it back from Pip’s path. They carried the water bowl closer. They folded the blanket differently, then unfolded it. Pip made no complaint. That, Rowan was beginning to understand, was not the same as being comfortable.

On the fourth morning, after Pip left most of his food untouched, Rowan opened the notebook. Their writing had become cramped at the edges of the page.

Breakfast: a few bites. Would not settle by radiator. Changed position several times. Walked from cushion to rug, stopped halfway.

They stared at the last sentence. There was no measurement for stopping halfway.

The routine review was still several days away. Rowan called the practice and described what had changed. They tried to keep their voice level, listing observations in order, as if clarity might produce a useful instruction. The receptionist asked them to bring Pip in that afternoon.

At the practice, Dr. Shah listened while Rowan read from the notebook. Pip sat in his carrier with his head turned towards the opening. The crooked white blaze on his face seemed sharper beneath the bright examination-room light. He did not struggle when Dr. Shah examined him, but he did not lean into her hand as he sometimes had. Rowan watched the small movements: the pause before he shifted, the careful placement of his paws.

“Is there another adjustment we’ve missed?” Rowan asked. “A different dose, another medicine? I can move things again. I can put his food anywhere.”

Dr. Shah took time before answering. She asked about his breathing, whether he had seemed acutely distressed, and how often he was eating. Rowan answered, then described the cushions and the radiator, the bowl, the chair he no longer climbed onto every time.

“You’ve made sensible changes,” Dr. Shah said. “And the treatment has helped in some ways. But his condition is worsening despite that. From what you’re describing, and what I’m seeing today, his discomfort isn’t something we can reliably ease now.”

Rowan felt the familiar impulse to search her words for an opening. “But he still eats sometimes. He still comes to sit near me.”

“Yes,” Dr. Shah said. “Those moments matter. They’re real. This isn’t only about the diagnosis being incurable. It’s about what his days are like, including the hours between those moments.”

She explained that they could talk through further tests or adjustments, but she could not promise either would reveal a useful option. She did not make waiting sound harmless. If Pip continued to struggle to eat and settle, she said, Rowan might be asking him to bear more discomfort than his remaining enjoyable moments could justify. She recommended considering a timely goodbye.

The word was quiet, almost ordinary. Rowan’s mind refused it anyway.

They asked what signs would mean Pip was suffering more. Dr. Shah answered as plainly as she could, and acknowledged that no single observation would settle the question. Rowan asked whether the fact that Pip still sought their company meant it was too soon. Dr. Shah said it meant he still wanted them; it did not tell them everything about how he felt when he was alone or unable to rest.

There were questions Rowan had not known they were waiting to ask. How would they know they hadn’t acted too early? What if he had another good morning? Dr. Shah did not pretend there was a certain answer that would remove the doubt. She said Rowan knew Pip’s ordinary habits better than anyone, and that choosing to prevent further distress could be an act of care even while it hurt.

On the way home, Rowan held the carrier with both hands. The pavement was wet from a brief shower; tyres hissed along the road, and a bus sighed at the stop. Pip was still. At the flat, Rowan set the carrier down and opened its door. He came out slowly, paused, then crossed to the rug.

He tried to lower himself. One shoulder dipped; he stopped, rose again, and turned towards the cushion. Rowan did not lift him or adjust the cushion beneath him. They sat on the floor nearby, close enough that he would not need to cross the room if he wanted them.

The question had changed without Rowan giving it permission. For days they had been asking what they could alter so that Pip could stay as he was. Now they watched him fail to settle, and wondered what staying was costing him.

Eli arrived that evening with groceries and put them away without asking where anything belonged. Rowan told him what Dr. Shah had said. They admitted they were afraid of saying goodbye unnecessarily early, and equally afraid of waiting because they could not bear to choose.

Eli sat beside them on the floor. “I’m here,” he said.

He did not promise it would be all right. Rowan was grateful for that, though gratitude did nothing to soften the ache in their chest. Pip rested a short distance away, his eyes half closed, one ear turning at the sound of their voices.

Rowan did not call the practice to make an appointment. Not that night. They stayed within reach and let Pip rest, trying to understand what another day of waiting would mean for him.

By morning, Rowan had slept in pieces on the sofa. Pip lay on the rug where he had finally settled during the night. When Rowan put down fresh water, he lifted his head but did not get up.

They sat near him with the telephone in their hand. They had imagined that making the call would require certainty. There was none—only yesterday’s assessment, the untouched food, and the effort Pip spent finding somewhere to rest. Rowan no longer wanted to wait for a crisis to make the decision for them.

When Dr. Shah came on the line, Rowan said, “I think we need to come today.”

Their voice sounded unexpectedly ordinary.

“All right,” she said. “We can arrange a quiet room late this morning. You can come straight through when you arrive.”

She explained that Pip would receive medicine to help him relax and sleep before the final injection. Rowan could remain with him throughout. There would be time afterward, too; they would not have to leave immediately.

“Bring anything familiar that you’d like him to have.”

Rowan looked towards the blanket folded over the sofa arm.

Eli arrived while they were getting the carrier ready. He asked whether they wanted him to come, and Rowan nodded before they could speak.

They took the blanket down. Pip had stolen it on his first night, working it away from Rowan until they were left with one cold knee and a kitten occupying the rest. The fabric was faded now, its edge softened by washing. Rowan folded it loosely rather than shaking it out. They wanted it to smell of the flat.

They prepared no special breakfast. Pip did not need another thing offered and refused.

At the practice, a nurse opened the side door before Rowan had to negotiate the waiting room. Somewhere beyond the corridor, a telephone rang and was answered. The ordinary business of the building continued, quietly out of sight.

The room had a wide padded bench and a window overlooking a narrow strip of garden. Rowan spread the blanket while Dr. Shah helped ease Pip from the carrier.

Eli stayed by the door.

“Here?” he asked. “Or just outside?”

“Outside for now.”

“I’ll be there.”

Dr. Shah examined Pip gently, then sat where Rowan could see her without turning away from him.

“The first injection will make him very sleepy,” she explained. “Once he’s deeply asleep, I’ll give the medicine that stops his heart. I’ll check him carefully before we go on, and afterward I’ll listen to confirm that he’s died.”

“Can I keep my hand here?”

Rowan’s fingers rested beside Pip’s cheek.

“Yes. You can keep touching him.”

She went through the consent form with them rather than simply indicating where to sign. Rowan read the words twice. Signing felt different from speaking on the telephone, though nothing about Pip’s condition had changed between the two.

“Would you like some time before we begin?” Dr. Shah asked.

“Yes.”

She showed them the bell beside the bench and left.

For a while Rowan listened to Pip breathing. A car door closed somewhere beyond the garden wall. On the blanket, a pale hair lay caught across a darker thread.

They moved their hand until the side of one finger touched the soft place beneath his ear. Pip did not turn towards it. Rowan left it there.

“You stole my egg,” they said softly.

Their mouth trembled around the small accusation.

“I looked away for practically no time.”

They could remember the plate, his determination, their own astonishment that something so small could be so certain about what belonged to him.

“After that, you thought breakfast was a shared arrangement.”

Pip’s eyes remained partly closed.

“And the chair. You never even waited for it to cool.”

Rowan stroked him once with their thumb, then let their hand become still. They did not call his name to make him look up. They did not search for a purr or offer food from their fingers. He had spent enough effort on things that used to be easy.

There were other stories, but Rowan did not need to tell them all. Pip could rest while they were quiet.

After a while, they pressed the bell.

Dr. Shah returned and knelt beside the bench.

“May I give him the first medicine now?”

Rowan looked at Pip, then back at her.

“Yes.”

She worked gently and told them when it was done. As Pip grew sleepier, Rowan kept their hand in the same place. His body gradually rested more fully against the blanket.

Dr. Shah checked him and waited a little longer.

“He’s deeply asleep now,” she said. “Are you ready for me to continue?”

Rowan wanted to say that they were not ready for any of it. They wanted this room to belong to an ordinary appointment, with Pip coming home afterward, displeased by the journey.

“Yes,” they said. “Please.”

They stayed beside him while Dr. Shah gave the final medicine. Nothing was required of Pip. Rowan watched the familiar shape of his head beneath their hand.

Afterward, Dr. Shah listened carefully.

“He’s died,” she said quietly. “I’m so sorry, Rowan.”

They nodded, though the words seemed to take time to reach them.

Dr. Shah left them alone.

Rowan kept their hand against Pip. He was still warm. They knew what had happened and found themselves unable to understand it for more than a moment at a time.

When they were ready to leave, they rang for the nurse, who helped them ease the blanket free. Rowan folded it slowly, matching the corners, then folding it once more because their hands needed something to finish.

Eli stood when they came into the corridor. He did not ask whether they were all right.

“Can you stay tonight?” Rowan asked.

“Yes.”

They took his arm. They could not yet think as far as the flat, or the place beside the radiator. For now, with the blanket held against them, they let him open the door and went through it with him.

Rowan set the folded blanket on the chair beside the radiator.

It had been folded once at the practice, then again because their hands had needed to finish something. Now it rested on the chair with its corners aligned, a small, deliberate shape in the room. Rowan did not unfold it. They took off their coat and hung it by the door. Eli did the same, then stood quietly while Rowan looked around the flat as if it had been rearranged in their absence.

Nothing had moved. That was the trouble.

The water glass Rowan had left on the table was still there. The book lay open, facedown, where they had set it aside. Pip’s toy remained near the skirting board, a little distance from the radiator. No small body came out from under the chair to investigate the cold air let in by the door. Rowan’s eyes went to the floor anyway.

Eli asked if they wanted tea.

“In a minute,” Rowan said.

The bowl was clean. Rowan remembered washing it before they left, rinsing away the last crumbs and setting it upside down to dry. They turned it upright now, though there was no reason to. The ceramic made a soft click against the counter. They lifted it, held it between both hands, and put it in the cupboard.

The cupboard door swung back before it caught. Rowan opened it again and shifted the bowl farther in, beside the plates. The motion was slow enough to feel like a decision. They closed the door with care.

The litter tray was next. It stood where it always had, clean and empty. Rowan had cleaned it before going to the practice; they had wanted to come home to one less thing waiting. The scoop hung from its hook. Rowan took it down, then set it back. They folded the liner over the rim, paused, and removed it. There was nothing to throw away. The new liner made a faint plastic whisper as they flattened it into place.

Eli stayed by the table. He did not reach for the tray or say that Rowan could leave it until morning. His presence made the room less empty without making it feel occupied. Rowan could hear him breathe, and the small sounds of their own work: the cupboard latch, the liner, a tap running briefly as they washed their hands.

They dried their hands and stood at the sink. Beyond the window, the harbor had darkened into a broken plane of slate and black. The tide washed against the steps, withdrew, and returned. From here the sound was low and even, as if the water had been following this same pattern for years and would continue whatever happened in the flat.

Rowan went to the table and opened the notebook.

They had written three entries before this one. Each had begun with the date and some detail they could hold: a meal Pip had eaten, a question for Leena, a change they had noticed. The first line of this entry came easily enough.

I put the bowl away. I changed the liner in the tray. The scoop is hanging on its hook. Pip’s toy is still by the wall.

Rowan stopped. The pen had pressed a dot into the paper where they had held it too long.

They looked at the neat list. It was accurate. It accounted for the things that could be moved and cleaned and returned to their places. It did not account for what happened after they stood up, when their gaze went automatically to the floor beside the chair. It did not account for the pause before they remembered there would be no quick turn of a head, no whiskers in the dimness, no soft weight finding its way against their ankle.

Rowan lowered the pen again.

The flat is too quiet. It is not peaceful. I can hear every small thing and none of it is the sound I keep expecting. I keep thinking I should be able to do something about that. I can wash the bowl and change the liner and put the toy away, but the silence stays where it is. It is unbearable, and I do not know what to do with it.

The words blurred. Rowan read them once, then set the pen down.

Eli came closer, but did not touch them until Rowan reached for his hand. His fingers closed around theirs. They sat together without trying to fill the room with talk. Rowan’s eyes kept drifting to the chair where the folded blanket rested. It seemed both carefully kept and strangely out of place, as though it belonged to a day the flat had not yet understood was over.

At some point Eli made tea. Rowan drank a little because the cup was warm and because he had made it. The heat touched their palms, then faded. They could not remember what they said, if they said anything.

When they finally went to bed, Eli settled on the other side, leaving Rowan space without making a show of it. The sheets shifted as he lay down. Rowan stared into the dark, listening to the building’s pipes and the muffled harbor beyond the window. They imagined standing up again, going to the kitchen, checking the bowl though it was already away. The urge rose and passed without becoming action.

Morning would come with its ordinary demands: light at the window, water in the kettle, the hour moving forward whether Rowan was ready or not. The tide would keep reaching the harbor steps and sliding back, steady but answerless. For now, Rowan lay beside Eli and faced the next ordinary hour, one breath at a time.

Rowan reached the bookshop before opening. Inside, the air smelled of paper and damp wool; someone had left a scarf drying over the radiator. They hung up their coat and stood behind the counter until the bell above the door stopped trembling. Then they fetched a cloth and began wiping the shelf beneath the window.

Straightening the books required small decisions. A spine pushed back too far. A paperback leaning against its neighbour. The harbor histories needed room, so Rowan shifted the walking guides along by an inch. Their hands could do these things. For a little while, there was only the soft drag of paper against wood and the problem of making a row stand level.

The first customer wanted a book for her sister.

“Something about gardens, but not too practical. She doesn’t actually like doing the work.”

Rowan found two books and opened one to a photograph of a walled garden. The woman considered the price, asked about the other, then chose the first. Rowan wrapped it, counted out her change, and remembered to wish her a good day. Only when the door closed did they notice they had been thinking about the books while they spoke.

Then there was the space before the next customer. Their thoughts went straight to Pip.

At this hour, they would once have wondered whether the kitten was warm enough, whether the towel in the box had slipped. The impulse to check still came, complete and urgent, before memory caught up with it. Rowan put both palms flat on the counter. After a moment, they went back to the shelves.

The hours passed that way: attention briefly occupied, then released into the same absence. Near closing, Rowan discovered a book of poems among the cookery books. They carried it to its proper place. Outside, rain stippled the shop window, making the harbor lights look farther away.

Eli arrived at the flat after dusk with soup in a lidded container and bread in a paper bag. He had offered before leaving that morning, and Rowan had agreed without being able to imagine evening.

“Shall I warm it up?” he asked.

Rowan nodded.

They stood in the kitchen while he found a saucepan. The bag crackled under his hand. He asked where the breadknife was, although he had used it before, and Rowan pointed to the drawer.

They ate at the small table. The soup tasted of lentils and tomatoes, and Rowan managed several spoonfuls before setting the spoon down. Eli ate slowly. He did not ask whether work had helped.

After a while, Rowan said, “I kept forgetting.”

Eli waited.

“Not for long. Just while someone was talking. Then I’d remember again.”

Their mouth tightened around the last word. They looked down at the bread, at the thumbprint they had pressed into its soft centre, and began to cry.

Eli put his spoon down. When Rowan reached across the space between their chairs, he moved closer. His hand rested over theirs without gripping. There was no suggestion to finish eating, no attempt to tell them what the day had meant. The kitchen clock ticked through several minutes.

Beyond his shoulder, the blanket lay folded on the spare chair. Rowan had brought it home from the practice and folded it until the edges matched. Looking at it had meant remembering the room they had left.

Now a loose corner hung over the chair seat, and they remembered Pip tucked against that same edge.

The first time had been in the chair beside the radiator. Rowan had spread the blanket across their knees, expecting the kitten to stay at the far end. Instead, Pip had advanced in cautious stages, one paw pressing down, then another, until a small flank rested against Rowan’s thigh.

For a while the kitten’s ears had followed every sound. A pipe clicked inside the wall; a door shut downstairs. Then the paws loosened. Pip’s head grew heavy against Rowan’s wrist. The warmth was astonishingly particular, concentrated in that small place.

Rowan looked at Eli.

“The first time Pip fell asleep on me, I had that over my knees.”

He glanced towards the chair, then back.

“I thought Pip would move away,” Rowan said. “I could feel the claws through it at first. Not scratching. Just holding on.”

Their voice broke. Eli stayed close while they wiped their face with their free hand. The memory did not soften the fact of coming home without Pip. It made the loss sharper in another way: this had happened, this trust had been given, and Rowan could still feel the place where the weight had rested.

“My foot went numb,” they said. “I wanted to move it, but I thought the blanket would pull. And my tea was right there, going cold.”

Eli’s thumb moved once across their knuckles.

“So I left it. I sat as still as I could. Even when I needed to cough, I tried not to let it shake my arm.”

The soup cooled between them. The blanket was still the one from the practice, but it was also the blanket on Rowan’s knees, holding a kitten who had finally stopped keeping watch. Rowan could not look at it without hurting. They could tell Eli about it, though.

“I waited until Pip woke up,” they said. “I didn’t move until then.”

Eli listened, and when Rowan began again, with the small paw settling against their wrist, he kept listening.

Story:

The first evening Rowan stayed in town past the hour they used to leave, the air had turned sharp enough to make their eyes water. They stood outside the grocer’s with a paper bag tucked under one arm and watched the last of the daylight flatten over the harbour. The sea was the colour of pewter. Wind worried at the shop awning and carried the smell of salt up the street.

At four, Rowan’s attention had gone automatically to the time. At half past, they had caught themself calculating how long it would take to walk home, unlock the door, put the kettle on, and check the bowl. The calculations arrived as faithfully as they ever had. Their body seemed to know the old route even when there was no longer anyone waiting at the end of it.

They went on to Eli’s instead.

He had invited them for supper without making it sound like a rescue. There was bread on the table, a pot of lentils warming on the stove, and two plates set out. Rowan hung their coat by the door. The flat smelled of onions and black pepper. Through the kitchen window, the evening was coming on in a slow blue wash.

Eli asked about the grocer. Rowan told him the pears were bruised and the man behind the counter had insisted they were still good. It was easier to talk about fruit than about the hour. They sat down. Eli passed the bread.

For a little while, they spoke of ordinary things: a loose slate on the roof above the stairwell, the price of heating, whether the harbour bus was running on its winter timetable yet. Rowan’s shoulders gradually came down from around their ears. They ate slowly. The lentils were plain and a little over-salted, and the bread was warm through the middle.

Then Eli nudged the last slice towards them.

Rowan looked at it and laughed.

Eli paused, his spoon halfway to his mouth. “What?”

“I was thinking of Pip.” The name still brought a small catch behind Rowan’s ribs, like a thread snagged on a nail. They waited for it to pass. It didn’t, but they could keep talking around it. “The toast. That morning Pip stole half my breakfast.”

Eli put down his spoon. “The egg?”

“No, the toast. The egg was another crime.” Rowan’s smile came more easily now, though it trembled at the edges. “Pip got this piece that was nearly twice as big as him. Dragged it off the plate and under the table. It took ages. He kept stopping to look over his shoulder, like I might have sent someone after him.”

Eli’s face had begun to change; Rowan could see he remembered the story, or was seeing it as they described it.

“And when Pip got it under there,” Rowan said, “he sat on it.”

Eli gave a startled laugh. “Sat on it?”

“Right on top. Completely still. As if sitting on it made it his. He looked at me through the table legs with this expression of absolute authority. The toast was sticking out on every side.”

Eli laughed again, and this time Rowan joined him. The sound came out too loud in the little kitchen. It surprised them, then loosened something. They saw it plainly: Pip’s narrow back, his ears pricked, one paw planted on the crust as though the toast might make a break for freedom. The absurd, serious possession of it.

For a few breaths, the memory was funny without being anything else.

Then laughter dwindled. Rowan rubbed a thumb along the edge of their glass. The ache returned, not as a punishment for having laughed but as the familiar fact underneath everything. Pip had been there, stealing breakfast and sitting on it. Pip wasn’t there now. One truth did not diminish the other.

“I miss him,” Rowan said.

“I know,” Eli replied.

He didn’t reach across the table or try to find a phrase large enough to cover the silence. Rowan was grateful. They ate the rest of supper, and Eli told them about a neighbour who had tried to carry a chair up the stairs alone. Rowan asked what happened. The evening continued, not healed, just continuing.

When the plates were empty, Rowan gathered them and carried them to the counter. Their hands moved through the familiar motions: glass, spoon, plate. At the table, the last slice of bread lay beneath the lamp’s yellow light.

Rowan glanced under the table.

The space was empty. There were only chair legs and a faint crescent of dust against the skirting board. Still, for one suspended instant, their eyes searched the shadows for a small shape curled around its stolen prize.

They stood with a plate in each hand. The thought came with such force that their fingers tightened against the ceramic. Then they set the plates down carefully, one beside the other.

Eli was rinsing the pot. He didn’t turn, but he said, “I always thought Pip knew exactly what he was doing.”

“Pip thought sitting on something was the same as owning it.”

“Maybe that’s true.”

Rowan smiled again, smaller this time. Outside, wind pressed against the salt-marked window and moved on. They helped Eli dry the pot. After, they stayed for tea, and when they left, they did not hurry down the street. The town was dark, the pavements damp, the sea making its steady sound beyond the houses.

At home, Rowan took off their coat and stood in the quiet flat. The bowl was still put away in the cupboard. They had chosen that, and they did not need to undo it tonight. Pip’s blanket remained folded on the chair. The favourite toy was beside it, untouched.

Rowan set the paper bag on the counter. For a moment they could almost hear the small scrape of claws beneath the table, the determined pause, the solemn little weight settling onto toast.

They let themself remember it. Then they turned on the kettle.

Story:

The kettle clicked off before Rowan remembered why they had put it on. They stood with one hand on the counter, listening to the flat settle around them. The radiator gave its small metal knock. Beyond the window, wind combed the sea into dark, restless folds.

In the weeks that followed, Rowan began to move through the rooms without consulting the care schedule. At first, the change was less a decision than a series of missed turns. They crossed the kitchen without checking the time, passed the chair without measuring how long Pip had been asleep, and walked through the town without calculating when they needed to be home. The lists remained in the diary, their neat columns still full of observations and tasks. But the days no longer arranged themselves around them.

The empty spaces did not disappear. Rowan still glanced down when they opened a cupboard, as if a familiar shape might be waiting at their feet. They still shifted their weight at night to make room for the small body that had once pressed against their ankle. On cold mornings, the radiator warmed the same corner, and the warmth seemed almost purposeful. Rowan could feel the absence most sharply in the habits their body had learned: a hand lowering, a glance turning, an ear listening for the scrape of claws.

They did not put everything away. The food bowl went into the cupboard, where its clean rim stayed hidden behind the plates. Pip’s favorite toy went into the side-table drawer. Rowan kept the blanket folded on the chair, where the cloth had held his warmth and the faint impression of his shape. Each choice felt small and definite. They were not ready to clear the flat, and they did not owe anyone a timetable for becoming ready.

Eli began dropping by now and then. Sometimes he brought bread; sometimes he came with nothing but a question about whether Rowan wanted company. He did not ask them to explain how they were doing before the kettle had even boiled. They learned to sit together without filling every pause. Some visits lasted an hour; others ended after tea. When Eli left, Rowan shut the door and returned to the ordinary work waiting in the flat: rinsing cups, folding laundry, opening a letter, making dinner for one.

One evening, as the days cooled and rain ticked against the salt-marked glass, Eli stayed for a simple meal. Rowan set out two plates. Their hand reached automatically for a third before they caught themself. The pause was brief, but Eli saw it. He did not look away or try to turn it into something easier. Rowan put the extra plate back in the cupboard.

They ate quietly for a while. Then Rowan remembered the toast.

“Do you remember when Pip stole half my breakfast?” they asked. “He dragged it under the table. It was nearly twice his size.”

Eli’s mouth lifted. “The toast?”

“He sat on it. Completely sat on it. Like he’d caught something enormous and had to pin it down.”

The picture arrived so clearly that Rowan laughed before they could stop themself: Pip’s compact, solemn body planted over the bread, his whiskers forward, his whole face composed with the gravity of a hunter guarding a prize. Eli laughed too, warm and unguarded. For a moment the kitchen held the sound without asking anything of it.

The laughter did not mend the loss. When it passed, Rowan could still feel the hollow where Pip had been, as real as the table beneath their hands. But the memory was funny. It had happened while Pip was alive, and Rowan could keep it without turning his death into a price that made the joy worthwhile.

Later, alone, Rowan opened the care diary. They turned past the pages of symptoms and measurements, times and tasks, their own handwriting growing more cramped as the days had narrowed. At the back, there was a blank page. They sat with the pen poised above it, then began.

I am grateful for Pip’s warmth. I am grateful for the stubborn way he claimed a chair, a blanket, my lap, and once, an entire piece of toast. I am grateful that I knew the weight of him beside me and the pressure of his paw against my wrist.

I miss him so much that some ordinary moments still take me by surprise. None of this cancels the other. I can be grateful and devastated at once. I can remember him clearly and still wish he were here.

Rowan capped the pen. They did not write another list.

At the window, the tide was coming in, laying its dark surface over the rocks. Rowan watched the water advance and draw back, advance again. The glass was clouded at the edges with salt. They placed their wrist against the cool sill and remembered Pip’s paw resting there, light and insistent.

The sea kept its daily rhythm beyond the window. The flat remained quiet. Rowan missed him.
