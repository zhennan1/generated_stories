## Chapter 1: The Salt and the Silence

The house had been waiting with its mouth open.

Theo stood on the front step with the key in his hand and listened to the sea worrying at the cliffs below Morrow Point. The sound traveled strangely in the fog—less like waves than breath drawn through clenched teeth. Behind him, his rented van ticked as it cooled. Inside the house, dust and old wood and his mother’s lavender soap waited in the dark.

He had not called it home in the six months since she died. He had called it the house, the place, Mum’s. Anything but home, as if the right word might ask something of him.

At twenty-four, Theo had become very good at leaving. He had left art school before the final exhibition. Left the studio he could no longer afford. Left London with three canvases, a suitcase, and an email from the gallery saying they were “going in another direction.” He had left the town after his mother’s funeral without washing the teacups in the sink.

Now there was nowhere else to go.

He turned the key. The lock gave with a tired click.

Her blue coat still hung behind the door. One sleeve had fallen loose from its hook, and Theo reached to set it right. His fingers touched the wool. For an instant he expected her voice from the kitchen—You’re late, love, or Have you eaten?—but the house offered only the refrigerator’s low hum.

He carried his suitcase upstairs, though he had no intention of unpacking. The bedroom smelled faintly of rain trapped in the curtains. On the desk, beneath a film of dust, lay a cracked ceramic bowl painted with yellow flowers. His mother had kept buttons in it. There were still two inside: a black one and a pearl-colored one, sitting apart like people who had forgotten how to speak.

Theo opened the window.

Morrow Point spread below him in quiet gray layers: roofs, the church spire, the harbor, and beyond it the white, erased horizon. The town had always seemed to know how to wait. Fishing boats waited at their moorings. Nets waited in sheds. The bakery opened at six and the café at seven, regardless of weather or grief.

He envied everything that could continue without deciding to.

That first night, he slept badly. At three, he woke certain that someone was walking through the downstairs rooms. He lay still, listening. A floorboard creaked. Rain tapped the glass. Then silence closed over the sound.

His mother had died in the spare room at the back of the house. The hospice bed had been dismantled and taken away, but Theo still saw its pale rectangle in the carpet. He had been there at the end. He had held her hand. He had gone downstairs to make tea because he could not bear the rasp in her breathing, and she had died while the kettle boiled.

He had never decided whether that meant he had failed her.

By dawn, the question had become unbearable.

Theo pulled on a sweater and boots. He did not bother with a coat. The fog outside was thick enough to be touched, dampening his hair and beading on his eyelashes as he walked downhill toward the shore.

Morrow Point at that hour belonged to gulls and fishermen who swore at them. No one else was out. Harbor lights glowed weakly in the mist. Somewhere a buoy clanged, patient and hollow.

He followed the seawall past the closed fish shop and down to the beach. The tide had withdrawn, leaving ribbons of kelp shining on the sand. His shoes sank with each step. He kept his eyes on the water because it was easier than looking back at the town, at the house, at the life he had not managed to save.

Near the pier, he stopped.

When he was eight, his mother had taken him into the garden after a storm. The wind had knocked over the old shelf beside the greenhouse, and a dozen pieces of pottery lay scattered in the mud. Theo remembered crouching among them, furious on her behalf.

“It’s ruined,” he’d said.

His mother, wearing yellow gardening gloves, picked up a blue shard. “Is it?”

“It’s broken.”

“Those aren’t the same thing.”

She carried the pieces to the kitchen table and wrapped them in a tea towel. Later, she showed him how to fit the edges together. The bowl had been ugly even before the storm, lopsided and painted with a clumsy orange sun, but when she set it on the windowsill the cracks made a pattern of their own.

“Look at the light,” she said. “It finds every break.”

Theo had looked. He had seen only the damage. She had waited until he saw something else.

The memory vanished beneath a sound so small he thought at first it was the beach settling.

A thin, furious squeak came from under the pier.

Theo turned. The tide had left a strip of wet sand beneath the pilings, and there, tucked behind a concrete support, sat a cardboard box collapsed on one side. A grocery receipt clung to it like a white flag. He crouched and heard the squeak again.

“Hello?”

The box trembled.

He reached beneath the pier. The cardboard was slick and soft, its bottom dark with seawater. Inside lay a kitten no bigger than both his hands together. She was mostly gray, with a white chin and one crooked splash of cream on her nose. Her fur was plastered to her narrow body. She shivered so hard her head seemed to flicker.

But her eyes were enormous.

Green, luminous, and fixed on him with an attention that felt almost accusatory.

“Oh,” Theo whispered.

The kitten opened her mouth. No sound came out.

He took off his sweater and wrapped her in it. She was cold through the fabric. For one awful second she went rigid, and Theo thought he had frightened her into stillness. Then she pressed her face against the heel of his hand.

A vibration began inside her, faint as a distant engine.

“You can’t stay here,” he said.

The kitten purred.

Theo looked up and down the empty shore. There was no mother cat calling from the dunes, no box of siblings, no sign that anyone intended to return. The fog had swallowed the end of the pier. The town behind him was waking by degrees, but no door opened.

He tucked the kitten against his chest and stood.

She weighed almost nothing. That frightened him more than her coldness did.

The walk home took longer because he kept stopping to check that she was breathing. Each time, her small ribs moved beneath his palm. Each time, the purr resumed, stubborn and soft.

In the kitchen, Theo found an old towel, warmed milk in a saucepan, and searched his phone for what kittens could and could not eat. He made a nest in a laundry basket beside the radiator. The kitten crawled out immediately and folded herself against his ankle.

He sat on the floor with his back against the cupboard. Outside, the fog pressed its blank face to the windows.

For the first time since returning, the house did not feel entirely empty.

Later, he found one of his mother’s notebooks in the desk drawer. The cover was green cloth, frayed at the corners. He opened to the first clean page and wrote the date.

*I found a kitten beneath the pier this morning.*

He stopped, listening to the faint purr at his feet.

*She is very small. She doesn’t make much sound. I don’t know if she belongs to anyone.*

The kitten lifted her head. Her eyes caught the gray morning light.

*Maybe that is why I brought her home.*

Theo held the pen above the paper for a long time.

*The strange thing is, I think we recognized each other. She had been left behind. So had I.*

He closed the notebook but did not put it away. When the kitten shivered, he reached down and covered her with both hands.

Outside, the sea kept breathing in the fog.

## Chapter 2: Juniper

By the time Theo got the kitten through the front door, the eastern sky had turned the color of watered milk. He set the cardboard box on the kitchen table and stood over it, dripping seawater onto the linoleum, unsure what came next.

The kitten looked back at him.

She had not made a sound on the walk home. Not when he tucked the box under his coat, not when the wind worried at the paper lid, not even when he stumbled over the uneven pavement and nearly dropped her. Now she sat in one corner, gray fur clinging to her ribs, the white of her chin bright against the gloom. Her eyes were enormous and green, too solemn for such a small face.

“All right,” Theo said. His voice sounded unused. “We’ll work it out.”

He found an old laundry basket in the cupboard beneath the stairs and lined it with a towel. Then he put the basket beside the radiator, though the radiator gave only a lukewarm shudder when he turned it on. He heated water in a saucepan, tested it against his wrist, and poured some into a saucer. The kitten sniffed it, offended.

Food was harder. The cupboards held tea, crackers, three cans of tomatoes, and a jar of honey crystallized into amber stone. Theo pulled on his coat again and walked to the little grocer on Harbour Street. The woman behind the counter recognized him before he recognized her. Morrow Point remembered people even when they tried not to be remembered.

“Your mother’s boy,” she said.

“Theo.”

“I know.” She gave him a look that contained condolences, curiosity, and restraint. “What do you need?”

“Milk? Something for a kitten.”

“Not cow’s milk. She’ll get sick.” The woman reached beneath the counter and brought up a small tin of kitten formula. “Mrs. Bell left it here last week. Her cat had kittens. Take it.”

Theo tried to pay. She refused, so he bought a packet of bread he did not want and carried both home in the rain.

The kitten was still in the basket. She had crawled into the fold of the towel and made herself nearly invisible.

Theo mixed the powder as instructed, warming it until it smelled faintly sweet. He had no bottle, only a teaspoon. The first spoonful ran down the kitten’s chin. The second disappeared. On the third, she opened her mouth with sudden, startling determination.

“There you are,” he murmured.

She drank in tiny gulps, trembling with the effort. When she finished, she turned her head away and closed her eyes.

Theo sat on the floor beside the basket. He expected relief, but what came instead was a strange pressure behind his ribs, a tenderness so sharp it felt almost like fear. The kitten slept. One paw twitched against the towel.

He remembered his mother’s cat.

Mabel had appeared in their garden on a July afternoon, all elbows and burrs, yelling at the back door as if she had been invited. Theo had been eleven and wanted to keep her immediately. His mother had knelt in the herb bed, her hands dark with soil.

“She’s not ours,” she had said.

“She can be.”

His mother had looked at the cat, then at him. “Everything abandoned just needs one person.”

Mabel stayed for fourteen years. She slept on his mother’s pillow, shredded the curtains, and regarded Theo with a contempt that somehow resembled affection. In the last photograph before his mother became too ill to stand, Mabel was visible at the bottom edge, a blur of orange crossing the garden path.

Theo had not thought about that photograph in months. Now the memory arrived whole: sunlight on the bricks, his mother laughing because Mabel had stolen a ribbon, the smell of rosemary warming under the afternoon heat.

The kitten woke and crawled out of the basket. She moved badly, her hind legs sliding on the linoleum. Theo caught his breath, but she reached his ankle and pressed her narrow body against it. A vibration began inside her.

At first he thought it was the radiator.

Then he crouched and placed two fingers against her side. The purr was fierce, continuous, absurdly loud for an animal that weighed less than a loaf of bread. It seemed to come from everywhere in her: bones, throat, ribs. She leaned into his hand as if she had known it all her life.

Theo sat back on his heels.

“Is that your answer?” he asked.

The kitten opened her green eyes.

He thought of the dunes beyond the road, where low juniper shrubs grew twisted by the wind. Their berries were blue-black, and their roots held fast in sand that shifted beneath them. They survived without looking like they ought to.

“Juniper,” he said. “I think that’s you.”

She purred harder.

The next morning, Theo took her into town wrapped in a scarf. He had meant to buy a proper bottle and return before anyone saw him, but Morrow Point’s café windows were fogged with warmth, and the smell of coffee reached him halfway down the street.

Anya stood behind the counter, tying back her dark hair. She had served him twice when he was a child and once, silently, at his mother’s funeral. Her face changed when she saw the bundle in his arms.

“That is either a kitten,” she said, “or the smallest burglar in Sussex.”

“Kitten.”

“Does she have a name?”

“Juniper.”

Anya came around the counter but stopped before touching her. “May I?”

Juniper lifted her head. Her eyes were open, watchful. Anya’s expression softened.

“She’s beautiful.”

“She’s sick, maybe. Or just cold.”

“Most things look sick when they’re cold.” Anya returned behind the counter. “Sit down. I’ll bring you something.”

“I don’t have much money.”

“I didn’t ask.”

She brought him black coffee, toast with butter, and, in a saucer set carefully on the floor, a little cream. Juniper lapped at it while Theo ate. The café filled gradually around them: two fishermen arguing over a tide chart, a woman reading the paper, the clink of cups. No one demanded that he be cheerful. No one asked whether London had cured him of Morrow Point.

When he stood to leave, Anya pointed at the saucer. “That will be here tomorrow.”

Theo looked at her.

“For her,” Anya said. “And perhaps for you, if you remember to come in.”

Outside, the fog had lifted from the harbour. At the end of the street, the lighthouse flashed once in the pale daylight. A broad-shouldered man in a yellow oilskin was coiling rope beside its gate.

“Ezra?” Anya called through the open door. “This is Theo.”

The man looked over. “Your mother’s son.”

Theo almost corrected him, then let it pass.

“Come by this afternoon,” Ezra said. “I’m mending nets. I could use another pair of hands.”

“I don’t know anything about nets.”

“Neither did the last pair. They learned.” Ezra glanced at Juniper, who was peering from the scarf. “Bring the cat.”

“She’s not a cat,” Theo said, though he knew that was exactly what she was.

Ezra smiled. “Then bring Juniper.”

That night, Theo placed his mother’s green notebook beside the lamp. Juniper slept in the laundry basket, one paw over her nose. He listened to her purr until the house no longer seemed entirely empty.

He wrote:

*She doesn’t meow. She just watches me like she already knows me.*

After a moment, he added:

*I named her Juniper. She has decided to stay alive until morning.*

He closed the notebook. From the basket came a tiny rustle, followed by that steady, impossible motor.

For the first time since returning to Morrow Point, Theo did not wish the night away.

## Chapter 3: A Small Unraveling

Three weeks made a routine of them.

Theo learned that Juniper woke before dawn and regarded the darkness as if it were a private appointment. He learned to warm her formula in a mug of hot water, to leave the bathroom door open because she disliked being unable to see him, and to keep one sock on the floor beside the bed for her to attack. She grew cleaner and rounder, though she remained small enough to fit beneath his palm. Her purr had become the house’s second clock.

He began taking her to the café in a canvas satchel lined with a towel. Anya put cream on the floor without being asked. Ezra, when Theo crossed the harbour to help with the nets, would pause in the middle of a knot to scratch Juniper beneath the chin. She tolerated this with solemn dignity, then slept in the crook of Theo’s elbow while he learned how to find a torn mesh and draw it closed.

The town did not become less quiet. Theo did.

Then, one Tuesday morning, Juniper missed the chair.

She had climbed onto the seat a dozen times before, using the table leg as a ladder. This time her front paws found the cushion, but her hind legs seemed to forget what they were for. They slipped backward. She hung there, claws hooked into the fabric, and made no sound.

Theo caught her before she fell.

“Easy,” he said, holding her against his chest. “You’re all right.”

She looked at him, green eyes clear and unworried, and began to purr.

He set her on the floor. She walked to the radiator with an odd, wavering sway. He blamed the polished boards, then the morning chill, then the fact that she had only just woken. When she stumbled over the threshold of the kitchen, he told himself she was still growing.

By evening, the explanation had multiplied. A kitten could be clumsy. A kitten could be tired. A kitten could simply have a bad day.

The next morning she fell from the windowsill.

The sound was small. That was what frightened him: not a cry, but the soft thump of her body against the rug. Theo crossed the room so quickly that his knee struck the table. Juniper had already righted herself. One back leg trembled beneath her. She turned in a tight circle, searching for the sunbeam she had been chasing, and walked past it.

He lifted her.

At the café, Anya saw his face and stopped polishing a glass. “What happened?”

“She’s missing things.”

“Things?”

“Jumps. The sill. Her feet.” He looked down at the satchel. “I think she’s weak.”

Anya did not offer the easy reassurance. “Take her to Okafor.”

“I will.”

“Today, Theo.”

Dr. Samira Okafor’s veterinary surgery occupied two rooms above the old pharmacy, reached by stairs that smelled of dust, disinfectant, and wet coats. Juniper disliked the examination table. She pressed herself flat against Theo’s wrist while Dr. Okafor listened to her heart and moved each small limb through its range.

“How long have you noticed the wobbling?” she asked.

“A few days. Maybe longer.”

“Any vomiting? Diarrhea? Fever that you’ve noticed?”

“No. She eats. She purrs.”

“As if those are the same thing.” Dr. Okafor smiled faintly, then examined Juniper’s gums and eyes. “Her temperature is a little elevated. Her reflexes are not as strong as I’d like, particularly in the hind legs.”

Theo’s fingers tightened around the edge of the table. “What does that mean?”

“It may be an infection. She was found outside, underfed and exposed. Some infections can cause weakness, especially in a kitten this young.”

“Can you treat it?”

“I can treat what we suspect.” She wrote on a pad, her handwriting compact and decisive. “This is an antibiotic. Give her the full course, even if she seems better. Keep her warm. No climbing for now.”

“And if it isn’t an infection?”

Dr. Okafor looked at him—not harshly, but directly. “Then we’ll reassess. One step at a time.”

One step at a time. Theo carried the phrase home as carefully as he carried Juniper.

That afternoon, while Juniper slept in her basket, he remembered another room and another doctor’s pad.

His mother had received her diagnosis in the sitting room because she had been too tired to go to the hospital again. Rain ticked against the windows. The doctor had spoken gently, using words that seemed designed to soften their own edges: treatment, progression, options, time.

Theo had stood behind the sofa with both hands braced against it. His mother sat beneath the lamp in her blue cardigan, her silver hair uncombed. When the doctor left, she stared at the rain for a long while.

Then she turned and smiled at him.

“We’ll fight it,” she said.

He had believed her because he needed the sentence to be a promise. Later, he understood the smile. It was not certainty. It was an offering: the last courage she could place in his hands without making him carry her fear as well.

In the kitchen, Juniper stirred. The medicine smelled bitter. Theo wrapped her in a towel, tipped the syringe carefully between her lips, and held her until she swallowed. She shook her head, offended, then crawled into his lap and began to purr.

He laughed once, helplessly. The laugh turned wet before it finished.

That night he slept on the floor beside her basket. Every hour he woke and put two fingers against her ribs. In the blue interval before morning, he opened the green notebook and wrote:

*I keep checking her breathing in the night. I don’t know when I became someone who is afraid of love.*

He read it twice. Then he touched the basket’s rim.

“Get better,” he whispered. “That’s all you have to do.”

Juniper opened one green eye, as if considering the request. Her purr rose through the dark, small and fierce.

Theo let himself believe the medicine might work.

## Chapter 4: What the X-Rays Couldn't Say

The medicine did not fail all at once.

For three days, Theo mistook every quiet hour for improvement. Juniper swallowed the antibiotic with increasing resentment, then slept beneath the radiator while he worked at the kitchen table. She still purred when he touched her. She still ate, though she needed to be lifted to the bowl. Once, on the fourth morning, she managed to climb onto the low cushion by the window.

Theo stood in the doorway, afraid to breathe.

“There,” he whispered. “See? There you are.”

Juniper turned toward the sun. Her front legs held her. Her back legs trembled, then folded. She slid onto her side without making a sound.

He finished the course because Dr. Okafor had told him to. On the final evening he held the empty bottle over the sink, watching the last amber smear run toward the drain. Nothing had changed. If anything, Juniper’s steps had grown more uncertain. She began to drag one paw. She bumped into the leg of the kitchen chair and looked up at him, startled less by pain than by the chair’s betrayal.

Dr. Okafor asked him to bring her in the next morning.

The surgery seemed colder than before. Juniper sat on Theo’s lap beneath a gray blanket while Dr. Okafor tested her reflexes again. Her expression tightened as she moved the little legs, tapped beneath the paws, watched for responses that came late or not at all.

“The antibiotics haven’t helped,” Theo said.

“No.”

“So it isn’t an infection.”

“I’m afraid not.”

The words entered the room and stayed there.

Dr. Okafor drew blood, took images, and sent them through the old machine in the back room. Theo waited beneath a framed photograph of Morrow Point before the seawall was built. In it, the harbor was nothing but boats and brightness. He counted the boards in the floor. He counted Juniper’s breaths. When the machine hummed, she pressed her nose into his coat.

The X-rays showed bones, joints, a spine that looked ordinary and pale. They did not show the thing that was taking her balance. Dr. Okafor studied the films with her arms folded, then called a specialist in the city. She used words Theo could not hold on to: progressive, neurological, degenerative. A rare inherited disorder, though no one could know where Juniper had come from or what had happened before the box beneath the pier.

When she hung up, she sat across from him rather than standing over him.

“What is it?” Theo asked.

“We believe Juniper has a rare progressive neurological disease. It affects the nerves that control movement. It will continue to worsen.”

“How do you stop it?”

Dr. Okafor’s silence was answer enough, but she gave him the words anyway. “We can’t. There is no cure. There isn’t a treatment that will reverse it.”

Theo looked down at Juniper. She had fallen asleep inside the fold of the blanket, one white chin resting on his wrist.

“Then give me the treatment that slows it.”

“There isn’t one I can promise. We can keep her comfortable. We can treat pain if pain develops. We can help with feeding and mobility. But the disease is incurable.”

“How long?”

Dr. Okafor lowered her eyes to the file. “It’s difficult to predict. Months, perhaps. Less, if it advances quickly.”

The room became very precise. Theo heard a tap dripping in the adjoining sink. He saw a thread coming loose from the blanket. Juniper’s ear flicked once.

“Months,” he repeated.

“I’m sorry.”

“No.” He stood too quickly, and the chair scraped the floor. “No, you don’t know that. You said it was difficult to predict.”

“I said that because it is.”

“Then she could improve.”

“I don’t want to give you false hope.”

“Hope isn’t false.”

Dr. Okafor did not argue. She only reached for a paper and wrote down the name of the disorder, along with instructions for making the house safer. Soft food. Low-sided litter tray. No stairs. Return if Juniper stopped eating, seemed distressed, or could no longer breathe comfortably.

Theo took the paper and folded it until the disease’s name disappeared inside itself.

At home, he opened his laptop.

He searched the disorder first. Then he searched kittens with weak hind legs, abandoned cats that recovered, miracle treatments, experimental therapies, nerve regeneration, veterinary hospitals in London, veterinary hospitals in cities he had never heard of. He read until language loosened and became a stream of bright, terrible fragments. Paralysis. Progression. Quality of life. Euthanasia.

He closed one page and opened another. Someone in Finland had given a cat an herb. Someone in Texas had changed a diet. Someone’s three-legged kitten had learned to climb curtains. He copied phone numbers into the margins of an old sketchbook, though he did not call them. He watched videos with the sound off, studying animals that moved differently from Juniper, looking for a resemblance that might save her.

By midnight, the house smelled of cold tea and dust. Juniper lay in the basket beside the radiator, awake and watching him.

“You’re not dying,” Theo told her.

She blinked.

“You hear me? They don’t know everything. X-rays don’t know everything. Doctors don’t know everything.”

He said it with the fierce confidence of a child arguing with weather.

A memory came to him then—not of a hospital, but of his mother’s studio in the last winter of her life. On the worktable lay a bowl she had made years earlier, shattered into six large pieces when a shelf collapsed. Theo had found her fitting the fragments together with patient hands. Thin lines of gold traced the breaks.

“It’s beautiful,” he had said, though he had meant that it was strange.

His mother had looked over her glasses. “You’re allowed to say broken.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were thinking it loudly.” She turned the bowl so the gold caught the lamp. “We don’t fix broken things, Theo. We make them more honest.”

He had hated the bowl then. Hated its bright seams, hated the way she admired what could not be made whole. Now he remembered her hands trembling as she set it down, and the care with which she had touched every fracture.

Juniper climbed out of the basket.

It took her several attempts. Her front claws caught the edge. Her hindquarters dragged behind her, refusing the instruction her mind sent them. Theo reached toward her, then stopped. She pulled herself across the floor, inch by inch, until she reached his foot.

He lifted her onto the couch.

She crawled across his stomach and settled on his chest, exactly above his heart. Her body was warm and astonishingly light. For a while she was still. Then her purr began, faint at first, a motor starting in another room.

Theo put one hand over her back.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

The purr deepened.

“I’m sorry I can’t fix it.”

She kneaded his shirt with one paw, missing the fabric twice before finding it.

Something inside him gave way. He bent over her, careful not to crush her, and sobbed with a force that made his ribs ache. The tears ran into his ears. Juniper continued to purr, indifferent to the size of his grief. She did not know the word months. She did not know incurable. She knew only the warmth beneath her and the hand moving slowly along her spine.

When he could breathe again, he carried her to the kitchen and opened the green notebook.

*The vet said months. Juniper doesn’t know. That’s the mercy and the cruelty.*

He left the pen beside the sentence. Juniper slept against his ankle while dawn thinned the windows. Theo did not search again. Not yet. He watched the pale light gather on her gray fur and understood, with terror, that time could be both the enemy and the only thing he had.

## Chapter 5: The Gospel of Small Things

For two mornings after the diagnosis, Theo woke with the name of Juniper’s disease already waiting for him.

It sat behind his eyes while he measured her food. It followed him to the sink, where the empty antibiotic bottle remained beside the soap. It appeared whenever Juniper tried to cross the kitchen and her back paw folded beneath her.

He did not open the laptop.

This was not discipline at first. It was exhaustion. He had reached the end of the internet and found, beyond every promise, the same dark room. On the third morning he put the laptop in the hall cupboard and shut the door. Then he stood there with his hand on the knob, breathing as if he had locked away a dangerous animal.

Juniper blinked up at him from her blanket.

“All right,” he said. “Today we’ll do something else.”

He carried her to the café in the canvas satchel, leaving the top open so she could see the sky. Morrow Point was washed clean by a night of rain. Gulls argued along the harbor roof. At Anya’s, the windows steamed with coffee and buttered toast.

“You’re early,” Anya said.

“I’m trying something new.”

“Coffee?”

“Living.”

Anya looked at him for a moment, then nodded as if he had ordered it before. She set down a saucer of cream. Juniper leaned from the satchel and lapped at it, her pink tongue making a tiny, determined sound.

Theo watched her and let the world become no larger than that saucer: cream, whiskers, the soft gray triangle of one ear. Anya brought him toast without asking. Outside, a fishing boat moved through the harbor, its wake folding and unfolding behind it.

Afterward, Theo walked slowly along the pier. He kept Juniper in his arms, one hand beneath her chest and the other supporting her hindquarters. The boards were damp beneath his shoes. At the end of the pier, the sea breathed against the pilings, and Juniper lifted her face into the salt wind.

“Not much of a walk,” he told her.

She purred against his coat.

At home he made the house gentler. He moved the chairs farther apart and rolled towels beneath the sharp corners of the table. He set a low tray beside the radiator and placed food and water where Juniper could reach them without climbing. He put folded blankets on the floor, then sat among them while she learned the new geography by touch.

In the afternoon, sunlight entered through the west window and made a bright rectangle on the wall. Theo noticed Juniper watching it. He lifted one finger and moved it through the light.

Her head followed.

He moved his hand higher. The shadow jumped. Juniper pushed herself upright, wobbled, and reached with both front paws. She missed. Theo moved the sun slowly down the wall.

Again she tried.

For ten minutes they chased that small, impossible thing. Juniper’s paws struck the plaster. Once she toppled sideways and looked offended. Theo laughed so suddenly that the sound startled him. She stared at his moving fingers, then tried again.

That evening, while he carried kindling toward Ezra’s shed, a memory rose without warning.

He was nine, standing beside his mother on the shingle beach. She had shown him how to choose a flat stone—not the prettiest one, but one with a good edge and a little weight. Her coat smelled of turpentine and lavender soap.

“Don’t throw it at the sea,” she said. “Ask it to skip.”

His first stone sank immediately. The second made one awkward hop. His mother clapped as if he had performed a miracle.

“Again,” she said.

“You’re only clapping because you’re my mother.”

“I’m clapping because it tried.”

He remembered the two of them staying until the tide reached their shoes, sending stones toward the horizon. Each skip was brief. Each disappearance was certain. She had never called that failure.

Ezra was waiting beside a heap of nets, his yellow oilskin shining in the late light.

“There you are,” he said. “I thought the town had swallowed you.”

“Nearly.”

Ezra handed him a needle thick as a pencil and pointed to a torn diamond in the net. “Through here. Around this strand. Back through the next.”

Theo’s first knot came loose. His second tangled. Ezra did not take the work away from him.

“Patience,” he said. “The net remembers what it was doing.”

They worked while the lighthouse turned its white beam across the harbor. Theo’s fingers grew sore. The torn place slowly narrowed.

“Why mend it?” he asked. “It’s going back into the sea. It’ll tear again.”

Ezra pulled the cord snug. “You don’t mend a net for the one fish. You mend it because the sea is still coming.”

Theo looked toward the water. The sea was darkening, immense and indifferent, but not cruel. He thought of Juniper’s paws reaching for the sun on the wall. He thought of his mother applauding a stone’s single hop.

That night a thunderstorm arrived from the open water. Rain struck the windows hard enough to make the glass tremble. When thunder rolled over the house, Juniper dragged herself from beneath the chair and crossed the floor in crooked, urgent movements.

Theo lowered himself onto the rug.

She climbed into his lap, buried her face against his shirt, and began to purr. The sound shook through both of them. Another flash whitened the room. Theo wrapped his arms around her, leaving space for her breath.

“I’m here,” he said. “I’m here.”

The tears came then, quietly at first, then without restraint. He cried for the months he could not lengthen, for his mother’s hands, for every creature that had ever been left beneath a pier. Juniper did not pull away. Her purr continued, small and steady beneath the thunder.

He did not apologize for crying. There was nothing shameful in being reached by love.

Later, when the storm had moved north, he opened the green notebook. Juniper slept beside his ankle, one paw resting against his sock.

*Today she caught the sun on the wall. It wasn’t much. It was everything.*

Theo closed the notebook and listened to the tide. Tomorrow there would be cream, if Juniper wanted it. There would be the pier, if she had the strength. There would be nets waiting for his clumsy hands, and sunlight would return to the wall whether anyone deserved it or not.

For the first time since Dr. Okafor had spoken the word incurable, Theo did not count what remained.

He counted what was here.

## Chapter 6: The Weight of Watching

The first time Juniper walked into the chair, Theo blamed himself.

He had pulled it out to reach the cupboard and left it a hand’s breadth from its usual place. Her forehead met the wooden leg with a small, dull sound. She sat back, bewildered, and raised a paw as though the obstacle might pass.

“Sorry,” he said, lifting the chair away. “That was me.”

The next morning she bumped into the doorframe. Nothing had moved.

He knelt in front of her and held out his hand. Her green eyes looked almost through it. Only when he spoke did she turn toward him, ears pricked, nose working.

At the surgery, Dr. Okafor dimmed the light and examined her eyes. Theo watched the narrow beam move across each pupil.

“It appears her vision is being affected,” she said afterward. “With her condition, that’s a change we have to take seriously.”

“Will it all go?”

“I can’t tell you how quickly, or how much. Keep her surroundings consistent. Talk before you touch her so she knows you’re there. Let me know if she seems frightened or uncomfortable, or stops wanting her food.”

On the walk home, Theo spoke about everything he passed: the shuttered fishmonger’s, a dog asleep outside the post office, two gulls fighting over something neither of them could lift. Juniper rested inside his coat. He could feel her nose against his shirt.

At home he checked the towels he had already tied around the table corners. One had slipped. He secured it, then cut an old blanket into strips for the exposed edges of the low cupboard. The scissors made a heavy, chewing sound. He left the bowls and blankets exactly where they were.

When Juniper woke and began to turn restlessly, he carried her to the litter tray. He waited with a hand near her shoulder while she steadied herself, looking away to give her a dignity she did not know she needed.

Afterward, she leaned into his wrist.

“I’m here.”

He said it so often that afternoon it ceased to feel like reassurance and became an account of his whereabouts. At the sink. Beside the window. On the floor, within reach.

By evening he was exhausted, though he had barely left the kitchen. Each time she slept, he watched for the next thing he might lose.

His old sketchbook lay beneath a stack of care instructions. Its last used pages held the frantic notes from his search for treatments. He turned past them to clean paper.

For a while he held a pencil without moving it.

He had made lists since his mother died, filled forms, underlined instructions. He had not drawn. Drawing required him to look without immediately finding something else to do.

Juniper slept with her chin on a fold of blanket. He began with her ear.

The first line was too hard. He rubbed it out, leaving a gray bruise on the page. The second found the angle. Then came the small slope of her forehead, the whiskers he could hardly see against the cloth. When she opened her eyes, he stopped breathing for a moment and tried to catch their shape.

He was seven again, at this same table, presenting his mother with a picture of a horse.

It had six legs. He remembered defending them: the horse was running very fast.

His mother laughed until she had to put down her tea. He had begun to crumple the paper, furious, but she caught his hand.

“No, darling. I love him. He’s wonderfully equipped.”

She pinned it above the stove. Years later, looking for string in her cupboard, Theo found that horse in a folder with every other drawing he had brought her. Crooked houses, enormous birds, a portrait in which her earrings were larger than her head. She had dated the backs in her small, slanting hand.

Now he turned the page and drew Juniper’s paw.

Over the next few days the sketches accumulated: Juniper curled against his sock; Juniper searching for the water bowl before his voice guided her; Juniper sitting with her hindquarters tilted, one front paw planted firmly against the floor. He wanted to straighten her in the drawings. Instead he kept looking.

On Friday, Anya brought bread after closing the café. She found him at the kitchen table, with Juniper asleep in his lap and the sketchbook open.

“May I?”

He nodded.

She turned the pages slowly. Flour marked the cuff of her cardigan.

“I keep thinking I should be able to get used to it,” he said. “Then something else changes.”

Anya sat opposite him. Outside, the harbor bell sounded through the mist.

“My husband used to leave his boots in the doorway,” she said. “I spent eleven years asking him to move them. After he died, I left them there until spring.”

Theo looked up. He had known she lived alone. Somehow he had never imagined the doorway before it was empty.

“What made you move them?”

“I tripped over them carrying soup.” She smiled, briefly. “I was so angry with him. It was almost like having him home.”

Juniper stirred. Theo lowered his hand for her to find.

“The love doesn’t stop when they stop being here,” Anya said. “It just changes addresses.”

“Where does it go?”

“I don’t always know.” She touched the edge of a drawing. “Sometimes here, perhaps.”

After she left, Theo ate the bread standing at the counter. Then he carried Juniper to her tray and back to the warm blanket. She found his fingers with her cheek and rubbed against them, impatient for the scratch beneath her chin.

He obliged. Her purr came faintly, but it came.

Later he opened the green notebook.

*I drew her eyes while she still has them.*

He stared at the sentence. Her eyes were still there, green and beautiful; it was what they gave her that was slipping away. He left the words as they were. They were what he had been afraid of when he wrote them.

From the blanket came a rustle. He set down his pen.

“Here,” he said, and reached for her.

## Chapter 7: The Almost-Not

The sound that woke him was smaller than a knock.

A claw ticking against the skirting board. Then another, too fast, and the soft, wrong thudding of something that could not get away from itself.

Theo pushed himself up from the sofa. He had left the little lamp on, as though Juniper might still find her way by it. On the blanket below him she lay on her side, her legs moving in hard, useless strokes. Her head pulled back. For an instant he thought she was caught in something.

“June?”

He knelt and reached for her. The movement that met his hand was nothing like her ordinary trembling.

“No. No, sweetheart.”

He wanted to gather her against his chest, to stop what was happening by enclosing it. Instead he dragged the footstool away and pulled the loose edge of the blanket clear of her face. His hands hovered, absurdly empty. He was certain she was dying, and he couldn't even decide where to put them.

“Juniper. I'm here. Juniper.”

Her jaw worked. He remembered enough not to put his fingers near her mouth, but remembering felt like abandonment too.

He found his phone on the floor. The screen said 2:17. He stared at it, forcing himself to watch the seconds, then called the emergency number printed on Dr. Okafor's instructions. It rang through to her.

“She's shaking,” he said. “She can't stop.”

Dr. Okafor's voice was rough with sleep, then clear. “Is she somewhere she can't fall?”

“On the floor. On her blanket.”

“Good. Don't restrain her. Keep the area clear. How long?”

“I don't know. Less than a minute since I looked at the time.”

She stayed on the line. He set the phone beside him and lowered the lamp's shade away from Juniper's face. His voice kept coming out of him, her name over and over, as though he were calling across the beach in fog.

Then her legs stopped.

The stillness was worse.

Theo bent so close that his breath stirred the fur on her flank. Beneath it, at last, he saw a shallow rise.

“She's breathing.”

“All right. She may be confused for a while. Keep it quiet. Let her come round.”

Juniper lifted her head and let it fall. When she tried to crawl, he cupped a hand beside her to keep her from striking the wall. Only when the stiffness had gone did he settle her loosely against his legs, supporting her without holding her still.

“Hello,” he whispered. “Hello, little thing.”

She nosed blindly at his wrist.

He began to shake then.

Dr. Okafor told him what would mean going straight to the emergency clinic: another seizure before she recovered, difficulty breathing, one lasting five minutes. She asked him to bring Juniper to the surgery when it opened, and to call again if anything changed. Theo wrote the instructions on the back of an envelope. His letters looked like someone else's.

“Have I done something?” he asked.

“No, Theo. This isn't something you've caused.”

After the call he sat with Juniper until the radiator clicked cold. Eventually a faint purr travelled into his wrist. He could scarcely bear it. She had come back into the room, into her small body, and he was grateful with a violence that made him feel sick.

Outside, a boat engine started in the harbor.

He thought: I could take her in and leave her there.

The thought arrived whole and practical. Dr. Okafor had warm blankets. She knew what to do. There would be someone who could hear that sound without falling apart. He could pay, somehow. He could ask them to call when it was finished.

He looked down at Juniper, appalled less by the thought than by the relief inside it.

Already he was arranging the words. You can look after her better than I can. A sentence with enough truth in it to conceal the rest.

He had done something like it before.

After his mother's funeral, he had stood in this kitchen while water cooled around the teacups in the sink. Someone had left half a lemon on a saucer. It was the lemon he remembered most clearly: the exposed flesh drying, an ordinary thing going bad while everyone spoke softly in the next room.

His bag had been packed upstairs.

He told people there was work in London. A canvas waiting. Rent that couldn't pay itself. All true, except that when he got back to the studio he turned the canvas to the wall.

For weeks he had taken the lids off paint tubes and put them on again. A blue stain dried on his thumb because he couldn't remember making it. Eventually he stopped going in except to collect his post.

He hadn't known what to call the guilt. He had been there for so much of her illness, had fetched water and counted tablets, had answered when she called. Still, leaving the house had felt like slipping out of a room where someone was asking him to stay.

Now Juniper's paw rested across the seam of his trousers.

By morning she could lap a little water. He carried her to the low tray, waited beside her, then brought her back to the blanket. These were things his hands knew how to do. He let them do them while the rest of him stood somewhere far away.

He put the satchel near the door for the visit to the surgery. Beside it he placed the envelope of instructions. He tried saying the request aloud, quietly enough that it barely disturbed the room.

“Could you keep her?”

Juniper turned her head toward his voice.

He could not tell whether she saw him.

At the kitchen table he opened the green notebook. The tide was drawing back beyond the houses, leaving the harbor steps slick and bare. Soon the surgery would open. Soon he would have to put on his shoes and become someone who could answer questions.

He wrote:

*I don't know if I'm strong enough to lose another one. But I don't know if I'm strong enough to leave either.*

The pencil stayed in his hand after the sentence was finished.

From the other room came a small rustle of blanket. Nothing more. Theo listened until he could distinguish it from the sea.

## Chapter 8: The Net and the Needle

By morning, the house had become a map of things Theo was afraid to cross.

The blanket where Juniper had seized lay beside the radiator. The overturned chair was still on its side. A glass of water waited on the floor, untouched now, catching the pale window light. Theo moved around all of it as if the room were a museum dedicated to the moment before disaster.

Juniper slept in her basket, one paw draped over the rim. Her breathing was shallow but even. Every few minutes her whiskers trembled.

Theo made coffee and did not drink it. He opened the hall cupboard, saw the dark shape of his laptop, and shut the door again. He took his coat from its hook. Then he stood listening to Juniper breathe.

“I’m going out,” he whispered.

Her ears did not move.

It was easier, he discovered, to leave when she seemed not to hear him.

He went first to the café, though Anya was still stacking chairs and the morning rush had not begun. She looked at his face, set down the cloth in her hand, and poured him coffee without asking.

“How is she?”

“Sleeping.”

“And you?”

He lifted one shoulder.

Anya slid a saucer across the counter. A small spoonful of cream sat in it, absurdly white.

“For later,” she said.

“I don’t know if she’ll want it.”

“She might. Or you might. Cream is not a contract.”

He almost smiled, but the expression fell apart before it reached his mouth. He left the saucer untouched and walked toward the harbor.

At Ezra’s shed, nets hung from the rafters in long, sagging curtains. The place smelled of tar, salt, and old wood warmed by the sun. Ezra was bent over a torn section of net, pushing a needle through the cord with patient hands.

“You’re early,” he said.

“I didn’t want to be home.”

Ezra looked up. He did not ask why. He only nudged a stool out with his boot and placed a netting needle beside it.

Theo tried to work. He made the first knot too tight and the second in the wrong place. The cord scratched his fingers. Beyond the open door, gulls argued over something silver in the tide line.

“Had a dog once,” Ezra said after a while.

Theo kept his eyes on the net. “Once?”

“Old black mutt. Name of Bramble. Came to me with half an ear and a habit of sleeping in the road. Wouldn’t move for carts. Thought the whole world ought to go around him.”

“What happened?”

“He got old. First his hips, then his heart. Near the end, he couldn’t make it down the steps.” Ezra’s needle paused. “I carried him outside every morning and put him in the grass. He’d lie there and smell the wind like it was news.”

Theo’s hands stopped.

“I kept thinking I ought to find a way to make it different,” Ezra continued. “A better medicine. A younger body. Some trick I hadn’t thought of. But Bramble never asked me to make him young. He asked me to carry him into the grass.”

The shed seemed to draw in around them, all its ropes and hooks and quiet tools.

“Did you know when it was time?” Theo asked.

“I knew it was time to stop asking the day to be another day.” Ezra threaded the needle again. “We don’t get to choose the tide. We only get to choose how we stand in it.”

Theo looked down at the net in his lap. The torn places were not failures of the whole thing. They were simply where the sea had found a way through.

He left before noon and walked home by the long route. At the gate, he stopped. He could see Juniper’s basket through the window. The kitchen was empty. For one cowardly second, he imagined turning around again.

Then a gray shape appeared in the doorway.

Juniper moved slowly, one paw placed with concentration before the next. She had somehow climbed out of the basket. She crossed the kitchen, bumped lightly against the cabinet, corrected herself, and came toward him.

“Hey,” Theo said.

She followed his voice.

He knelt. She reached him, pressed her nose to his trousers, and began to purr. The sound was faint, uneven, almost no more than a vibration against his shin.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

Juniper put both front paws on his ankle.

He lifted her carefully. She curled into his lap as though she had been searching for exactly that place. Her head settled beneath his hand. The fear he had been holding all morning did not vanish; it changed shape, becoming something he could carry.

“I’m here,” he told her. “I’m here.”

That afternoon, he went to the cemetery.

His mother’s grave stood beneath a wind-bent cypress at the far edge, where the ground sloped toward the sea. Theo had not visited since the funeral. He had found reasons: the house, the gallery, the next train, the unbearable weather. Now there was only the stone, pale and ordinary, and his name for her cut into it.

He sat in the grass.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

The words were smaller than he expected. They did not open the sky or summon any answer. They only entered the wind.

“I’m sorry I left. I’m sorry I was angry that you got sick. I’m sorry I thought being gone meant I couldn’t hurt anymore.”

A gull cried over the cemetery wall.

He put his palm against the stone. It was cold.

“I forgive you,” he said, though he was not sure what he was forgiving. The illness. The dying. The way she had smiled and told him they would fight it. The fact that she had left him with a house full of unfinished things.

Perhaps forgiveness was not a verdict. Perhaps it was setting down a weight long enough to feel the shape it had made in his hands.

A memory rose, bright and terrible: his mother in the hospital bed, thinner than the blankets, her fingers searching for his. He had been twenty-two and furious with grief already, though she was still alive.

“Don’t be afraid to break,” she had whispered.

He had thought she meant the bowl, or his paintings, or the careful life he had built around not needing anyone. Now he understood that she meant him.

On the walk home, he stopped at the pier. The boards creaked beneath his feet. Below, the water folded and unfolded itself in the pilings, patient as breath.

He imagined bringing Juniper here when the weather was mild. Cream in a saucer. A blanket. The lighthouse path, if she could manage it. The beach where he had found her, though he would carry her over the rough places. He would take photographs. He would draw her. He would tell her the whole story, even if she understood only his voice.

A last good day, not because it could last, but because it could be given.

That night, Juniper slept against his ribs. Theo opened the green notebook and wrote:

*I’m not leaving. Not before she does.*

He touched the ink until it dried, then closed the notebook and listened to the tide come in.

## Chapter 9: The Last Good Day

Theo woke before the light, though for a moment he did not know why. The house was blue with early morning. Beside him, Juniper lay on the blanket, her small body rising and falling beneath the flannel towel he had folded over her.

He listened.

The tide breathed beyond the open window. A gull called once, then seemed to think better of it. Juniper made a faint sound—not a meow, never that, but the softest questioning trill—and turned her face toward him.

“Morning, little shrub,” Theo whispered.

Her tail moved once.

He had planned the day carefully, as if care could make a shape around time. He warmed her breakfast, carrying the bowl to her instead of asking her to cross the kitchen. She ate a little. He waited, then offered a little more. When she finished, he wiped a bright crumb from her white chin.

“Big day,” he told her.

Juniper blinked at him with eyes that had once seemed impossibly green. Their color was still there, though a veil had gathered over it, like mist over the sea.

He put her in the satchel lined with a blanket. Her head rested against the opening, and one paw hung over the edge. Theo carried her down the quiet street to Anya’s café.

Anya had left the front door propped open. The smell of toast and coffee drifted into the salt air.

“There she is,” Anya said, coming around the counter. “Our guest of honor.”

Juniper’s ears twitched at her voice.

Anya set a saucer on the floor, filled with a little cream. Juniper leaned forward. Theo lowered the satchel until she could reach it, and she lapped carefully, pausing between each taste. A white crescent appeared on her nose.

Anya laughed softly. “Very dignified.”

Theo took out his phone. He had photographed Juniper before, but always quickly, as though pictures were a way of proving she was still there. Today he asked Anya to take one of them together.

In the photograph, Theo was looking down. Juniper was looking toward the cream. The morning light touched the side of her face.

“Take another,” Anya said. “This time look at her.”

He did.

Afterward, Anya packed a piece of toast for him and another small container of cream for later. She did not say what both of them knew. She only pressed his hand.

“Go slowly,” she said.

“We will.”

The lighthouse path wound out of town through grass bleached silver by wind. Theo carried Juniper against his chest, one hand beneath the satchel so it would not swing. The lighthouse rose ahead, white against the wide sky. Its lantern room flashed once in the sun.

At the gate, Ezra stood mending a section of net.

“Perfect weather for a passenger,” he said.

Theo opened the satchel. Juniper lifted her head at Ezra’s voice.

Ezra touched one finger to her forehead. “Still keeping him in order?”

Her purr began, thin but unmistakable.

They sat together on the low wall. Theo gave Juniper drops of water from a bottle cap. Ezra spoke about the morning tide, the fishing boats, a seal seen near the outer rocks. Juniper listened to the voices and the wind. Once, when a gull passed overhead, she followed its cry with her head.

Theo took another photograph.

Then he drew her.

The sketchbook rested on his knee, the pencil moving slowly. He drew the slope of her skull, the white under her chin, the paw curled over the blanket. He did not try to make her stronger than she was. He did not correct the weakness from her legs or the uncertainty in her gaze. The drawing was tender because it was true.

“Your mother used to draw the sea badly,” Ezra said.

Theo smiled. “She drew everything badly.”

“Kept trying, though.”

“She did.”

From the lighthouse path they went down to the beach. Theo carried her over the stones and set the blanket on a patch of dry sand above the tide line. This was where he had first walked with her, months ago, when she had been tiny enough to fit inside the crook of one arm and the whole world had still seemed negotiable.

Juniper lay against his stomach. The wind lifted her whiskers. Theo told her the story anyway: the box beneath the pier, the soaked cardboard, the enormous green eyes, the way she had watched him as if she had been waiting.

“You were so small,” he said. “I thought I could save you.”

She purred.

“I was wrong about that. You saved me too.”

The beach blurred. He bent over her, careful not to let his tears fall on her face.

A memory came—not of sickness, but of a summer long before it. He had been eight, and his mother had taken him to this same beach with a striped towel and two bruised apples. The sea had left a red-orange starfish in a shallow pool. Theo had picked it up, astonished.

“It’s a gift,” he said.

His mother knelt beside him. “Maybe. But gifts from the sea need to go back.”

He had placed the starfish on the wet sand and watched the water gather around its arms. It seemed to him then that giving something back was the same as losing it. His mother had held his hand until the tide carried the starfish away.

Now Juniper’s paw rested over his fingers.

“Not yet,” Theo whispered. “Not today.”

They stayed until the sun leaned west. At home, he spread blankets on the sitting-room floor, opened the window, and placed water within reach. Juniper could not manage the climb to the sofa, so Theo lay beside her. He showed her the photographs on his phone, though she could not see them clearly, and read aloud from the old notebook—the harmless entries, the first one about being left behind, the line about her watching him as if she already knew him.

She fell asleep with her cheek against his wrist.

Theo sketched until the room darkened. Then he wrote in the green notebook:

*If love could heal her, she would live forever.*

He closed it and turned off the lamp. In the dark, the tide moved closer, withdrew, and came closer again. Juniper’s purr trembled beneath his palm.

Theo kept his hand there until sleep found them both.

## Chapter 10: The Pier at Dawn

The tide was going out when Theo woke.

For a few seconds he did not know where he was. Gray light rested against the ceiling. The window stood open, and the room smelled of salt, damp wool, and the faint medicinal sweetness of the food Juniper had eaten the night before. Then he felt the small weight against his wrist.

Juniper was awake.

She lay on her blanket with her chin lifted, listening to the sea. Her green eyes had become cloudy at the edges, but they found him when he leaned close. She made no sound. Her purr began beneath his fingers, so faint he felt it more than heard it.

“Morning,” Theo whispered. “You ready?”

He did not know whether he meant the pier, or the day, or himself.

He wrapped her in the blue towel Anya had given him and tucked her into the satchel, leaving the top open so she could feel the air. Outside, Morrow Point was still asleep. The houses along Gull Street were dark, their windows holding the last weak reflections of dawn. A gull cried somewhere over the harbor, and another answered.

The walk to the pier took longer than it had on the first morning. Theo stopped whenever Juniper shifted, checking her breathing, offering a finger for her to smell. She pressed her nose against it each time. The familiar gesture steadied him: her small warm breath, the whiskers brushing his skin.

At the foot of the pier, Dr. Okafor was waiting beside her old green car. She wore a dark coat over her scrubs and carried a canvas case. Her face was tired, but her voice, when she greeted him, was calm.

“Good morning, Theo.”

“Is it time?”

She looked toward the water. “You tell me.”

He opened the satchel. Juniper stretched one paw toward him. Her body had grown so light that carrying her felt less like lifting an animal than carrying a bundle of blankets that happened to breathe.

“She ate a little,” he said. “She drank. She purred.”

“All good signs of comfort.” Dr. Okafor touched Juniper gently between the ears. “But she’s having trouble settling, isn’t she?”

Theo looked down. “She woke twice. She couldn’t find the litter tray. And then she just stood there, frightened.”

Dr. Okafor nodded. She did not say the words he had come to hear, and for that he loved her a little. She let the truth arrive at its own speed.

They walked out onto the pier. The boards were wet beneath their feet, and the fog had drawn a white curtain across the far end. This was where Theo had found her, beneath the third crossbeam, in a box softened by rain. He could still remember the shock of her eyes opening: enormous, green, furious at being alive.

He sat on the old bench and placed Juniper in his lap. Dr. Okafor set her case beside her boots.

“I’ll give her something to relax first,” she explained. “Then the final medication. It will be quick. She may become sleepy before her heart stops. You can keep holding her. Talk to her if you want.”

“If I cry?”

“You can cry.”

“If I change my mind?”

“We can wait. We can go home. There is no prize for doing this before you’re ready.”

Theo looked at Juniper. She had begun to purr again, her head tucked beneath his palm.

“I’m ready,” he said, and the words did not feel true until he spoke them. “I’m not ready. But I’m ready for her.”

“That is usually what ready means.”

The first injection went into the loose skin at Juniper’s shoulder. She flinched once, then settled against Theo’s stomach. He stroked the cream-colored mark on her nose. Beyond them, the tide moved beneath the pier with a hollow, breathing sound.

“Do you remember this place?” he asked her. “I do. You were in a box, and I was pretending I didn’t need anything. You fixed that.”

Her eyes slid closed.

Theo told her everything. He told her about the first bowl of formula, about the name Juniper and the dunes where the shrubs bent under the wind. He told her about Anya’s cream and Ezra’s yellow oilskin, about chasing the sun across the wall, about the thunderstorm and the night she followed his voice into his lap. He told her that she had made a house out of a place he had only been hiding in.

The fog shifted.

For one instant, he saw his mother standing at the far end of the pier, wearing the red cardigan she had worn while gardening. She held a chipped cup in one hand. Juniper, impossibly young again, sat at her feet, tail curled neatly around her paws.

His mother smiled. “You don’t have to keep everything alive to prove you loved it.”

Theo shut his eyes.

“I know.”

Juniper’s purr trembled beneath his hand.

“She says it’s okay,” he whispered.

“No,” said his mother. “I’m saying it’s okay.”

Then the vision changed, as memories do. His mother was in the garden, kneeling beside the broken pottery. Juniper was there too, batting at a fallen gold leaf. Both of them looked up at him with the same impossible patience.

It’s okay, they told him.

It’s okay to break.

Dr. Okafor touched his sleeve. “She’s deeply asleep now.”

Theo bent until his cheek rested against Juniper’s head. Her fur smelled of dust, salt, and the soap he used on her blankets.

“Thank you,” he said. “Thank you for finding me. Thank you for staying. You can stop now. I’m here.”

Dr. Okafor gave the final medication. Theo felt no dramatic change, only the gradual loosening of the little body in his arms. The purr faded. A breath came, then another, each farther apart.

He counted them because counting was something to do.

At the last one, the sea seemed to draw a long breath of its own.

Theo waited. Dr. Okafor listened with her stethoscope, then rested two fingers against Juniper’s chest. Her expression softened.

“She’s gone,” she said.

The sentence entered him quietly. It did not shatter anything. It opened an absence, clean and enormous.

He held Juniper for a long time. Dr. Okafor stayed beside him without looking at her watch. When he was ready, she wrapped Juniper in the towel and carried her gently to the car. Later, at the surgery, there would be forms and a small metal container and the practical language of cremation. For now there was only the pier, the retreating tide, and Theo’s empty arms.

The fog lifted as the sun rose. Morrow Point appeared piece by piece: the lighthouse, the harbor roofs, Anya’s café with its blue door. The world had not stopped. A fisherman shouted from the quay. A gull landed on the railing and regarded Theo with one bright, unkind eye.

Three days later, Dr. Okafor gave him the ashes in a plain ceramic urn. Theo carried them to the beach alone. He stood where the wet sand held the sky and loosened the lid.

The ashes were finer than he expected. They lifted in the wind, silver-gray, and settled on the tide. Some clung briefly to his fingers. He rinsed them away in the water.

“Go on,” he said. “You know the way.”

For the first time, releasing her did not feel like abandoning her.

At home, he opened the curtains. He washed Juniper’s bowls, but he did not throw them away. He folded the blankets and placed one beside the sitting-room window. Then he set a canvas on the easel.

The first line he drew was not of the pier or the sea. It was the curve of a small gray back asleep against his wrist.

That evening, he opened the green notebook and wrote:

*She was never mine to keep. She was mine to love. That was the whole assignment.*

Theo put down the pencil. Outside, the tide came in, carrying the light toward shore.
