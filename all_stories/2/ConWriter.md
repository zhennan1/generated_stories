The veterinarian nodded and led us toward an examination room. I followed, carrying Pip as steadily as I could. The next step was no longer something I had to imagine. It was the open door ahead, the table beyond it, and my hand remaining near Pip’s paw while we found out what his body needed next.


The veterinarian closed the door behind us, and the harbor vanished into a muted hush. The examination room smelled of antiseptic, warmed metal, and the faint animal musk that clung to every clinic. A narrow window looked toward the backs of the seaside buildings, where chimneys and slate roofs stood between us and the water. I could not see the waves, but I heard them: a low, repetitive shushing beneath the fluorescent hum.

I placed Pip’s carrier on the table and unlatched it. He remained curled around his toy until I offered my hand. Then he reached forward, sniffed my fingers, and climbed out with careful dignity. His hind legs wobbled when they touched the metal surface. I caught myself before grabbing him. The veterinarian steadied the towel beneath his paws and asked me to describe the change once more.

I opened the notebook. “He played for maybe five minutes. Then he walked to the water dish and seemed tired. His breathing was faster, but it settled while he rested.” I turned the pages toward them. “I wrote down everything else. Food, litter, medication, how long he slept.”

The veterinarian read in silence, one gloved finger moving down the page. Pip leaned against my wrist. His body was warm, his ribs delicate beneath the fur. I kept my other hand near his shoulder while the veterinarian listened to his chest, checked his gums, and pressed gently along his abdomen. Pip objected to the thermometer with a thin, furious cry.

“I know,” I murmured. “An insult of the highest order.”

His ears flattened, but when I scratched beneath his chin, he began to purr. The sound was uneven and faint, more vibration than music, yet it filled the room. I remembered an afternoon when the weather had cleared after days of rain. Pip had chased a strip of sunlight across the apartment, leaping from the matting to the windowsill and back again until he collapsed beside my books. I had taken a picture of him sprawled upside down, one paw covering his face. In the photograph, his whiskers pointed in every direction and his mouth was open in a ridiculous smile.

I had written beneath it in my diary: *Pip has defeated the sun. The sun may recover eventually.*

Now his purr faltered as the veterinarian moved the stethoscope. I closed the notebook around my thumb and forced myself to look at him rather than the instruments.

The veterinarian finished the examination and pulled a stool close. “The breathing episode is concerning, but he is stable at this moment. The weakness in his hind legs has progressed since his last visit.”

I nodded. “I knew it had.”

“As we discussed, the blood work and the earlier imaging point toward a rare inherited storage disease.” They spoke slowly, leaving space between each phrase. “His cells cannot process certain substances correctly. They accumulate, especially in the nervous system and muscles.”

I stared at the notebook. The blue lines blurred, then sharpened.

“What does that mean for him now?”

“It means the disease is progressing. There is still no curative treatment available. We can support his breathing, manage discomfort, protect his appetite, and adjust his environment as his abilities change. Some cats live longer than expected with careful nursing. Others decline quickly.”

The word *incurable* did not arrive as a blow. It seeped into the room, filling every corner until there was nowhere left for denial to stand.

Pip lifted his head and tapped my wrist with one paw. His claws caught briefly in my sleeve. I covered his paw with my fingers.

“How long?” I asked.

The veterinarian did not give me a number. They explained what to watch for instead: breathing that failed to settle with rest, refusal of food or water, distress that medication could not ease. They showed me how to count his breaths while he slept and told me to call before any crisis rather than waiting for one. They adjusted his medication, added a small supply of emergency support, and wrote the instructions in the notebook beneath my cramped observations.

I copied each line carefully. My hand shook so badly that the letters leaned into one another.

“Can I still let him play?”

“Yes,” they said. “Short, gentle play. Let him choose. Joy is not dangerous.”

That sentence broke something open in me. I bent over Pip, pressing my forehead lightly against his.

“Did you hear that?” I whispered. “You’re allowed to have fun.”

He answered with a small chirp and reached toward the dangling cord of my hoodie. I moved it slowly across the towel. Pip followed it with his eyes, then hooked it with one paw. The movement took effort, but when he caught it, his pupils widened. For three breaths, he was not a diagnosis or a schedule of symptoms. He was a kitten who had won.

I laughed, and tears fell onto the back of my hand.

Before we left, I added one final entry beneath the care instructions:

*The illness has a name. It still cannot be cured. Pip still caught the cord.*

I placed the notebook in my bag, secured his medication, and lowered him into the padded carrier. This time he turned once and settled without hesitation. I lifted the carrier from the examination table and held it close.

The veterinarian opened the door for us. Beyond it, the clinic hallway led toward the waiting room, the front bell, and the quiet harbor road home. I stepped through with Pip’s weight against my knees, carrying the terrible knowledge and the next instructions together.


Halfway down the hallway, I stopped to shift the carrier into both hands. The medication bag slid against my wrist. Behind us, the veterinarian’s door closed with a small click, and I stood listening to it as though another instruction might come through the wood—something overlooked, something that would change what I was carrying home.

Pip scratched once at his towel.

“All right,” I said. “We’re going.”

In the waiting room, I paid with the card I usually reserved for emergencies. The receptionist tucked the receipt into the medication bag without asking whether I wanted it. I was grateful for that small omission. Outside, the bell trembled above the door, and the harbor air struck my face, cold enough to make my eyes water without explanation.

I took the sheltered side of the road, away from the wind coming between the warehouses. Fishing boats rested low in the harbor. Someone had left a yellow bucket overturned beside the railings, and water dripped steadily from its rim. Everything seemed offensively ordinary until I realized how badly I needed it to be ordinary: the bakery shutting its door, rope creaking against a bollard, somebody sweeping sand from a shop entrance.

At the bait shop, my steps slowed.

I could see the narrow gap where I had first found Pip, tucked behind a stack of empty crates. In memory, I was crouching there again, younger by only a little time and impossibly certain that getting him indoors would solve everything. My phone torch lit two enormous ears. When I reached toward him, he hissed with such determination that I laughed.

“Fair enough,” I had told him. “Stranger danger.”

I had gone home for a towel and returned afraid he would be gone. Instead, he had emerged from behind the crates and attacked the towel’s corner, seemingly forgetting for a moment to fear me. Carrying him upstairs afterward, I had kept saying, “You can stay,” although he had no understanding of tenancy and I had barely learned how to manage my own.

Now I bent slightly over the carrier.

“You can still stay,” I whispered.

Pip blinked through the mesh. I straightened before anyone could ask why I was crying outside a closed bait shop and carried him the rest of the way home.

The stairs demanded care. I held the carrier level against my chest, climbed slowly, and stopped on the landing when my arms began to tremble. Inside the apartment, I set him down before taking off my coat. The room smelled faintly of washing powder and the food I had left covered in the kitchen.

I opened the carrier. Pip put his front paws onto the floor, then hesitated as his back legs followed. One paw slipped on the bare board.

My hand moved beneath his belly before I had time to think.

“Just a second.”

I helped him onto the rug and fetched the matting offcut from beside the wall. The shopkeeper’s gift had seemed generous before; now, kneeling with it across my lap, I felt grateful enough to be embarrassed. I trimmed a loose edge, brushed away the scraps, and laid it between the rug and Pip’s bed. Then I moved his water closer and brought the low-sided litter tray within reach, leaving space between each place.

Pip watched this rearrangement with his ears angled forward.

“Terrible service,” I told him. “Furniture moving while the guest is resting.”

He stepped onto the matting. His claws caught securely, and he crossed it in several slow, uneven steps. I kept my hands nearby but let him finish. At the bed, he sniffed the blanket and climbed in.

I sat back on my heels. It was only a short strip of floor. He still had the illness. Yet he had crossed without slipping, and for a moment I could look at what I had done without immediately seeing everything I could not do.

After offering food, I checked the written instructions and put the medicines where I could reach them without leaving him for long. I saved the clinic’s emergency number in my phone, then copied it onto paper because batteries died and frightened people forgot passwords. Beside the notebook, I left a pen uncapped.

*Home after the clinic,* I wrote. *Ate a little. Crossed the new matting. Resting now.*

Below that, after a pause:

*I keep wanting care to mean getting him well. Tonight it means making the floor easier.*

A thin patch of sunlight reached the bed. Pip lifted his head when my sleeve cast a moving shadow across it. I held still, then moved my wrist a fraction. His paw came up and landed squarely on the shadow.

“Caught it.”

He kept his paw there. I waited until he withdrew it before moving again.

Soon he lowered his chin onto the blanket. I watched his breathing settle, counted as the veterinarian had shown me, and wrote the observation down. Then I put the notebook aside. There would be more things to record, but I wanted him to have some time with my hand empty.

I rested my fingers beside his forepaws.

“Good night, little trouble,” I whispered, though the window was still pale.

The familiar farewell caught in my throat. I bent and kissed the fur between his ears, then stayed on the floor while the sunlight slowly left his bed.


When the room grew dark, I reached up and switched on the little lamp beside the sofa. Pip opened one eye at the click, then closed it again. Beyond the window, the harbor lights made broken yellow paths across the water. I could hear someone fastening a shutter downstairs, followed by footsteps retreating along the empty street.

I had not eaten. Getting up felt like desertion, which was absurd and did not make it easier. I warmed yesterday’s soup and brought it back in a mug. Sitting beside Pip’s bed, I drank with one hand and let him lean against the other. Once, his whiskers stirred toward the steam.

“Absolutely not,” I said. “You have your own dinner.”

His ears turned toward me. I smiled before I remembered I was frightened.

There were more moments like that over the days that followed. Pip licking a smear of food from his nose. Pip objecting when I washed his blanket and substituted a towel. Pip watching a gull on the opposite chimney with an intensity his weakened body could no longer translate into stalking. I brought his bed nearer the window without putting it in the draft, and sat beside him while the gull rearranged its feathers.

*Later,* I wrote in the diary. *He watched the chimney for ages. I opened my coursework beside him and managed half a page. He fell asleep with his paw on the corner.*

I left space beneath the entry, then added: *I am allowed to remember that this was a good afternoon.*

One evening, while searching my phone for the clinic’s number, I touched an old video by mistake. Pip filled the screen, smaller and rounder, pursuing a paper ball beneath the kitchen chair. I heard my own laugh.

I remembered filming it: my knees cold against the boards, the kettle boiling itself hoarse behind me. He had pounced too enthusiastically and landed inside my slipper. For several seconds he stayed there, astonished, while I laughed so hard the picture shook. Afterward, I had carried him against my chest to turn off the kettle. He had bitten a button on my cardigan as though this were his contribution to making tea.

On the bed beside me, the present Pip lifted his head at the recorded sound of my voice.

“Still you,” I whispered, setting the phone aside.

Later, he could not settle. I checked what the veterinarian had written, counted his breaths, and called rather than waiting for morning. We spoke again after the prescribed measures failed to give him lasting relief. I described his efforts to change position, the untouched food, the way he kept raising his head.

“Bring him in,” the veterinarian said gently. “We’ll help him.”

I lined the carrier with his familiar blanket. My fingers fumbled at the door catch, and I had to stop and breathe before lifting him. He weighed so little that I wanted to argue with my own hands.

I carried him down the apartment stairs, pausing at each landing to keep him level. Outside, the town was quiet beneath a low, pale sky. We passed the hardware shop, its window crowded with buckets and coils of rope. I thought of the matting upstairs, the little path that had helped him reach his bed. I was glad I had asked for it.

At the clinic, the veterinarian examined him, then sat with us. They explained what could still be tried and what they believed it would mean for him. I asked my questions twice. They answered twice.

“Can you make him comfortable?” I asked.

“Yes.”

“And if we go home?”

Their answer was careful, but clear. I looked down at Pip, his chin resting in the hollow of my hand.

“I don’t want him to keep struggling,” I said. Agreeing felt unbearable. So did asking him to endure more because I could not bear goodbye.

They brought a soft towel. I put his blanket over it and stayed beside him while the first medicine let his body relax. For the first time that night, his head rested without lifting again.

I told him about the slipper. About the gull. About the abandoned kitten who had frightened me with one magnificent hiss behind the bait shop.

“You were very fierce,” I said, and my voice broke. “I’m so glad I went back for you.”

When the veterinarian asked whether I was ready, I kissed the place between his ears.

“Good night, little trouble. You’re home with me.”

I kept my hand around his paw through the last injection, and afterward, until the veterinarian quietly told me his heart had stopped. There was no answer I needed to give. I stayed.

Back upstairs, I set the empty carrier beside the door. The matting still led to his bed. I could not put either away.

I opened the diary beneath the lamp.

*Pip died with my hand around his paw. He was comfortable. I miss him already.*

For a while, that was all I could write. Then I turned back through the pages—food, medicines, breath counts—until I found the photograph of him upside down in the sun.

I placed it beside the last entry, leaving both in view.
