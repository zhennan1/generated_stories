## Chapter 1: The Recruit

Thomas Merrick enlisted beneath a sky the colour of pewter.

The recruiting sergeant had set up his table in the village hall, between a trestle of treacle buns and a stack of patriotic pamphlets. Behind him hung a flag and a painted card of a soldier striding through smoke, his bayonet bright as a sword.

WHO WILL STAND WHEN ENGLAND CALLS?

Thomas had been standing before the question for nearly a year.

At seventeen, he was tall enough to pass in his father’s old Sunday coat, though the sleeves ended above his wrists. The sergeant asked his age, and Thomas said nineteen.

“Fine age,” the man replied. “Strong age.”

Thomas signed his name. The pen trembled only once.

Outside, rain threaded the windows of the village square. The church bell sounded eleven, and a cart rolled through the mud with a slow, patient creak. Nothing had changed, yet Thomas felt that the whole of England had opened beneath his feet.

His father did not speak when he came home.

Mr. Merrick was the village schoolmaster, a narrow man with silvering hair and spectacles he polished whenever he wished to avoid an argument. He found the enlistment paper on the kitchen table beside the bread knife. He read it, folded it, and placed it exactly where it had been.

“Your mother would have been against it,” he said.

“She would have understood.”

“Your mother understood that bullets do not care for understanding.”

Thomas looked towards the window. Beyond the rain stood the schoolhouse, its windows dark, though he could almost hear the children reciting their times tables.

“I can’t stay here while everyone else goes.”

“Everyone else is not going.”

“Enough are.”

His father removed his spectacles. Without them, his face seemed older.

“Poems have made this war for you,” he said. “Poems and newspapers.”

On Thomas’s bedside table lay a volume of Tennyson, its spine cracked from use. He had read of charging guns, noble hearts, and men who rode into history beneath banners snapping in the wind. War had become, in his imagination, a door. Behind it waited danger, but also purpose. He was tired of being merely the schoolmaster’s son, tired of days that arrived already measured and ended without leaving a mark.

“I want to do something that matters,” he said.

His father put his spectacles back on.

“You matter here.”

It was not an answer Thomas could accept.

The last weeks passed quickly. He was measured for khaki, given boots that bruised his heels, and instructed to report to camp. Neighbours shook his hand. Mrs. Bell from the bakery pressed two currant buns into his palm and told him not to waste them on officers. The vicar spoke of courage. The girls at the post office watched him with solemn, admiring eyes.

On his final evening, Thomas and his father walked beside the river. Frost silvered the reeds; the water moved blackly beneath the bridge.

His father gave him a small notebook bound in brown leather.

“For your observations,” he said.

“Of the war?”

“Of anything. A man ought to remember what he has seen.”

Thomas turned it over in his hands. Its pages were clean and cream-coloured.

“I’ll write every day.”

“Then write the truth.”

At the station the next morning, his father stood stiffly while the train filled with young men and noise. Thomas wanted an embrace, but there were too many watching. Instead, his father gripped his hand with surprising force.

“Come back,” he said.

Thomas nodded.

The train pulled away. His father walked alongside it for several yards, then stopped. Thomas watched him grow smaller among the rain-wet figures until the fields swallowed everything.

Training camp was a world of shouted orders and perpetual mud. Thomas learned to fold blankets into exact rectangles, scrub his mess tin until it shone, and march until his feet blistered. He learned that a rifle had a weight and balance of its own, that every button mattered, and that sleep could be taken wherever it was offered.

On the third day, a wiry recruit with a red scarf tied around his neck dropped into the bunk opposite.

“Billy Preece,” he said. “Aberdare.”

“Thomas Merrick.”

“England?”

“Somerset.”

Billy looked him over. “You look like you’ve never held a shovel.”

“I have.”

“Have you held one properly?”

Thomas did not know how to answer. Billy laughed, a sharp, cheerful sound.

He was a miner, he explained, and had spent four years underground. He could tell a good roof from a bad one by the way the dust settled. He could also swear in three languages, though he admitted the third was mostly invented.

“You read?” Billy asked, seeing the book in Thomas’s kit.

“Poetry.”

“Does it help?”

“It gives a man courage.”

Billy lay back on his blanket. “A good pair of socks gives a man courage. Poetry gives him blisters.”

Thomas smiled despite himself.

They became friends through shared chores and stolen food. Billy showed him how to wrap his feet in dry cloth and sleep sitting upright. Thomas read aloud in the barrack room when the lamps were low and the other men were too tired to complain. Sometimes Billy listened with his eyes closed.

Once, when Thomas finished a passage about glory, Billy said, “Glory sounds like something officers put on a plate when there isn’t enough meat.”

Yet Thomas continued to believe.

He believed while practising bayonet drills beneath a hard white sky. He believed when the sergeant described the trenches as unpleasant but temporary, and when letters from home praised their courage. He believed when newspapers printed photographs of stern-faced battalions marching beneath flags.

At night he opened his father’s notebook.

For several evenings he wrote nothing. He sharpened his pencil, listened to the men breathing, and stared at the blank page. What could he put there? That his feet hurt? That he missed the river? Those seemed too small.

In late October, orders came.

Their battalion would cross to France.

The news moved through camp like an electric current. Men shouted, laughed, swore, and wrote letters. Billy found Thomas beside the railway siding, watching steam rise from the waiting transport.

“Nearly there,” Billy said.

Thomas felt the old stirring in his chest—the hunger that had brought him from the village, the sense of a great page turning.

“Nearly,” he answered.

That night, with the train rocking eastward through darkness, he opened the notebook once more. The carriage smelled of wool, tobacco, and damp leather. Billy slept with his mouth open, one hand curled around his rifle.

Thomas rested his pencil on the first page.

Outside, unseen fields slipped past beneath the stars. He listened to the wheels beating their steady rhythm and imagined banners lifting beyond the sea, imagined himself walking towards them with his comrades at his side.

The page remained blank.

His heart did not.

## Chapter 2: The Maze

France announced itself by smell.

Before Thomas saw the trenches, he smelled coal smoke, wet earth, and something sweet-sour beneath both, like meat left too long in a cupboard. The train had carried them through ruined villages and fields stripped bare. Now it stopped beside a cutting where a sergeant shouted directions, and the battalion spilled out with packs, rifles, and the nervous noise of men pretending not to be nervous.

Billy Preece adjusted the red scarf at his throat.

“Welcome to the continent,” he said. “Mind the holes.”

Thomas stepped down and sank to his ankle in mud. It closed around his boot with a wet grip. When he pulled free, the mud sighed.

“Your first lesson,” Billy said. “The army has given the earth permission to eat you.”

They marched along a track of broken boards and puddles filmed with oil. Far away, guns spoke in slow succession—not the sharp crack of rifles, but a grinding thunder that seemed to come from beneath the ground. Each report travelled through Thomas’s boots before reaching his ears.

He had imagined the front as a place one entered beneath banners, with officers calling men forward and the horizon bright with purpose. Instead there were lorries coughing smoke, men bent under loads, and a horse lying stiff beside the road with its legs drawn beneath it.

At a communication trench, a broad, grey-moustached corporal counted them over.

“Merrick?”

“Here, Corporal.”

“Preece?”

“Still regrettably alive.”

The corporal studied them. His face was weathered, his eyes pale as old glass.

“Haskett. You’re in my section. Keep your heads down, your rifles clean, and your feet dry. You’ll fail at least two of those before supper.”

He turned and began down the trench without checking whether they followed.

The trench was not a line but a crooked passage, bending and doubling upon itself. Duckboards floated in brown water. Timber supports leaned at uncertain angles. Men appeared from side passages and vanished into others, pale faces beneath helmets, carrying tins, ammunition, and coils of wire. Thomas lost sight of Haskett twice and found him waiting each time with patient disappointment.

“First time?” Haskett asked.

“Yes, Corporal.”

“Thought so. You’re looking for the end.”

“Is there one?”

“There’s several. None of them good.”

Their dugout was a dark chamber beneath the trench, thick with damp wool, paraffin, and men. A candle burned in a bottle. Someone had chalked a crude woman on one wall, her features blurred by smoke. Thomas was shown a narrow board where he could sit and a place to stack his kit.

Then a young officer entered, ducking beneath the beam.

He was scarcely older than Thomas’s cousin, with a narrow face and bright, restless eyes. Mud streaked his tunic, but his boots were clean.

“Lieutenant Allingham,” Haskett said. “New men, sir.”

“All right. Merrick, Preece.” The lieutenant smiled, though his attention seemed fixed on something beyond the dugout. “Welcome to our little kingdom.”

A shell landed somewhere behind the line. The candle guttered. Dust sifted from the roof.

Allingham looked upward and murmured, almost to himself, “The weariness, the fever, and the fret.”

Thomas recognized the words. “Keats, sir?”

The lieutenant’s eyes settled on him. “You read?”

“A little.”

“Then keep reading. It may save your life. Or make you understand why it needs saving.”

He smiled again and went out.

Billy waited until he was gone. “Does poetry stop shrapnel?”

“Perhaps not.”

“Then I’ll stick with a helmet.”

By evening, Thomas had learned that the trench possessed its own hours. At stand-to, before dawn and again at dusk, every man climbed to the fire step with his rifle ready. The enemy parapet showed as a broken black ridge across the mud, perhaps a hundred yards away, though the distance seemed both vast and intimate. No one spoke above a whisper.

Thomas tried to see movement opposite. He saw only sandbags, wire, and mist crawling low over the ground.

“Eyes adjust,” Haskett muttered beside him. “Don’t force them.”

The guns were quiet for nearly ten minutes. In that silence Thomas heard water dripping, a rat scratching beneath the boards, and his own heart beating in his throat.

Then a flare rose from the German line.

The world became white. Torn sandbags, wire, and puddles shone with terrible clarity. A rat perched on the parapet, its whiskers silvered, before darting away. The flare faded, leaving the darkness deeper than before.

“Stand down,” Haskett said.

Thomas climbed carefully from the fire step. His boot slipped between two boards, and he caught himself against the wall.

“Careful,” Billy whispered.

“I’m fine.”

“No one asked.”

Later, Haskett sent Thomas on sentry duty with an older private named Lomas. Rain began tapping against their helmets. Lomas showed him how to rest the rifle without letting its barrel touch the sandbag, how to listen for the difference between incoming and outgoing shells, and how to wake the man behind him without shouting.

“Most important,” Lomas said, “if you hear nothing, don’t imagine it means nothing’s happening.”

Thomas tried to obey. The darkness seemed full of hidden intentions.

The shell came without warning.

A shriek tore across the sky. Lomas shouted, “Down!”

Thomas turned toward him instead of obeying. The explosion struck the trench twenty yards away. Earth and timber leapt upward. The shock flung Thomas against the wall, and for several seconds he heard nothing but a high, thin ringing.

When his hearing returned, someone was screaming.

He found himself on his knees, rifle clutched to his chest. Mud covered his hands. A splinter had cut his cheek, though he did not feel it.

Lomas seized his collar. “You hit?”

Thomas swallowed. “No.”

“Then move.”

They stumbled toward the dugout. Men were hauling a wounded soldier from the wrecked section, his trousers dark with blood. Another man stood staring at the crater as though he had forgotten what trenches were for.

Inside, Billy pressed a sleeve against Thomas’s cheek.

“Just a scratch,” he said.

Thomas looked at the blood on Billy’s cuff. His hands had begun to shake. He tucked them beneath his arms.

Haskett noticed. Of course he noticed.

“Cold?” the corporal asked.

“Yes.”

“Then be cold quietly.”

Thomas nodded, ashamed. He imagined his father at the village schoolhouse, bending over exercise books beneath the tall windows. He imagined the brown notebook in his pack, still blank. There was nothing he could write that would explain the dark passages, the stink of wet earth, or the sound a man made when dragged from beneath a fallen beam.

Above them, the guns began again, one after another, until the whole earth seemed to grind its teeth.

Thomas sat among the others and listened. He told himself that fear was merely the body preparing for courage.

He did not yet know that fear could remain after courage had spent itself.

## Chapter 3: Eve of Battle

The order came down at noon, carried along the trench by a runner whose boots made small sucking sounds in the mud.

“Raid tonight,” Corporal Haskett said.

He crouched beneath the duckboards, examining a rifle bolt by candlelight. Around him, the men sat on ammunition boxes or leaned against the timber supports. No one asked what sort of raid. The word was enough.

Thomas felt the blood leave his hands.

“German front line?” Billy Preece asked.

“Unless you’ve heard they’ve moved Berlin closer,” Haskett replied. “Clean your rifle.”

Thomas took his weapon into his lap. He had cleaned it every day since arriving, but now every grain of mud seemed a fatal error. He worked the bolt, wiped the metal, inspected the chamber. His fingers trembled.

Billy noticed. “It’s only a rifle, lad.”

“It’s meant to fire.”

“That’s generally the idea.”

Thomas managed a weak laugh. It vanished beneath the distant growl of artillery.

The orders came in fragments. After dark, a party would cross no-man’s-land, cut through the wire, enter the German trench, and return with prisoners or information. Maps, unit markings—anything useful. Lieutenant Allingham had explained it in a voice too quick to sound calm.

“Keep low,” he had said. “No firing unless necessary. Bayonets only if—”

He had stopped there.

Now Haskett caught Thomas’s boot and pulled it onto his knee.

“Your puttee’s loose.”

“I can do it.”

“I can see that.” He unwound the strip of cloth. “You’ve wrapped it like a parcel. It’ll hold water against your leg. That’s how you lose a foot without seeing the enemy.”

He wound it again, firm but not tight, from ankle to calf.

“Like this. The army gives you a rifle and expects you to know the rest by dying of it.”

Billy held out his own leg. “Do mine, Corporal.”

“Do your own.”

“Mine’s artistically arranged.”

“It’s a disgrace.”

The exchange drew a few tired smiles. Thomas copied Haskett’s method, smoothing the cloth and fastening the buckle. The small task steadied him. Outside, guns muttered along the horizon. The trench shuddered now and then, but the dugout held.

At dusk a tin of rum appeared. It passed from hand to hand, each man taking a mouthful. When it reached Thomas, the smell was sharp and medicinal. He drank, and fire ran down his throat.

Billy took the tin. “Careful. You’ll become a poet.”

“I’m already a poet.”

“You’re a schoolmaster’s son who reads poetry. Same thing.”

“Not in Wales.”

Billy drank and passed it on. For a while they sat shoulder to shoulder, listening to the men talk. Someone unfolded a photograph of a baby. Someone else wrote a letter by the candle. A man at the far end hummed a song about a girl in Birmingham and forgot the second verse.

Thomas thought of home: the schoolhouse windows glowing against the evening, his father moving between desks with chalk dust on his sleeves. He wondered whether the old clock still lost seven minutes a day. He wondered if his father had read the newspapers and imagined his son among the fighting.

Lieutenant Allingham entered carrying his helmet under one arm. His collar was unbuttoned, and mud filmed his clean boots. He paced the narrow dugout, turning at each end.

“Do you know what Keats wrote about autumn?” he asked.

Billy glanced at Thomas. “Is this the part where poetry stops shrapnel?”

Allingham seemed not to hear.

“Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness,” he murmured. “Close bosom-friend of the maturing sun.”

A shell landed beyond the trench. Dust sifted from the roof.

“Beautiful,” Allingham said.

Thomas watched his bright, restless eyes.

“Sir,” he asked, “will you be coming with us?”

The lieutenant stopped. For an instant he looked very young.

“Yes,” he said. “I’ll be there.”

Another shell fell closer. The candle guttered, and Haskett swore as he checked the supports.

When darkness settled, Thomas climbed to the fire step with Billy and looked over the parapet. The sky had cleared. Stars crowded above the torn earth.

No-man’s-land lay pale and broken below: craters black with water, tangles of wire, shattered posts. A flare rose far away and turned the mud silver.

Thomas had seen stars from the hill behind his village, but never like this. There, they had belonged to peace. Here they shone above the wreckage as if heaven knew nothing of men.

“Pretty,” Billy said.

Thomas nodded.

“For a place trying to kill us.”

“That’s the strange part.”

Billy looked at him. “You’ll make a poet yet.”

Haskett ordered them down.

Later, in the dugout, Thomas opened the brown leather notebook his father had given him. For weeks the pages had remained blank. He had thought a diary should contain important things, while soldiering seemed made of damp socks, bad tea, and waiting.

Tonight, everything seemed important.

He bent over the page.

*October 31st, 1916*

*We have been told that we are to make a raid tomorrow night. I am afraid, though I do not think I ought to write that. It seems cowardly to put fear into words, where it may remain after the fear itself has passed.*

*The men are cheerful. Billy says the Germans will be offended if we do not visit them. Corporal Haskett has shown me how to wrap my puttees properly. I have cleaned my rifle twice. Lieutenant Allingham recited Keats while the shells fell, which was both ridiculous and beautiful.*

*I keep thinking of Father and the schoolhouse. I wonder whether he would say I am ready. I want to believe that I am.*

*Tomorrow I may prove myself a man.*

*I do not know if proving it means being brave, or merely doing what I am told while frightened. Perhaps they are the same thing.*

*There are stars tonight above the German wire. They are very clear. I have never seen anything so beautiful, and I wish I could show them to someone at home.*

*Tomorrow may bring glory. It may bring death. I pray that I shall not disgrace myself, and that all of us shall return.*

*I am afraid. I am hopeful. I think these two things can live together.*

Thomas listened to the guns. Then he closed the notebook and held it against his chest until his hands grew still.

## Chapter 4: The Raid

Before dawn, the guns began.

They did not begin so much as wake all at once. One moment the trench was black and crowded with men; the next, the earth leapt beneath them and the sky opened in orange flashes. Shells shrieked overhead and burst beyond the German line. Dirt sifted down the back of Thomas’s neck.

A man nearby prayed in a low, hurried voice. Billy Preece shouted something that vanished in the concussion.

“What?” Thomas yelled.

“I said,” Billy roared, “if we live through this, I’m never complaining about the rain again.”

Another shell fell short. The trench floor bucked. Thomas’s teeth struck together, and for an instant he saw nothing but white.

When his sight returned, Haskett was moving among them, checking straps and bayonets as though they were preparing for inspection.

“Keep your heads,” the corporal said. “Don’t bunch up. If you fall, get out of the way.”

Lieutenant Allingham appeared at the traverse, helmet askew and face pale beneath the grime. In one hand he held his revolver; in the other, wire cutters.

“The barrage is doing its work,” he said.

A shell burst beyond the parapet. The lieutenant flinched.

“Hope it is, sir,” Haskett replied.

The bombardment rolled on. Thomas tried to think of the notebook tucked inside his tunic, of the words he had written only hours before: Tomorrow I may prove myself a man.

The sentence seemed to belong to another boy.

Then the whistle shrilled.

For one beat nobody moved. Then the men climbed.

Thomas pulled himself over the sandbags and saw the world beyond the trench transformed. The wire lay flattened in places and snarled in others. Craters brimmed with black water. Smoke crawled over the ground. Flares climbed above the German line, opening into green-white suns.

He dropped into the mud. It seized his boots at once. He stumbled, recovered, and ran—or tried to. The man ahead disappeared into a crater. A machine gun began to fire.

Its sound was a ripping chatter, as if some enormous insect were tearing the dark apart. Mud leapt in a line before them. Men fell. Someone screamed for his mother.

Thomas threw himself down. The earth struck his chest. Under the glare of a flare, he saw Haskett crouched behind a mound, Allingham waving men forward, and Billy crawling through the mud with his red scarf blackened and wet.

Thomas rose because Billy was rising.

A strand of wire caught his tunic and spun him around. He tugged at it, hands slipping, while bullets snapped overhead.

“Leave it!” Haskett shouted.

Thomas tore free, leaving a strip of cloth on the wire, and reached the German trench with the others. He fell into it, landing on a body.

For a moment he thought the man alive. Then he saw the hole beneath his ear.

A German soldier came around the corner.

He was young—no older than Thomas, perhaps younger. His face was smooth and pale with terror. A bayonet trembled on the end of his rifle.

They stared at each other.

The German’s lips moved. Thomas could not hear him over the guns. Perhaps he was calling to someone. Perhaps he was saying his mother’s name. The thought struck Thomas with absurd force.

The boy lunged.

Thomas stepped aside and fired.

The rifle kicked against his shoulder. The German struck the trench wall. His eyes remained open, astonished, as though Thomas had performed some impossible trick.

Thomas stood with the rifle hanging in his hands.

“Move!” Haskett bellowed.

The trench erupted around him. British soldiers crowded through the narrow passage, shouting and firing. A grenade burst ahead, filling the air with dust and splinters. Thomas looked again at the German boy. Blood darkened his tunic. His mouth worked soundlessly.

Thomas crouched beside him without knowing why.

“Are you hurt?” he whispered.

The question was foolish. The boy’s eyes found his, and Thomas saw there not hatred but the same fear that had lived in his own body since the first shell fell.

Then the boy’s gaze slipped away.

Thomas rose, shaking so badly he could scarcely hold the rifle.

The raid had become a confusion of orders and smoke. A dugout collapsed. Someone dragged out a map case. Someone shouted that the wire party was returning. Flares whitened the trench, then turned it red. Men ran past carrying wounded.

“Back!” Allingham shouted. “Back to our line!”

Thomas followed Billy through a gap in the parapet and into no-man’s-land. The machine guns found them again. Mud burst around their feet. A man ahead spun and fell.

Thomas reached for him, but Billy seized his shoulder.

“No!”

“He’s alive!”

“He’s not ours to save!”

Billy dragged him onward. Thomas ran with his head down, the dead boy’s face flashing before him each time he blinked.

Then the guns stopped.

Not all at once. One ceased, then another, until silence gathered in the spaces between the remaining shots. The men crouched in a shell crater, breathing hard. Somewhere nearby, a wounded soldier whimpered.

Thomas looked up.

A skylark rose from the torn earth.

It climbed uncertainly from a ridge of mud and broken wire, then found the air. Its song spilled into the gray morning, clear and intricate, untouched by the guns, the bodies, or the men pressed into the crater.

No one spoke.

The bird climbed higher, almost invisible against the paling sky. Thomas felt something break inside him—not loudly, like a shell, but with the quiet finality of ice giving way beneath a foot.

All his life, beauty had seemed proof that the world was ordered. Poems had taught him that courage made suffering meaningful, that sacrifice rose cleanly from blood, that men became noble by facing death.

The skylark knew none of this. It sang because it was alive.

“Come on,” Billy said.

They reached the British trench as the sun lifted behind the smoke. Haskett hauled Thomas over the parapet and pushed him down among the duckboards.

“Sit.”

Thomas sat. His rifle slid from his hands. Mud covered his sleeves, knees, and face. Around him, men counted one another, called for bandages, and searched for the missing. Allingham stood at the entrance, staring toward the German line. His helmet was gone. His lips moved, but no poetry came.

Billy dropped beside Thomas.

“You all right?”

Thomas looked at him.

“I shot a boy.”

Billy’s expression tightened. “We all did what we had to.”

“That isn’t what I said.”

For a while Billy did not answer. Then he unwound the red scarf from his neck, wrung water from it, and handed it over.

“Wipe your face.”

Thomas obeyed. The cloth smelled of smoke and wet wool.

Beyond the parapet, the skylark was still singing. Its voice reached them faintly through the ruin, beautiful and unbearable.

Thomas thought of his father, the schoolhouse, and the blank pages he had once believed he would fill with adventures. He knew now that no poem could make this clean. No banner could cover the German boy’s frightened eyes. No word like glory could hold what had happened in that trench.

He sat beside Billy while the wounded were carried past and listened to the lark until its song disappeared beneath the returning guns.

## Chapter 5: Duckboards and Rain

The dead were buried at noon, behind the support trench where the ground rose slightly and the German guns had not yet found the range.

Some had names. Some had pockets containing letters, photographs, tobacco, or buttons. Some had nothing by which they could be distinguished from the mud. The chaplain spoke, but the wind carried half his words away.

Thomas stood among the burial party with a spade in his hands. He had not spoken since the raid.

One of the dead had lost a boot. His grey sock showed through the mud, and his bare foot pointed upward, as if it still searched for a step.

“Don’t look at that,” Billy murmured beside him.

Thomas looked anyway.

The earth fell in heavy clods. With every dull impact he saw again the German boy’s face widening behind the bayonet. Thomas had imagined that the boy might be calling for his mother. Perhaps he had called for God. Perhaps for nothing.

When the burial was finished, the men returned to the trench. No one spoke. Speech had become dangerous. It let things out.

At the fire step, Corporal Haskett caught Thomas by the sleeve.

“Boots off.”

Thomas stared at him.

“Did you hear me, Merrick?”

“I’m all right.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Haskett pulled him into a dugout scarcely large enough for three men. The roof sagged with damp, and a candle guttered in a bottle neck. He pointed at Thomas’s feet.

“Off.”

Thomas obeyed. His boots came away with a sucking sound. His socks were soaked through and grey around the toes.

“You want trench foot?” Haskett asked.

“No.”

“Then dry them.”

“There’s nowhere dry.”

“Make somewhere.”

From beneath a plank, Haskett produced a rag and a small tin of grease. He rubbed Thomas’s feet until the skin burned.

“Every chance you get,” he said. “Socks off. Rub them. Grease them. Change them if you can. Keep your boots loose when you sleep and tight when you walk. A man can lose his feet without a bullet ever touching him.”

Thomas watched the corporal’s cracked hands. Those hands had dragged him from the trench lip after the raid, slapped mud from his face, and forced him down when the shells began falling.

“Thank you,” Thomas said.

“Don’t thank me. Learn.”

Haskett showed him how to wrap his puttees so they supported the ankle without cutting off the blood. Then he showed him how to sleep.

“Five minutes,” he said. “That’s all you need.”

“I can’t sleep.”

“You can. You’ll sleep standing if you have to.”

Thomas leaned against the wall. Haskett wedged a folded greatcoat behind his neck and set the rifle beside him.

“Close your eyes.”

Thomas heard every sound: boots scraping along the duckboards, guns muttering in the distance, a man coughing in the next bay, water dripping from the roof. Then, without warning, he fell asleep.

He woke with a start.

Haskett was shaking his shoulder. “Five minutes.”

Thomas had dreamed of the German trench. In the dream, the boy stood beneath a summer tree, wearing no uniform. Waking made it no better.

“Again,” Haskett said.

“I’m not tired.”

Haskett looked at him. “That’s because you’re stupid.”

A shell burst beyond the parapet. Dust sifted from the roof. Thomas flinched.

“Cold quietly,” Haskett said.

Thomas understood now that the words were not a rebuke. They were an instruction, almost a kindness.

The company spent the next day counting what remained. Names were called twice because the first time no one answered. Three men had been killed outright. Five were missing. Eleven had gone to the dressing station, though two would not return.

Billy came back from a ration detail with a tin mug of tea and a cigarette tucked behind his ear.

“Found this beauty,” he said. “French tobacco. Tastes like a burnt boot, which proves its authenticity.”

Thomas took the cigarette. His fingers shook as he struck the match.

Billy noticed but said nothing. He lit his own from the same flame.

Across the trench, Private Lomas tasted his tea. “If this is what the army calls tea, I’d hate to see its coffee.”

“It’s not tea,” another man said. “It’s water that’s heard of tea.”

The men laughed. The sound was thin at first, then warmer.

Thomas found himself smiling. The German boy remained, as did the dead in their shallow row. But Billy’s shoulder pressed against his, and smoke curled between them. For a few seconds, Thomas was not alone inside his own head.

Lieutenant Allingham had been worse since the raid. He paced the fire step with his revolver hanging loose in one hand, his face pale beneath the grime. When the guns began, he recited poetry under his breath.

“Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird,” he murmured.

“Sir?” Thomas said.

Allingham stopped. He looked at Thomas as if seeing him for the first time.

“Keats,” he said. “The nightingale.”

A shell struck close enough to rock the trench. Allingham gripped the parapet.

“Thou wast not born for death,” he repeated, louder.

“Begging your pardon, sir,” Billy called, “but if the bird’s got any sense, it’s gone south.”

Allingham stared at him. Then he laughed once, sharply, almost painfully.

“Yes,” he said. “Perhaps it has.”

The rain began after dark.

At first it tapped softly on the sandbags. Then it thickened into a steady roar. Water ran down the trench walls in muddy fingers. The duckboards vanished beneath brown water.

Men raised ammunition boxes onto ledges and tied blankets above their heads. One soldier cursed the rain, the army, the Kaiser, and the Almighty in a single breath.

“Save your strength,” Billy told him. “There’ll be plenty left to curse tomorrow.”

By midnight the water reached their ankles. Thomas held his rifle beneath his coat. His feet were numb despite Haskett’s instructions.

Haskett moved along the line, checking each man.

“Keep moving your toes,” he ordered. “If you can’t feel them, stamp.”

Thomas stamped. Mud splashed his trousers.

Beyond the parapet, no-man’s-land had disappeared. There were no stars, no flares, no horizon—only rain, earth, and the low trembling of guns.

Thomas had once imagined battles as moments: the charge, the clash, the banner above the smoke. But the war did not remain in moments. It soaked through socks and coats. It entered the lungs of sleeping men and waited beside them when they woke.

It was not a battle.

It was a condition.

Thomas stood beside Billy in the rain, shoulder to shoulder with men who had been strangers a week before, and listened to the guns breathing in the dark.

## Chapter 6: Gas

The alarm entered his sleep as a school bell.

For a moment Thomas was late for lessons. His father stood beside the blackboard, waiting for him to take his seat. Then the bell became a hammer beating metal, and someone was shouting a single word along the trench.

“Gas!”

He woke beneath his groundsheet with his mouth open.

All around him men were becoming strange, blind-faced creatures. Haskett had his mask on already and was striking a suspended shell case. Billy’s hands moved at his own face. Thomas reached for the satchel against his chest and could not make his fingers find the fastening.

He had practised this. On a field in England he had done it quickly enough to please a sergeant.

The flap slipped from his hand.

“Mask, Tom!”

He drew breath to answer.

Something hot and foul went down his throat. Not smoke: it seemed to bite inside him. He coughed, lost the mask, caught it by a strap. His eyes filled so abruptly that the trench became a row of wavering lights.

Billy seized his wrists, pushed them aside, and hauled the mask into place. Rubber dragged against Thomas’s cheek. He fought it for a moment, certain that it was stopping his breath, until Billy shook him.

“Leave it!”

The voice came thickly through rubber and cloth.

Thomas tried to breathe. Air came, but the burning stayed.

A low cloud was moving along the trench, yellow-green where a lamp caught it, almost colourless beyond. It folded round the sandbags and filled the hollows in the duckboards. He could see no end to it. Somewhere a man was coughing with a violence that made Thomas’s own chest tighten in sympathy.

Haskett pointed towards the communication trench. Billy hooked an arm beneath Thomas’s shoulder.

They went stumbling through the narrow turns. The eyepieces clouded; each lamp swelled into a blurred moon. Thomas’s boots struck wood, water, something soft he dared not identify. He coughed into the mask and tasted sourness. There was nowhere for the cough to go. It shook him until his knees folded.

Billy dragged him upright.

“Not here. Come on.”

Thomas wanted to say that he was coming. He wanted Billy to stop pulling because every jolt hurt. Instead he hung against him, furious and helpless, while the trench wall scraped his sleeve.

Behind them the alarm kept ringing.

***

At the aid post someone took away his mask.

He was on a bench beneath a blanket, and then on the floor, kneeling over a basin while a hand held his forehead. He vomited until there was nothing left but strings of bitter fluid. Each attempt to draw breath brought another cough.

“How long before you got it on?”

He tried to answer the medical officer. No sound came except a thin rasp.

“Not long,” Billy said. “A breath. Perhaps two.”

The officer listened to Thomas’s chest. His fingers were cold through the shirt.

“Keep him quiet. No walking about.”

A stretcher passed close enough for its blanket to brush Thomas’s boot. The man upon it lay very still, except for the quick movement at his throat.

Thomas watched that movement until an orderly stood between them.

They put him on a narrow cot. The roof above was supported by beams with bark still clinging to them. Water gathered on one beam and fell beside his ear. He began waiting for each drop. When one took too long, he became frightened that time had stopped.

Billy sat on an ammunition box with his mask loose about his neck. There were pale grooves in his dirty face where it had pressed.

“You look a picture,” he said.

Thomas tried to laugh. Pain opened beneath his ribs.

“Don’t encourage him,” the orderly told Billy.

“Never known him need encouragement before.”

Thomas shut his eyes.

His father was washing the classroom windows. The water ran green down the glass. Thomas wanted to warn him not to open them, but his father could not hear. Then the German boy stood outside, looking in. He lifted a hand as though asking permission to enter.

Thomas struggled upright.

Billy caught him before he fell.

“No. Stay there.”

“The window.”

“What window?”

“Don’t let him—”

“All right.” Billy held his shoulders. “It’s shut.”

Thomas searched the dugout wall. There was only earth, a hanging coat, the little wavering flame of a candle. He could not understand where the window had gone.

Billy eased him down without letting go.

***

The worst hours came after midnight.

Thomas had believed that reaching the aid post meant he was safe. Yet his chest grew tighter while he lay still. Men came and listened to him, spoke beyond his hearing, came back again. He watched their faces for something they would not tell him.

He had imagined death arriving with a visible wound, something a man could put his hand over. Now nothing showed. His tunic was whole. Beneath it, each breath required work, and when he managed one he had immediately to begin another.

“Billy.”

“I’m here.”

“Go to sleep.”

“On that box? You must think I’m a miner.”

Thomas turned his head. Billy’s grin looked strained in the candlelight.

“Will they want you back?”

“Probably.”

“You ought—”

“You can report me when you’ve got your voice.”

Thomas lay quiet. After a while he moved his hand beyond the blanket. Billy took it, awkwardly, as though helping him across a ditch.

Neither spoke of it.

***

By morning he could breathe without lifting his shoulders.

Recovery came slowly and made no promises. Some hours he felt almost well; then a cough bent him double and left him trembling. The medical officer said there might be lasting damage. Rest first, he said, and they would see.

On the fourth day Thomas stood at a basin to wash. A piece of mirror hung above it by a twist of wire.

He had expected mud, perhaps a beard beginning. Instead he found a narrow face with bruised-looking hollows beneath the eyes. His lips were cracked. When he lifted a hand to his cheek, the stranger did the same.

Behind him Billy waited with a clean shirt.

“Still you,” he said.

Thomas looked away from the mirror.

“I had the thing beside me. All that time.”

“I know.”

“I couldn’t open it.”

Billy put the shirt on the cot. “Next time I might be the one fumbling.”

Thomas wanted to object, but Billy was looking at him steadily.

He sat down before his legs gave way. His rifle had not been fired that night. No bullet had touched him. A fastening had stuck beneath his fingers, he had taken a breath, and now he could not cross a room without resting.

Billy sat beside him. From beyond the dressing station came the rattle of a cart and a driver coaxing his horse.

Thomas listened until his breathing eased. When Billy offered him the cup, he steadied it with both hands.

## Chapter 7: The Lull

By the time Thomas returned to the company, the water in the shell holes had acquired a skin of ice.

He came back by stages: first to a billet where he slept beneath a roof that admitted daylight but kept out most of the rain, then to carrying stores, and finally along the communication trench with his pack dragging at his shoulders. Twice he stopped to cough. Billy stopped too, finding something wrong with his boot each time.

Haskett inspected him without greeting.

“What did the doctor say?”

“Light duties.”

“Then you’ll have light duties. Plenty of heavy ones for the rest of these idle bastards.”

He took a coil of wire from Thomas’s pack and handed it to another man. Nothing more was said.

For several days the guns seemed occupied elsewhere. There were occasional shells, the distant chattering of a machine gun, a rifle shot that made every conversation hesitate. But there were hours when a man could hear water travelling beneath the duckboards.

The quiet was not peace. Thomas still reached for his mask when someone struck a suspended tin with a spoon. Yet he began to sleep for longer than he meant to, and once woke to discover that Billy had eaten his ration of jam.

“Medical advice,” Billy said. “Sweet things are bad for your chest.”

“You’d better take my cough as well.”

“No room. Full of jam.”

Thomas laughed, then coughed until his eyes watered. Billy waited, holding out the mug of tea without looking at him.

***

Letters arrived in a sack stiff with old mud.

A man named Ellis received three from his wife and read them in the wrong order, announcing with satisfaction that the baby had recovered from an illness he had not yet learned it possessed. Someone else received socks so large that Haskett suggested applying for a transfer to the navy and using them as hammocks.

Thomas’s father wrote about the school stove, which smoked whenever the wind turned east. The younger children had been collecting horse chestnuts for the war effort. Mrs Bell had asked after him. A branch had come down from the elm beside the church, missing the wall by inches.

At the bottom, below his careful signature, his father had added: *You need not find anything cheerful to tell me. I should like to know how you are.*

Thomas folded the letter along its creases.

“What’s home doing?” Billy asked.

“Waiting for the stove to be mended.”

“Same as the army, then.”

Billy’s own letter was crowded with small handwriting. He read parts aloud: a neighbour’s marriage, trouble with a hen, his mother’s opinion of the price of coal.

“Living on top of it and can’t afford to burn it,” he said.

Then he put the paper inside his tunic and sat a while with his palm resting over it.

That afternoon the men sang. The song began in the next traverse and arrived raggedly, some knowing the words and others supplying obscenities of their own. Billy sang in a surprisingly gentle tenor. Thomas joined for the chorus, softly, saving his breath.

When the singing ended, Ellis said they ought to send a party across to find out whether the Germans had enjoyed it.

“Take a collection,” Billy said. “Might pay us to stop.”

***

Thomas found the flower while scraping mud from a shovel.

It grew on the inner slope of the parapet, below a torn sandbag: a small white daisy, its stalk bent almost flat, two petals browned at the tips. He had mistaken it at first for a scrap of paper.

“Billy.”

Something in his voice brought Billy quickly.

“What?”

Thomas pointed.

Billy leaned close. His hands, blistered from carrying timber, rose around the flower without touching it. He made a shelter with them against the wind.

“Well,” he said.

They looked at it.

Thomas could see the yellow centre, minutely patterned, and grains of earth caught where the petals joined. Behind it lay a splinter of wood and the rusted neck of a broken tin. He wondered how long it had been there while they passed beneath it with ammunition and buckets.

“Late for you,” Billy murmured.

“Perhaps it doesn’t know.”

“Lucky little sod.”

Neither laughed. Billy kept his hands cupped until the gust had passed, then lowered them carefully.

For the rest of the day Thomas found reasons to go that way. Once he saw Haskett glance at the flower and shift his elbow before resting it against the parapet.

***

That night the moon cleared the clouds.

Water shone in the craters. Strands of wire, invisible by daylight against the ruined ground, caught the light and held it. Thomas stood beside Allingham in a shallow recess, listening to a shell pass high overhead.

The lieutenant had become very thin. He kept rubbing the place on his finger where a ring had been.

“‘Already with thee! tender is the night,’” he said.

Thomas looked at him.

“Keats,” Allingham added, almost apologetically.

“I remember.”

“‘And haply the Queen-Moon is on her throne, / Cluster’d around by all her starry Fays.’”

Another shell whined over them. Both men waited. The explosion came far behind the line.

Allingham let out his breath.

“Rotten place to read him.”

Thomas looked across the silver pools. He remembered the skylark, its song reaching him in the crater when he could still feel the rifle’s recoil.

“Perhaps,” he said.

Allingham did not continue. They stood together until Thomas’s cough drove him back under cover.

***

In the morning, a fatigue party came to repair the parapet.

Thomas saw the shovel descending and called out. The man holding it paused, irritated, while Thomas worked the daisy free from the loosened earth. Its roots were shallow and came away broken.

He stood with it in his hand, foolishly sorry.

Billy found him sitting outside the dugout.

“Couldn’t leave it there,” Thomas said.

“No.”

“It’ll die now.”

Billy sat beside him. After a moment he said, “You can keep it, though.”

Thomas took out his father’s notebook. He opened it beyond the written pages and laid the flower between two clean leaves, straightening the bent stem with his smallest finger. He wrote nothing.

When he closed the book, a little earth remained on his knee. He brushed it away and slipped the notebook into his breast pocket.

Billy handed him his tea. This time, Thomas held it steady.

## Chapter 8: No Man's Land

The order came at dusk.

A section was to go out after midnight and repair the broken wire before the next bombardment. Two men would carry the coils, two the stakes and hammers, and the rest would cover them. The sergeant called for volunteers, though everyone understood that volunteering merely made the choosing less public.

“I’ll go,” Thomas said.

Billy looked up from cleaning his rifle. “You’ll not.”

“I’ve already volunteered.”

“Then un-volunteer.”

Thomas smiled faintly. “You make that sound simple.”

“You’re still coughing.”

“Not much.”

“You coughed twice while saying that.”

Haskett, winding a puttee around his ankle, glanced at them. “Preece. Pack the wire.”

Billy turned. “You’re taking him?”

“I’m taking whoever can crawl without falling asleep.”

“I don’t fall asleep.”

“You snore when you’re awake.”

The men nearby laughed. Billy did not. He rose and slung the coil over his shoulder.

“If you go,” he said, “I go.”

“It isn’t a picnic.”

“I know. I’ve been to one.”

Haskett gave them both a hard look. “Then try not to spoil it.”

They climbed over the parapet shortly after midnight.

A low mist covered the ground, pouring into the craters and curling around the broken stakes. Thomas pushed the coil before him as he crawled. Billy followed with the hammer and stakes tied across his back. Behind them, the British trench blurred into a line of lamps and whispers. Ahead lay no-man’s-land, its shell holes black with rain.

They moved by touch. Mud entered Thomas’s sleeves and mouth. His lungs rasped in the damp air. Once something skittered inside a crater, and he stopped.

“Rat,” Billy breathed.

Thomas went on.

They reached the gap in the wire. Shellfire had torn the old barrier upward, leaving twisted lengths stretched across the earth. Billy drove in the first stake while Thomas unwound the coil. Each blow of the hammer was soft and dull.

The mist thickened.

A flare rose from the German line.

For an instant, no-man’s-land shone green and white. The craters opened like wounds. A boot lay without its leg. In one hollow, a dead man’s hand showed pale above the mud.

Thomas saw Billy kneeling beside him.

The shot was hardly louder than a snapped twig.

Billy jerked forward. The hammer dropped from his hand. Thomas caught him beneath the shoulders.

“Billy?”

Billy looked down at the dark stain spreading beneath his collar.

“Ah,” he said, almost with surprise.

Rifle fire cracked across the field. Thomas pulled him into a crater as earth spat around them.

“Come on,” Thomas said.

Billy tried to push himself along, but his arms failed. Thomas hooked his hands beneath Billy’s armpits and dragged. The coil caught on a stake. He abandoned it.

“Leave me,” Billy whispered.

“Shut up.”

“You’ll get hit.”

“Shut up.”

They crawled over the crater’s rim. A machine gun opened somewhere to the left, beating across the darkness. Thomas threw himself over Billy and waited. The bullets passed above them.

When the firing stopped, he dragged again.

The British trench seemed impossibly distant. Haskett’s voice carried through the mist.

“Merrick! Bring him in!”

Thomas’s fingers slipped on Billy’s tunic. He caught hold again. Mud filled his sleeves. Billy felt strangely light, as if the wound had already taken some part of him away.

At last the trench wall rose before them. Hands reached down. Haskett seized Thomas by the back of his coat, while others pulled Billy over the parapet.

Billy lay on the duckboards, his face grey. Haskett pressed a dressing against the wound, then shook his head.

“No,” Thomas said.

Billy’s eyes opened.

“Tom.”

“I’m here.”

Billy’s hand moved across the boards. Thomas took it.

“Did we fix it?” Billy asked.

“No. We’ll go back.”

A faint breath escaped him. It might have been a laugh.

“Always was a poor workman.”

“You’ll be all right.”

“Don’t tell lies.”

His fingers loosened.

Thomas held them while the trench carried on around him: men shouting, boots passing, a stretcher scraping against the wall. Above them, a shell crossed the darkness with a hollow shriek.

Thomas did not flinch.

In the morning, Haskett had Billy carried to a patch of ground behind the reserve trench. The graves there were shallow and crowded. Thomas took a broken entrenching tool and began to dig.

The earth was frozen in places. His gloves split, and blood stained the handle. Haskett came once and told him to stop. Thomas kept digging.

They lowered Billy in his greatcoat. Someone had folded the red scarf across his chest.

Thomas removed the notebook from his pocket. Between its pages lay the pressed white flower, browned at the edges. He placed it in Billy’s hands.

“Lucky little sod,” he whispered.

The earth came down.

That afternoon the guns began again. Men ducked as shells burst along the German line. A new recruit dropped flat with his hands over his head.

Thomas stood on the fire step.

The explosions no longer seemed directed at him. Nothing did. The war had taken the German boy, then Billy, and between those losses there was no room left for fear of what might happen to Thomas himself.

That evening he opened his father’s notebook and began a letter to Mrs. Preece.

Dear Mrs. Preece,

I am very sorry to tell you that Billy died bravely while carrying out his duty. He was a good soldier and a fine comrade, and all of us admired his courage. He thought of home until the end. You may take comfort in knowing that he did not suffer for long, and that he was buried with his friends.

Thomas stopped.

The sentences were neat and respectful. They were also false in every way that mattered. Billy had suffered. He had asked not to be lied to. No words could hold the mud, the blood, the surprise in his voice, or the warmth leaving his hand.

A shell struck nearby, shaking dust from the dugout beams.

Thomas dipped his pen again and continued writing.

## Chapter 9: The Long Dark

The months lost their names.

There was only rain and the intervals between rain; mud that swallowed boots and mud that hardened around them; the changing of the boards beneath their feet and the changing of the men upon them. Thomas learned that a battalion could disappear without a battle anyone would remember. A trench collapsed. Fever took three men. A shell found the cookhouse. A patrol returned two short.

He became a lance corporal in March. Haskett handed him the stripe as though it were another piece of equipment to keep clean.

“You’re responsible for telling fools when to duck.”

“Does it come with extra pay?”

“It comes with extra names to remember.”

Thomas sewed the chevron on himself. His needle shook less than it had when he first arrived.

He could distribute rations in darkness, wake a sleeping sentry without making him cry out, and judge which stretch of trench would flood first. Men obeyed him because he did not waste their strength. During bombardments he spoke quietly, giving each man something small to do.

Sometimes he felt nothing while he did it. Afterward, when his hands trembled, he put them in his pockets.

New recruits arrived with smooth faces and shining buttons. One, a boy called Wilkes, asked whether the German trenches were really as close as people said.

“Close enough.”

“Have you been in them?”

“Yes.”

“What are they like?”

Thomas remembered astonished eyes, a bayonet, the weight of another boy resisting it.

“Wet,” he said.

Wilkes laughed. Two weeks later a shell struck while he was carrying tea. They buried what they could find behind the reserve line. Rain drummed on Thomas’s helmet while the chaplain read.

There was no time to stand afterward. Someone needed the stretcher; someone shouted for men to repair the traverse. Thomas wiped the spade and went back.

That evening he could not remember whether he had said Wilkes’s name.

Lieutenant Allingham went in May.

A shell struck the dugout entrance at dusk. Thomas found him sitting against the wall with one hand pressed to his thigh. Blood slipped between his fingers.

“Only a scratch,” Allingham said.

“It isn’t, sir.”

“No.” The lieutenant looked down. “I suppose it isn’t.”

As the bearers lifted him, he caught Thomas’s sleeve.

“Have you read the new volume of—”

He stopped. His free hand moved toward the bare place where his ring had been.

“Never mind. Tell them at home I was brave.”

“Tell them yourself, sir.”

Allingham managed a smile. “Preferable, certainly.”

Word came that he had reached England. No letter followed. Thomas sometimes pictured him in a white bed, with a window opening onto trees, trying to remember a line he had once known by heart.

Summer brought dust and flies. Then there was another winter, and another spring, and the Germans came forward in such numbers that the men scarcely slept. Thomas turned eighteen, then nineteen. His father’s letters arrived in batches, their dates already belonging to another world.

He answered when he could. He said that his boots were sound, that he had received the parcel, that he hoped the school roof had been mended. He did not open the diary to write. Between its pages remained the pale outline left by Billy’s flower.

In September, Haskett lost his leg.

Thomas was inspecting duckboards when the shell burst behind them. He found himself facedown, his mouth full of mud. Haskett lay beside the fire step. Below one knee there was nothing Thomas could recognize.

“Don’t stare,” Haskett said.

Thomas crawled to him and shouted for bearers. He tightened the tourniquet with a piece of broken wood while Haskett gripped the edge of a board.

“Keep talking,” Thomas said.

“About what?”

“Anything.”

“Your bloody feet, then. Still wearing those socks?”

The bearers reached them. As they lifted him, Haskett held out his hand.

“Keep the lads in order.”

“I will.”

“And yourself.”

Thomas took his hand. The handshake was firm and absurdly formal. Then the stretcher went around the traverse, leaving a dark smear along the boards.

Thomas stood until someone asked him for orders.

In October the rumors returned: Germany was finished; peace had been offered; the fighting would end before Christmas. Men repeated each report, then bent over their rifles again. They had heard such things before.

Thomas checked ammunition and posted sentries. At night he sometimes remembered Billy’s scarf folded across his chest in the grave. He would press his empty hands together beneath his blanket until they warmed.

On the morning of the eleventh of November, orders reached them that firing would cease at eleven.

A man laughed and asked whether the Germans had received the same orders. Another began packing his kit until Thomas told him to leave it.

The guns continued. Each explosion seemed suddenly wasteful in a new and unbearable way. Thomas watched the minute hand on a borrowed watch, then looked away from it.

At eleven, the firing stopped.

A few distant reports followed, too late, and their echoes rolled away.

Thomas stood on the fire step with his field glasses lowered. He could hear water dripping from a broken board. Cloth scraped as a man beside him shifted his shoulders. Somewhere beyond the wire a bird called.

Nobody answered it.

The men waited. One crouched when a loose plank fell. Another removed his helmet and held it against his stomach. A soldier farther down the trench began to cry, quietly at first, then with the helpless gasping of someone who could not get enough air.

Thomas looked from face to face. These men were still here. There would be no need, this morning, to choose who must go out and who might stay.

He felt no joy yet. Only a loosening, as when a hand releases the throat.

The guns were quiet.

Thomas listened, and listened, for the sound beneath the silence.

## Chapter 10: The Silence and the Sound

That evening someone found a candle, and they sat about it as if it were a fire.

There was no need to shade the flame. This seemed to Thomas almost more extraordinary than the silence. A man could strike a match without drawing death towards his face. Along the trench, small lights appeared in dugout mouths.

“They’ll have fireworks in London,” someone said.

“Rather them than me.”

A few men laughed. Then they listened again.

Thomas took the brown notebook from his breast pocket. The leather had swollen and dried so often that it no longer closed squarely. On the first written page he found the boy who had expected battle to settle the question of what he was.

*Tomorrow I may prove myself a man.*

He passed his thumb over the words. He remembered writing them, the pressure of his knee beneath the book, Billy beside him with the rum. He did not cross them out.

Farther on lay the pale mark of the daisy. He turned to a clean page.

*11 November 1918.*

*The guns are quiet and I cannot trust it. I hear the voices of the dead in every wind.*

He stopped. The candle guttered, and a drop of wax fell on his sleeve.

*Billy ought to have had this evening. I keep thinking there has been a mistake in the names.*

*I do not know what became of Haskett after the hospital. Allingham reached England. I hope there is someone with him.*

*The German boy would have been older now. I cannot remember whether his hair was fair or whether it was the light.*

*Father asked me to write the truth. This is what I know: I am alive, and I want to come home. I am ashamed that wanting it is so easy to write.*

He put the pencil away.

Across from him, a soldier slept with his mouth open and his hands still clasped between his knees. Thomas drew the man’s slipping blanket over his shoulder.

***

They did not go home next morning.

There were inspections, roads to clear, stores to count, orders which changed before anyone could obey them. Peace acquired its own fatigues. Men who had survived bombardments quarrelled over leave lists. Some drank. Some slept whenever they were permitted and woke angry at having lost another hour.

Thomas wrote to his father that he was safe. He read the sentence several times before sealing the envelope.

In December, a card reached him from Haskett, written in a large, unfamiliar hand.

*He says to tell you he is doing well,* it began. Below, squeezed into the remaining space in Haskett’s own cramped writing, was: *Woman here keeps taking the pencil off me. Mind your feet. Both of them.*

Thomas laughed once, sharply. The man beside him looked up.

“Good news?”

“Yes,” Thomas said. He had to clear his throat. “Yes.”

There was nothing from Allingham.

Before they left the sector, Thomas walked through the trench alone.

Rain had softened the firing step. A length of duckboard lifted under his boot and settled with a wet sigh. Here he had once stood unable to stop his teeth chattering; here Billy had made room for him, although there had been no room. He could no longer find the exact place.

He had wanted to visit the grave, but the burial ground lay miles behind their present position, beyond roads broken and rebuilt during the advance. He had its location written down. That would have to do for now.

At a traverse he found a spoon sunk upright in the mud. He almost picked it up, then left it. Someone might come looking, he thought, with the old absurd instinct to keep another man’s things safe.

At the end of the trench he climbed out by the steps.

Nothing fired at him.

He stood exposed beneath a low sky, waiting for his body to believe it.

***

His release came in the spring.

A clerk asked his name, age, trade. Thomas answered, hesitating over the last.

“Schoolmaster’s son isn’t a trade,” the clerk said, not unkindly.

“No.”

They gave him papers and money and a suit that pinched beneath the arms. Without his equipment he felt badly balanced, liable to pitch forward. He kept reaching towards the place where his rifle should have been.

On the crossing he stayed on deck despite the cold. The sea slapped white against the ship. Below, men sang; above them gulls hung in the wind, making little corrections with their wings.

He had posted his father the date, but the trains changed, and then changed again. At the last station he discovered he could walk the remaining miles faster than wait for the connection. He shouldered his small bag.

The road was drying after rain. Water shone in the cart ruts, and the hedges showed their first green. His chest ached on the hill. He stopped, bent slightly, and waited for the coughing to pass.

A bird rose from the rough grass beside the hedgerow.

He knew the sound before he looked up.

The skylark climbed, singing without pause, until it was no more than a flickering point above the field. Thomas stood with one hand against the gatepost. He heard again the bird above the shattered ground after the raid; saw the German boy’s mouth opening; felt Billy’s fingers tightening once around his own.

He tried to take a breath and could not.

The tears came without sobbing at first. Then he lowered his bag and covered his face. The song went on. There was nothing in it that knew about Billy, nothing that could be persuaded to stop for him.

Thomas wept until his sleeves were wet. When he looked up again, he could no longer see the bird, although he could still hear it.

He picked up his bag.

***

His father was at the school gate, fastening it with a loop of wire.

For a moment Thomas saw only how much older he had become. Then his father dropped the wire.

“Thomas?”

He crossed the road quickly, but stopped a pace away, looking at the narrow face, the unfamiliar suit.

“You got my letter?” Thomas asked.

“Yes. I thought tomorrow.”

“So did I.”

His father put out a hand, changed his mind, and embraced him. Thomas stood rigid for an instant. Then he let the bag fall and held on.

Over his father’s shoulder he could see the school windows. One pane had been replaced; the new glass caught the light differently.

“I’ve brought the notebook,” he said.

His father’s hand moved once across his back.

“Come inside.”

At the threshold Thomas paused. Behind him a cart rattled down the village street, and somewhere beyond the houses the skylark was still singing. He listened, then followed his father in.
