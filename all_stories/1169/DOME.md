By the time Will Mercer reached his section, he had learned to watch the boots of the man ahead. Looking farther along the communication trench only showed him more turns, more sagging revetment, more water trembling between the duckboards. Once, a board tilted beneath him and let his right foot sink past the ankle. He pulled it free with a sucking noise which seemed indecently loud. Above the trench, a strip of late-October sky was fading behind the broken branches of an orchard.

The corporal waiting at the dugout entrance checked his name against a damp paper.

“Mercer. Eighteen?”

“Yes, Corporal.”

“Jonah Price. You’re with this section. Shaw will show you where to put your things.”

Will straightened as much as his equipment allowed. He had expected questions about his training, perhaps a word about the work ahead. Price glanced instead at his boots.

“Get those socks changed when you can.”

Inside, the dugout smelled of wet wool, earth and candle smoke. A man sitting on a timber shelf drew his knees aside.

“Here. Mind the roof.”

Will struck his helmet against a beam anyway.

“Most do,” the man said. “Alfie Shaw.”

There was room for Will if he sat sideways, with his rifle between his knees. Water travelled down a support post and gathered beneath the shelf. Alfie shifted a haversack away from it, then produced a folded scrap of cloth from inside his tunic.

“Dry your feet with that. Between the toes, especially.”

“I’ve only the one spare pair of socks.”

“Keep them under your tunic till you need them. And loosen your laces when we’re resting. Tight boots won’t warm you.”

The cloth was scarcely larger than a handkerchief. Will hesitated before using it.

“Go on,” Alfie said. “It’s what it’s for.”

Will had imagined hardship as something a man endured by setting his jaw. He had not expected it to require so much attention to socks.

Before he could settle, Price sent them to collect food. Alfie and Will carried a lidded dixie between them on a pole, stepping together along the uneven boards. Steam escaped through a dent beside the lid. At each traverse they had to turn awkwardly, lifting one end, lowering the other. Once they waited while men carrying ammunition squeezed past.

“Thought it might be hot when we got it there,” Will said.

“It was hot when somebody put it in.”

Alfie did not sound aggrieved. He watched Will’s footing and warned him before each missing board.

After the food came the clearing. Mud had washed beneath a length of duckboard and lifted it against the trench wall. Price showed them where to begin.

“Two men lifting. Scrape underneath, then lay it flat. Don’t leave the tools across the passage.”

He spoke without haste, assigning each man a place. Will worked until his shoulders trembled and his hands felt swollen inside their wet gloves.

During a pause, he said, “I suppose we’re fortunate to have got here in time, Corporal. To help finish it.”

Price looked down the trench.

“Clear the sump as well. That water’s finding its way into the dugout.”

Will bent to the shovel again, his face hot. Bitterness, he thought. The older soldiers had been through too much to speak of honour now. Still, it seemed a pity that they should discourage those who had come willing to do their share.

Later, Price sent him with Alfie to carry water to the dressing station in a ruined farmhouse cellar. Only one wall remained above ground. A door laid across packing cases served as a table, and the candle beside it had been fixed upright in a lump of mud.

The stretcher-bearer there, Private Samuel Dyer, took a water tin from Will and tested its lid before setting it down.

“Put the other beside the steps. Thank you.”

A wounded British soldier sat with his back against the wall, watching Dyer bind his forearm. His sleeve had been cut away. He kept apologising for the trouble.

“Rest it there,” Dyer said. “No, leave the hand loose.”

On the next stretcher lay a young German with a dressing pressed against his scalp. A soldier with a rifle stood near the stairs. Will noticed the German’s open coat, the grey fabric darkened by rain, and one boot with its lace trailing.

Dyer finished the bandage, then knelt beside him.

“Hold this cup, Mercer.”

Will took it. The German lifted his head towards the water.

“Only a little at once,” Dyer said.

Will steadied the back of the man’s head while he drank. His hair was gritty against Will’s palm. When he coughed, Will drew the cup away too quickly and spilled water over his own wrist.

“That’s all right. Give him a moment.”

Dyer said it in the same voice he had used with the British soldier. He adjusted the dressing, checked that it held, and reached for another bandage.

That night Will discovered that exhaustion did not necessarily bring sleep. His hips ached against the timber shelf. Somewhere close by, a man snored, stopped, then began again with a choking rattle. A drip struck an empty tin at irregular intervals. Whenever Will thought it had ceased, it struck once more.

He lay with his kit gathered beside his head, as Alfie had advised. His boots were loosened, his damp socks tucked beneath his clothes. Beyond the dugout, guns sounded heavily and then were quiet. He listened for footsteps, though he did not know whose footsteps he expected.

Morning brought another carrying party. Will’s right shoulder had stiffened so badly that he needed Alfie’s help with an equipment strap. Neither mentioned it. They fetched bread, shifted fallen earth from the entrance and searched for a shovel somebody had left behind a traverse.

When Will finally opened his notebook, a muddy thumbprint marked the page.

He had bought it to preserve the important moments of his service. There would be advances, he had thought, difficult duties faithfully performed, things his father might read with pride. Even now he wanted a sentence worthy of his arrival at the front.

He wrote the date. Underneath it he noted the food journey and the work on the boards. Then he put down Alfie Shaw’s name, with a reminder about dry socks, and Samuel Dyer’s, so that he would not have to ask again.

The rest could wait until he was less tired.

Before closing the notebook, he added that Alfie had lent him a cloth. He ought to find something to give him in return.

By the third day Will could find the dressing station without following Alfie’s heels. Left at the broken pump, right where a length of railway sleeper held back the trench wall, then past the dugout with a groundsheet nailed across its entrance. Coming back, everything looked different. He learned to turn and look behind him at each junction.

Price sent him forward with a folded message tucked inside his tunic. The corporal made him repeat the destination before letting him go.

“You’ll pass a turning with a white arrow. Ignore it. That goes nowhere now.”

The arrow was clearer than any of the landmarks Will had been given. Beyond it, the trench ended in a mound of fresh earth. He understood why Price had insisted.

Farther on, a stretcher had become wedged at a bend. One bearer stood almost knee-deep beside the boards while the other tried to lift his end clear of a projecting timber. There was nowhere to pass. Will helped steady the handles, feeling the weight pull at his sore shoulder. Beneath the blanket, a boot moved once.

By the time he delivered the message, the officer who wanted it had gone to another post. Will waited beneath a piece of corrugated iron while someone fetched him. He had imagined messages travelling swiftly through a battle, each man knowing where the next would be. His own had acquired a damp corner against his chest.

The dinner journey took longer. A shell had brought down part of the communication trench, and the carrying party had to return to the last junction and approach by a narrower route. At each stop, the dixie knocked against Will’s leg. He could smell the stew escaping round its lid.

At the dressing station, Dyer asked whether they had water coming.

“Behind us,” Alfie said.

“How far?”

Alfie looked at Will, then back along the trench.

“Couldn’t say.”

Dyer nodded and went inside. Will wished they could have given him a better answer.

They delivered the stew barely warm. Afterwards Alfie scraped mud from his trousers with a broken piece of wood.

“When I get home, I’m having my dinner at a table,” he said. “Chair pulled right in. Plate that stays where you put it.”

Will smiled, then found himself imagining the chair: an ordinary wooden one, with all four legs on a dry floor.

Returning for the empty dixie, he paused beside a shell-hole beyond a low stretch of revetment. The guns had fallen quiet. Water filled the bottom, sheltered from the wind, and several stars shone in it. He leaned a little nearer. A drop fell from the torn bank and the stars lengthened, broke, then gathered themselves again.

“Mind that edge,” Alfie said behind him.

Will stepped back. Later he wrote, Stars showing in water in a shell-hole. He considered adding something about their beauty, but it seemed an odd word to put beside the rest.

That evening Price gathered the section near the dugout entrance. Rainwater darkened his tunic across the shoulders.

“Patrols have seen movement opposite. They reckon there’ll be an attempt against our forward posts tomorrow morning. Local business, from what we’ve been told. We’ll be in position before daylight.”

Nobody spoke.

“Clean your rifles. Check your ammunition. Mercer, you keep with Shaw.”

“Will there be an advance afterwards?” Will asked.

Price studied him briefly.

“If there is, you’ll be told. For now, make certain you can work your bolt.”

Will felt his face warm. He had heard weariness in the corporal’s voice before and called it bitterness to himself. Now he watched Price move along the men, checking that each understood where he was to go.

In the dugout Alfie helped him inspect his rifle, then stretched out with his cap over his eyes. Will checked the bolt again after he had finished. His fingers felt clumsy. He wanted to ask how much warning there would be, but Alfie might already be asleep.

He opened his notebook beneath the candle. There were names, reminders, journeys; nothing yet that resembled the account he had intended to keep. He wrote the date, 29 October 1918, and began.

Tomorrow we are to have our first real business with the enemy. I am glad that the waiting is nearly over, for a fellow cannot know what use he will be until he has been tried. I hope I shall acquit myself well and give Father no reason to be ashamed. This sounds rather grand when I read it back. What I mean is that I should like to do what is required without having to be told twice.

Corporal Price says it will probably be a small affair. He has seen enough to judge, though he does not promise anything. I have cleaned my rifle and tried the bolt several times. It works perfectly well. I tried it once more because some grit might have got in while we were looking at it. Everything becomes dirty here, however careful one is.

Alfie says to keep near him. He said it quite ordinarily, while putting his things away, as though we were going on another carrying party. I am fortunate to have somebody who knows the ways. I must remember to return his cloth. It is still inside my tunic, and perhaps I had better keep it until after tomorrow, since he said there was no hurry.

I saw stars in a shell-hole tonight. They were very distinct, although the water looked brown when there was light enough to see it. I cannot describe it properly. There are things here I should like to tell them at home which would sound foolish in a letter.

I said when I arrived that we were fortunate to be in time. I still think a man ought to do his share. It is only difficult to settle to sleep with so much to remember. I have put my kit where I can reach it. I shall write again tomorrow.

Morning came grey and wet. Will had barely slept when the first shell struck beyond the traverse. The duckboards jolted beneath him; earth pattered down his collar. He reached for his rifle and found his fingers fumbling at the sling.

“Stand to!” Price called. “Alfie, gun team. Will, pass the reserve magazines forward. Keep the communication bend clear.”

Another shell burst before Will could answer. He heard the last instruction without understanding how it belonged to the first. Clear the bend: the ammunition box must be moved. He dragged it from beside his assigned place and backed into the passage. A corner caught between two boards. When he pulled, the box swung across the trench.

A man carrying ammunition stopped against it and swore.

Will stared at him. His ears rang; the man’s mouth seemed to move without making words.

Alfie crouched in front of him. “Will. Look here.”

He looked.

“Stay low. Push the box against the wall. Tight in. Now open it.”

Will shoved until his shoulder hurt.

“Magazines. One at a time. Take them to the gun.”

The instructions gave him something he could finish. He lifted a magazine from its oily wrapping, crouched along the trench, and placed it beside the gunner. Then he went back for another.

Rifle fire cracked beyond the parapet. Through smoke Will glimpsed a figure near the wire, then another bending low. The gun answered. He ducked so sharply that the magazine struck his knee.

“Bring it along,” the gunner said.

He brought it. His hands shook on every journey, but the box emptied.

Price beckoned him down from the gun position. “Tell Sergeant Hale the timber at traverse three has shifted. Passage needs bracing. Keep away from the turning.”

“Traverse three,” Will repeated. “Timber shifted. Needs bracing.”

He took the longer route. Once he stopped, unable to remember whether Price had said three or four; then he heard the order again in his mind. He found Hale behind the support trench’s sandbagged junction, delivered the report, and waited until the sergeant repeated the number.

By his return the figures at the wire had disappeared.

“Only a few of them,” Price said. “Keep watching the wire.”

The rifle fire dwindled, and the shells became more widely spaced before stopping. In the pause Will heard a loose length of wire tapping a post, a small sound the guns had swallowed.

Price went from man to man, then set them to clearing the passage. Will pulled loose boards away while Alfie shifted earth. Beneath a fallen revetment they found Lomas, conscious, his trouser leg dark with blood.

“I can’t get up,” he said, trying again.

“Leave that to us,” Alfie told him.

They eased him onto a groundsheet stretched between two timbers. Will took the front with another man. Every stumble along the communication trench brought a groan from behind him. He began counting his steps, watching each place where his boot would land.

At Dyer’s dugout they lowered the stretcher onto the floor. Wet wool, paraffin and blood thickened the air. Dyer knelt beside Lomas, cut the cloth from his leg, and directed Will’s hand onto a dressing.

“Hold there. Steady pressure.”

Will watched the blood creep towards his thumb until Dyer replaced his hand with a bandage.

“Will I lose it?” Lomas asked.

Dyer tightened the dressing. “First we stop this bleeding.”

A shell landed nearby. Dust poured between the roof timbers. Will hunched over Lomas without thinking. When he straightened, Alfie had vanished.

Will found him behind stacked ration tins, crouched in a recess scarcely wide enough for his shoulders. His palms pressed flat against the earth. His breath came quickly, barely lifting his chest.

“Are you hurt?”

Alfie shook his head but did not look up.

Will almost reached for his sleeve, then let his own hand fall.

Will knelt beside him. From there he could see the entrance, a crooked opening full of grey light. He remembered how Alfie had caught his attention beside the ammunition box, then given him one thing to do.

“The entrance is clear,” he said. “You can see it from here. Dyer’s just over there.”

Alfie’s eyes followed his pointing hand.

“Stay sitting. There’s room for me.”

Will settled beside him, leaving the doorway in view. He could hear Dyer asking someone for water, then explaining how to support a wounded arm. He kept his voice low and ordinary whenever Alfie looked towards the roof.

After a while Alfie said, “It’s being buried. Alive.”

Will waited.

“I can manage up there. But under all this—” His fingers scraped the wall. “I can’t.”

“You needn’t move yet.”

“Don’t tell them.”

“I won’t.”

They sat until Alfie’s breathing deepened. He rubbed his face with both hands.

“You did all right with the magazines.”

“I blocked the passage.”

“And moved it.”

“After you told me.”

Alfie glanced at him. “Call it even, then.”

Will felt a small easing in his chest. He had been so occupied with needing Alfie that he had never considered Alfie might need him.

That evening he opened his notebook beneath the candle. He wrote the date, 30 October 1918, and paused over the clean space.

He recorded the box in the passage, Alfie’s instructions, and Price’s message about traverse three. He wrote Lomas’s name, Sergeant Hale’s, and Dyer’s request for water. He left out the words spoken in the recess.

At last he added: Alfie helped me when I could not think what to do. Afterwards I stayed beside him when he needed someone.

He had expected an account his father might read with pride. What lay on the page was smaller: a box moved, magazines carried, a message remembered. He read it twice and did not cross anything out.

Outside, rain gathered in the shell holes. A pale fragment of evening sky trembled on the brown water, looking for an instant almost like a star.

By the third morning, Will knew where to turn sideways with the ration tins. The communication trench narrowed beside a fallen revetment, and a man carrying anything broad had to lift one end before advancing the other. Alfie went ahead with the bread sack over his shoulder.

“Mind the nail,” he said.

“I know.”

“Then mind it knowingly.”

Will lifted the tins clear. Their handles cut into his fingers, but he brought them through without spilling anything. Ahead, Alfie waited while a runner squeezed past, his sleeve dark with mud.

Nothing was said about the recess in Dyer’s dugout. When they went below, Will did not watch Alfie or choose a seat for him. He kept the confidence as carefully as he carried a message: it belonged to someone else.

Their work gave them plenty to talk about. A loose duckboard tilted under anyone stepping on its end, tipping foul water over his boots. They spent half an hour trying to secure it with a bent spike and a hammer whose head worked loose.

“Your turn,” Alfie said.

“You’ve only struck it twice.”

“Two excellent blows. Follow those.”

They changed places whenever their backs began to ache. At last the board held. Alfie tested it with both feet and bowed, one hand against the trench wall.

That evening the food arrived short. A damaged container had lost part of its contents somewhere behind the support line. Alfie divided their bread against a tin lid, examined the pieces, and exchanged them.

“You’ve given me the larger one,” Will said.

“Yours has the hole. Makes it look smaller.”

Will broke a corner off and put it back in Alfie’s hand.

They ate with their knees nearly touching. Beyond the traverse someone was arguing about a missing sock.

“When this is finished,” Alfie said, “I’m going back to the docks. My uncle reckons he can speak for me.”

“Same work?”

“Same wages, if they’ll pay them. Then Nell and me will get married.”

He spoke without lowering his voice, as though arranging something for the following Sunday.

“She wants a kitchen with a proper window. Not a room where you put the kettle beside the bed. And a canary.”

“Why a canary?”

“Her aunt had one. Nell liked hearing it while she washed up.”

“Do you like them?”

“Never lived with one. We’ll have to see how we get on.”

Will pictured a yellow bird above a basin, a wet plate in Nell’s hands. He had never seen her, yet the kitchen came readily to him: daylight on a sill, Alfie home from work, his coat hung somewhere to dry.

Later he wrote: Alfie’s Nell wants a canary in the kitchen. He added the remark about getting on with it. There was no reason his father would find it important. He kept it nevertheless.

Over the following days, Price began sending him without first calling Alfie over.

“Support post. Ask whether the carrying party’s left. Bring back numbers, not ‘some men.’”

Will repeated the destination and went. He ignored the turning marked with the white arrow without having to remind himself. He returned with the number of men, their time of departure, and the information that one had gone back for a shovel.

Price nodded. “Good.”

Will carried that single word with him until a shell landed beyond the next traverse. The wall struck his shoulder as he dropped. Earth pattered on his neck. For several moments he could remember neither his errand nor which way he had been facing.

Then he checked the paper in his pocket and continued. His hands shook throughout the delivery.

At dawn, rain hung from the wire in close-set drops. Will noticed it while waiting for orders: each barb carried a small brightness. Then Price stepped beside him with a message to repeat.

Reports disagreed about the route to a damaged drainage section. One man said the rear communication trench was passable; another had seen fresh shelling there but could not say where.

“Take the rear route,” Price told the work party. “Keep intervals. If the crossing’s gone, come back. Don’t climb over.”

They returned before noon. Fresh earth had buried the crossing, and water was rising behind it. The drainage work remained undone.

Price listened, asking twice where the damage began.

“Right. Leave the tools here. We’ll need another approach.”

“Thought that way was clear,” one of the men muttered.

“So did I,” Price said. He unfolded the damp sketch again.

Will held a candle while Price marked the obstruction. His pencil left a pale groove in the paper.

“Were you always doing this?” Will asked.

“What, getting conflicting reports?”

“Making the arrangements.”

“Municipal offices. Rates and correspondence.” Price rubbed the pencil point against his thumbnail. “Usually a roof over it.”

“You’ll be glad to get back.”

Price did not answer immediately.

“There was generally time to send a letter twice,” he said. Then he looked towards the entrance. “Find Hale. Tell him the party’s back and the work’s outstanding.”

At Dyer’s station that afternoon, Will helped remove a wounded man’s boot. Dyer supported the ankle while Will eased the leather away. The man apologized for the smell.

“No apology needed,” Dyer said.

Afterwards, washing the basin, Will asked whether any of it could be worth what happened to them.

Dyer examined a clean dressing before answering.

“I can’t tell him his leg was necessary,” he said. “I can try to keep it.”

A call came from the entrance. Dyer set the dressing down and went to meet the next stretcher.

That night, a patch of sky showed in shell-hole water. Will remembered looking at the stars on his first night and wanting them to tell him something. Now he watched until a footstep disturbed the edge and the light broke apart.

Under the candle he wrote down the blocked crossing, the unfinished work, and Price’s old employment. He recorded the wounded man’s apology, though he had not learned his name.

Below that he wrote: Ask Dyer.

He meant the name. Reading it back, he saw that it might mean something else, but left it alone. There was still space beneath it, and on the next page.

The rumor had reached the trench before breakfast: peace talks, an armistice perhaps, though no one knew who had heard it first or how much of it was true. The orders board remained unchanged. Men still filled sandbags, cleared drains, and carried food where it was wanted.

Will was shifting a stack of pickets away from a leaking traverse when Alfie came along with two ration tins hooked over one arm.

“Cook says the other lot won’t come back for theirs,” Alfie said. “So I’m to spare them the trouble.”

The track to the ruined village ran outside the trench for a stretch, exposed between a broken wall and the blackened houses. It was an ordinary errand, one that had passed from man to man until Alfie was sent. Will was on a separate task, still holding a picket under his arm.

“Could wait till the shelling stops,” he said.

“Could be waiting till supper.” Alfie glanced at the low sky and went on.

A shell burst beyond the village. Dust rose behind the roofless houses. They both looked towards it; then another landed closer, and a third came in with a thin whistle.

Alfie lowered his head and continued along the track. Will watched him go, the tins knocking against his leg. He thought of calling him back, but had no reason beyond the sound of the guns. The noise often moved away again. The work remained.

The next shell burst near the path.

The blast struck with a tearing force. Will saw Alfie flung sideways, his hand opening as he fell. The tins bounced apart and rolled over the stones, one towards the rubble. Alfie did not get up.

Will dropped the picket and started forward. More shells came down. Grit sprang from the wall, fragments struck stone, and he threw himself behind the traverse. Something cracked against the timber above him. He pressed his face into the dirt, one arm over his neck. When he raised his head, Alfie was still on the path.

Will pushed to his feet. Price caught the back of his tunic and dragged him down.

“He’s hit,” Will said.

“I can see that. Stay here.”

“I can reach him.”

“Not while they’re ranging the path.” Price held him fast. “You go out now, you’ll be hit too.”

Through a gap in the broken wall, Will could see the track. Each flash made the village briefly clear, then smoke and dust closed over it. He could not tell if Alfie was breathing. He could not see enough to know where he had been struck.

Once, when a flare had lit the trench and Will had stood to watch it, Alfie had shoved him down with a laugh. “Stay low, Mercer, unless you want to be counted.” The remembered words made Will strain harder against Price’s grip.

He obeyed because the shells kept falling and because Price would not release him. Fury tightened his jaw; shame followed close behind it. He could do nothing but watch the gap and wait for the explosions to move elsewhere. He asked twice if they could go. Price kept his eyes on the track, measuring the pauses.

At last the shells shifted east. The next silence held long enough to matter.

“Now,” Price said. “Dyer. Mercer. Along the wall.”

Dyer came from the dressing station with a field dressing in his hand. They crossed low, stepping around bricks and splintered timber. One tin lay beside the path, the other had stopped against a broken curb. Will saw a dark mark on the stones and kept his gaze beyond it until he reached Alfie.

Dyer knelt and checked his throat, then looked at Will and shook his head. The small movement left no room for hope. Alfie’s face was dusty, one eye half open. The wound Will could see seemed too small to account for the stillness. He stared at it, waiting for some clearer sign, while Dyer rested a hand briefly on Alfie’s shoulder.

Then they lifted him. Will took his legs, Dyer his shoulders. The body sagged between them, heavier than Will expected. Behind them, the ration tins remained scattered along the track.

They lowered Alfie inside the trench. Will let go too quickly, and Dyer steadied the body. He did not scold him. Price came over once the men nearby were under cover.

“You kept me there,” Will said.

“The shells were landing across the path.”

“You might have let me try.”

“And watched them take you too?” Price looked towards the village. His face was grey with dust. “There wasn’t a safe way to get him while the bombardment was on.”

Will wanted another answer, one that did not end with the exposed track and a man left on it. Price had none. His hands trembled, barely, and Will hated him for the order anyway. Then he hated himself for obeying it, though he knew he would have been no use dead beside Alfie.

A few days earlier, Alfie had talked about going back to the docks. He meant to ask the foreman about the early shift, he said, and save enough for a wedding with Nell before winter. They might manage it sooner if they stopped wasting their pay at the canteen. He spoke as though he were arranging the following Sunday: work, a visit to Nell, a walk if the weather held.

There had been nothing grand in it. Alfie expected to go home and take up the ordinary plans he had made there. Will could hear his casual certainty more clearly than any words said over the body.

In the dugout, his notebook lay beneath his spare shirt. On the open page he could see Dyer’s name, with Ask Dyer beneath it. He did not take the book out. He sat with his hands clasped while the guns sounded farther off, and left the notebook shut.

The calling began in a pause between shells. Will heard it from the dugout steps: a thin sound, broken by silences long enough to make him think it had stopped. Then it came again.

Dyer stood at the parapet, listening. Beyond the trench, smoke drifted through the roofless houses. There was a crater beside a fallen wall, with a strip of pale earth showing at its lip.

“A German,” Dyer said.

Will had recognised one of the words. He stayed on the step.

Dyer went to Price. “He’s in that hole beyond the wall. We could get a stretcher to him.”

A shell struck somewhere behind the church. Dust rose through the bare rafters of a house.

“Not yet,” Price said.

Dyer waited. He did not call across the open ground or climb onto the fire-step again. Will watched Price studying the village and felt the same useless anger he had felt when Alfie lay on the path. Now it was a stranger waiting, and Will resented being made to hear him.

Another shell fell farther off. Then there was a stretch of quiet in which water ticked from the damaged revetment.

“Go,” Price said. “Three of you. Keep along the wall. If it starts landing close, get down and stay down.”

Dyer took his dressing bag. Another man fetched a stretcher from a recess by the steps.

“Will. Can you take the other end?”

He wanted to say no. He had carried Alfie’s legs only a little while before; he could still feel the weight against his palms. He looked at the empty stretcher, then took the handle.

“Yes.”

They followed the trench to its shallow end and went out bent low. Splintered brick crunched beneath their boots. At the wall they stopped; Dyer listened, then led them round its broken end.

The German lay halfway down the crater, one leg crooked in the mud. Blood had soaked his trousers and made a black patch beneath his thigh. His rifle lay beside his hand. Dyer pushed it away before kneeling.

Will lowered his end of the stretcher. He looked at the field-grey sleeve, the buttons, the boot that had churned a small hollow in the earth. He did not want to touch any of it.

“Help me straighten his leg,” Dyer said.

Will climbed down.

Dyer cut the sodden cloth away. The man cried out and caught at Will’s wrist. Will shook him off, then took hold above the knee as Dyer showed him.

“Easy. There. Keep him still.”

The third man steadied the German’s shoulders while Dyer opened a field dressing. Blood welled from a wound in the outer thigh.

“Your hand on this, Will. Firmly.”

He put his palm over the pad. It grew warm at once. The German made a sound through his teeth and tried to turn his face towards him.

Will looked at Dyer instead.

“Keep pressing. Just like that.”

He obeyed. His arm began to ache. He was angry at the voice, at the grey cloth under his other hand, at the time they were spending here. When the pad shifted, he corrected it without being told.

Dyer secured the dressing. Together they lifted the man onto the canvas, supporting the wounded leg. He was heavier than he looked. Will hated discovering that weight again.

They had to bring the stretcher up the crater slope sideways. Loose earth ran beneath Will’s boots; the bearer opposite him swore softly and found firmer footing. Dyer kept one hand on the wounded leg.

At the wall, a shell passed overhead. They crouched with the stretcher between them until it burst beyond the village. The German’s breathing rasped close to Will’s knee. He wanted the sound farther away. When they lifted again, he adjusted his grip so the canvas stayed level.

Price waved them into the trench without speaking.

The dressing station smelled of iodine, damp wool and the sourness of crowded men. Under the low roof, an orderly made room on a trestle bed. Dyer checked the bandage, which had begun to darken at one edge, and set another pad over it.

Will stood with his hands hanging. He had expected someone to tell him what to do next. Dyer was speaking to the orderly about the bleeding and how far they had carried the man.

“You can leave him with us,” he told Will.

Will looked once at the grey coat on the floor and went out.

Outside, the trench had resumed its small noises: a mess tin scraping against wood, a man coughing, someone asking whether the rations had been brought in. Will went past before he heard the answer.

He wiped his hands on his trousers, but the dried blood at his nails remained. Inside the dugout, he sat where he had been sitting before the calls began.

Alfie ought to have been there to complain about the food. Or to talk again about the early shift at the docks, calculating what he and Nell could save. Will tried to remember the exact way he had said it. For a moment, all he could hear was the German crying out when Dyer cut his trousers.

That made him angrier.

He pressed his hands between his knees. The man might live now; Dyer had not said. Will hoped he would, though he did not want to go back and see him. He wanted Alfie, sitting close enough to knock elbows with, talking about something that would happen next week.

The notebook lay beneath his spare shirt. For a while he watched the place where its corner pressed against the cloth. Above him, boots passed the entrance and went on along the trench.

He made no move to uncover it.

Will was still in the dugout when the orderly came along the trench. He heard the boots stop outside, then Price asking where the man had come from. The reply was too low to catch. Above the entrance, a loose end of wire tapped against a post. Will watched his hands until Price called them out.

The morning was pale and cold. Rainwater stood in the footprints along the duckboards, and beyond the parapet a gun fired, waited, fired again. The orderly carried a folded sheet inside his tunic. He took it out with care.

“From battalion, through company headquarters.”

Price read it, shielding the paper from the wind.

“Who’s it addressed to?”

“Forward platoon, village approaches. All section commanders.”

“Say that again.”

The orderly repeated it. Price checked the name at the bottom, then gathered the men who could leave their posts.

“Hostilities will cease at eleven hundred hours today, November the eleventh,” he read. “Troops will stand fast on the line reached at that hour. Defensive precautions will be maintained.”

“They told us that yesterday,” a man said.

Will remembered other hours, other days when somebody had sworn the end was settled. A shell burst beyond the village, throwing a dark spurt above the broken roofs. He watched the earth fall back.

“This is the order we’ve received,” Price said. “Eleven by my watch. Until then we hold this position. Afterwards we hold it. Nobody goes out towards the village without orders. Make sure the men at the posts understand.”

The orderly showed him his watch. Price adjusted his own, then checked it again.

There was still more than an hour to wait.

Will went back for his rifle. He wiped a patch of moisture from the barrel and found himself examining the rag afterwards, as though it might tell him whether the message was true. Outside, somebody asked if the rations would come up before eleven. Nobody knew. A man who had been scraping mud from the firestep resumed his work, pushing each spadeful into the same shallow heap.

The guns continued. Will listened for a change in them and could not hear one.

As the hour approached, Price went along the trench, speaking at each post. He stopped near Will with his watch in his palm. A shell passed overhead; its burst came from somewhere behind them, followed by the thin fall of scattered debris.

Price looked down, then raised his head.

Will waited.

He expected another report from the battery to their left. When it did not come, he listened for the sound overhead instead. Water dripped beneath a duckboard. Someone coughed. The loose wire struck its post twice, paused, struck again.

He drew a breath and held it without meaning to.

Still no shell came. The sounds nearby remained small and separate. He could hear a man working his tongue against a sore tooth.

A laugh broke out farther along the bay. The man stopped, looked about him, and laughed again, louder. Near the dugout another man had put his forearm across his eyes. His shoulders were moving. Two others consulted a watch between them. The man with the spade went on scraping until the blade caught a stone; then he stood looking at it.

Price put his watch away.

“How many at the far bend?”

“Two.”

“Both at their posts?”

“Yes.”

He wrote the number down and went from man to man, getting an answer before making each mark.

“Mercer?”

“Here.”

Will’s voice sounded unusually loud.

The muscles at the back of his neck began to loosen. He wanted to sit down. He wanted hot tea and enough time to drink it without having to set the mug aside. Then he wanted Alfie beside him, complaining that they had announced a ceasefire without improving the breakfast.

He looked towards the dugout before he could stop himself.

Alfie had not lived to hear this. Will stood with the relief still in him, unable to separate it from the hurt.

The afternoon brought things that had not been finished: water to carry, ammunition boxes to shift from a flooded recess, a damaged length of duckboard to replace. Price kept the posts manned. No one had orders to pack.

Dyer was still working beneath the low roof of the dressing station. When Will brought water, he found him easing a stiffened pad away from a wound. The German lay with his eyes closed, his grey coat folded under the trestle. Will watched until he saw the blanket rise.

“Put it by my elbow,” Dyer said. “Not there. I’ll knock it over.”

Will moved the bucket.

“Warm water next, if you can get it.”

“All right.”

Dyer bent over the bandage again. Will took an empty kettle from beside the entrance and went to find somewhere to fill it.

Later, from the firestep, he looked towards the village. The broken walls were unchanged. A doorway showed black beneath a lintel that seemed scarcely supported. Something pale moved in an upper window, and Will lowered his head before he recognised a strip of curtain lifting in the wind. He waited, then looked again. No one came into the street.

Towards evening he returned to the dugout and uncovered the notebook.

He turned past Dyer’s name to a clean page. For a while the pencil rested against the paper without moving. Then he wrote Alfie’s name, and beneath it Nell’s, and a few words about the early shift at the docks.

He could not remember how much Alfie had reckoned they could save. He left a space rather than guess.

Will folded down the corner and closed the book.

Outside, someone called for a hand with the rations. He put the notebook beneath his spare shirt and went out.

On the morning of November 11, the company orderly came along the trench asking for Price. He steadied himself on Will’s shoulder to step over a broken length of duckboard. When Price came up, the orderly took a folded sheet from inside his tunic.

“It’s come down from battalion,” he said. “Captain says to hold where you are till eleven. Hostilities cease at eleven. Troops will stand fast on the line reached at that hour.”

A gun fired beyond the village while Price was opening the paper. He asked the orderly to repeat the message, and the orderly said it all again. Then he held up his watch for Price to see.

“Set it by the captain’s this morning.”

Price took out his own and moved the minute hand forward a little.

“A fellow told us the same thing yesterday,” said the man beside Will. He was mending the strap of his haversack with a piece of wire. “Said it was all arranged.”

“Who told him?” Will asked.

The man shrugged and went back to the strap.

After the orderly had gone, Will kept expecting him to come back with a correction. He stayed at his post. A thin root stuck out of the wall at the height of his knee, and the water that ran down it fell on the instep of his boot. He shifted his foot and put it back where it had been. A shell burst near enough for him to feel the shock through the shoulder he had braced against the earth.

“Eleven,” Price said.

Will listened for the next shell. There had been gaps all morning, some long enough for a man to start on a job. Beside his boot the buckle tapped against a board. He could hear water running underneath it. Then a man in the next bay asked for a match. He asked quietly, and Will heard someone farther off tell him to try the left pocket. Will found himself waiting for the scrape of the match. By the time it came, his shoulder was clear of the wall.

Someone laughed, stopped, and laughed again. At the bend, a man put his face into his sleeve and cried. Two others held their watches side by side. The man beside Will bent the loose end of the wire under the buckle and tugged the strap to see if it would hold.

Will caught himself smiling. He wanted to say Alfie’s name as softly as the man had asked for a match and see him turn round. Alfie had died with the guns still going. Will stood drawing one easy breath after another until Price called to him.

“Ceasefire. Same posts. Will, get me the number at the far end.”

Will came back with two names. Price wrote them on a scrap of card and asked for the names of the stretcher bearers as well. He wanted to know which of them had stayed with Dyer.

In the afternoon they shifted the ammunition boxes out of a flooded recess. Will lifted them one by one, resting each on his knee while the man above him got a grip. Then they laid a sound length of duckboard across the gap at the bend. It rocked under Will’s boot and he knelt to wedge a piece of brick beneath one end.

Dyer was still working under the low roof of the dressing station when Will brought water. The German lay with his eyes closed, his grey coat folded under the trestle. Dyer was easing a stiffened pad away from the wound. Will watched until the blanket rose at the man’s shoulder.

“Has he stirred since you came in?” Dyer asked.

“I saw him breathe.”

Will wished he had something better to tell him. Dyer waited with the pad in his hand until the blanket moved again. Then he nodded towards the bucket.

“Put it by my elbow. Warm water next, if you can get it.”

Will moved the bucket. He took the kettle from beside the entrance and waited his turn at the brazier behind a man warming a mess tin. By the time he brought it back, he needed a scrap of sacking round the handle.

Later, from the firestep, he looked towards the village. A doorway showed black beneath a cracked lintel. Something pale stirred in an upper window and Will lowered his head so quickly that the rim of his helmet scraped a sandbag. He waited, then looked again. A strip of curtain lifted out over the sill, its hem dark with rain. The street was empty as far as the bend. He stayed up long enough to see the curtain settle and lift again.

Towards evening he uncovered the notebook in the dugout. He turned past Dyer’s name to a clean page. For a while he held the pencil against the paper, bearing down just hard enough to make a dot. Then he wrote Alfie’s name, and below it Nell’s, and a few words about the early shift at the docks.

Alfie had counted up what they might save on his fingers. He’d remembered the coal halfway through and opened his whole hand to start again. Will could picture the split in Alfie’s thumbnail as he pressed each finger down. He waited for the sum to come back to him, then left a space for it. He folded down the corner of the page and closed the book.

Outside, someone called for a hand with the rations. Will put the notebook beneath his spare shirt and went out to take the other end of the box.

The orders reached them soon after daylight on 12 November. The runner stopped beneath the sagging entrance sheet and read from a damp slip: the men remaining in the forward trench were to withdraw to a reserve position. They would go when the carrying party had cleared the communication trench. Until then they were to pack and stand ready. Rain tapped the sheet above his head. Somewhere behind him a cart wheel creaked, a small, distinct sound Will would not have heard two mornings before.

Price took out his list. He flattened it against a packing case, shielding the paper with his shoulder.

“Mercer?”

“Here.”

Price made his mark. Will had straightened at the sound of his name, ready for a rebuke, though there was nothing yet to be rebuked for. Price glanced at his unfastened kit and moved on to the next man.

At the dressing station, Dyer was wrapping the unused dressings in oilcloth. The wounded man beneath the blanket had been carried away before dawn. His place remained visible: a hollow in the sacking, a dark stain beside it. Will looked there before he could prevent himself.

“Did they get him down?”

“They took him to the next post,” Dyer said. “That’s all I know.”

He pressed the air from the oilcloth parcel and tied it with string. His fingers were swollen round the nails.

Will went back to his corner. He shook the loose dirt from his spare shirt, folded it around his socks and pushed both into the kit. The shirt still held the shape of the notebook. He set the book on his knee while he tightened the straps around his blanket. One buckle would not catch. He worked at it until the cold metal hurt his thumb, then found that he had twisted the strap underneath.

A duckboard tipped outside. At its hollow knock he bent forward, his shoulders rising towards his ears. A man swore at the board and stamped it flat.

Dyer appeared with a mug.

“Yours packed?”

Will nodded.

“Have this. I’ve had some.”

Will took it between both hands. A skin had begun to form on the tea, but beneath it there was still warmth. Dyer stood for a moment, looking along the trench, then returned to fetch the dressing case. He had not waited for thanks. Will drank carefully, keeping the chipped place away from his lip.

There was time before they moved. He opened the notebook near the front.

The first entry was still there, the pencil darker where he had pressed too hard. He read the passage about the stars in the shell-hole water. The words did not show them as he remembered them. Farther down were things he would not have written this morning. He rested his thumb beside the lines without touching the pencil to them.

He turned past Dyer’s name to the folded corner. Alfie and Nell; the early shift; the space left for the sum Alfie had worked out. He left that space empty too. On the next clean page he wrote the date.

“12 November. We are to leave this trench this morning. Price has the list and I have answered my name. I am putting this down before I pack the book. There is mud in the stitching, although I have tried to keep it wrapped.

“On my first night I saw the stars in the shell holes. They moved when the water moved. I tried to write them down and could not get it right. I thought then that being frightened was something I would get past. I am frightened still. A board shifted under someone’s foot this morning and I bent down before I knew what it was.

“Alfie wanted the early shift at the docks. We talked about looking for work there together. He counted what he could save and had to begin again because he had left out the coal. I cannot remember the sum. I remember the split in his thumbnail. He would have been good company on the walk to work. There ought to have been all those mornings for him.

“I wrote about Nell wanting a canary in the kitchen. I can picture the cage better than I can picture peace. The firing has stopped here, but I do not know what I will do in a place where I need not listen for it. I want to go home. I am afraid of going there without Alfie.

“Dyer gave me his mug of tea while mine was packed. He said he had had some already. It was warm enough to hold between both hands. I have left the first entry as it was. I do not know any better way to write that night now.”

Will lifted the pencil. Water dripped from the roof into a tin near his boot. He moved the notebook farther onto his knee and waited until the last damp-looking stroke had lost its shine.

Outside, Price told someone to put back a length of telephone wire.

“Leave that. It’s staying.”

Will closed the book. The first entry and the new one lay together, with the names and unfinished notes between them. He wrapped it in the scrap of dry cloth he had kept for it and slipped it inside his tunic, where he could reach it without undoing his kit.

He returned the mug. Dyer took it by the handle and tucked it into his haversack.

“Ready?”

“Nearly.”

Will went back for his load. He got one arm through, then the other, and leaned forward until the straps settled on his shoulders. The buckle held this time. Price was at the turning with the list folded in his hand. Will joined the men along the wall and waited for the word to move.

Will went with the others when Price gave the word.

The communication trench narrowed where a fresh fall of earth had pushed its side inward. Men turned their shoulders to pass, packs scraping the boards, and one at a time climbed the shallow steps cut into the wall. Will’s boots found each tread carefully. The load pulled at his neck, though Price had checked the straps before they started. Under his tunic the notebook rested against his chest, wrapped in its cloth. He could feel its corners when he breathed deeply.

At the top, the morning opened around them.

It was cold enough to make the inside of Will’s nose sting. Rain lay on the ground in a thin skin, caught in wheel ruts and boot prints, and darkened the torn grass. Along the wire, drops held to the barbs and strands. The light was weak, the colour of a tin cup left outside, but it gathered in the water and made a bright line wherever the wire crossed the grey field.

Will looked at it, then at the ground before his feet.

The sight did not answer anything. It did not make the dead less dead or turn the years behind them into something that had been necessary. For a moment he thought of Alfie’s split thumbnail, and of the sums he used to work out for the docks, always starting over when he found what he had forgotten. That morning Will could not see the docks or imagine the walk there. He could only see the wet wire, and the small points of light caught along it.

He let himself look.

The section moved in a loose file toward the rear, following a track between low banks of earth. No one marched in time. They were tired enough to save their strength for keeping upright, and the silence among them had changed since the firing stopped. It was not a peaceful silence. Men still listened when a board creaked or a distant door slammed. Will found himself listening too, even when he knew what made the sound.

Price went ahead, list folded in his hand. He stopped at each turn to count them, touching a finger to the names. Dyer walked near the back, checking that no one had left a bandage or a medical roll behind. He carried his satchel high under one arm to keep it clear of the mud. When Will caught his eye, Dyer gave him a brief nod. Will nodded back. There was no need to say what they had already said.

The path dipped. The man in front of Will slowed, then stumbled as his boot slid against the edge. Will checked his own step and made room. Behind him came a young soldier whose face looked pinched with cold. He had been carrying his pack badly for some distance; one shoulder was lower than the other, and his breath came in short pulls through his mouth.

“Steady,” Will said.

The soldier’s hand found Will’s sleeve. Will held his arm until the man had both feet under him. The pack shifted, and a strap slipped free, dragging its buckle through the mud.

“Your strap.”

The soldier bent for it, but his knees wavered. Will crouched first and lifted the strap clear of the ground. Mud clung to its end. He wiped it once against his trouser leg, fed it back through the buckle and pulled it snug.

“Try it now.”

The young man straightened. He stood still for a second, his eyes on the fastening, as if he had expected it to come apart again.

“Thank you.”

Will gave a small shrug. “It was hanging.”

The words sounded plain enough. He was glad they did. He had no proper thing to say about fear, or the night before, or what waited beyond the next bend. He knew the soldier’s tiredness without knowing his name. That was enough for the few seconds they had stopped.

Price called from farther up the track. The file began to move again, and Will stepped aside to let the young man go ahead. His own shoulders ached beneath the straps. A buckle tapped his hip with every step. He adjusted the load once, careful not to crush the notebook, then followed the line of men toward the rear.

The trench fell behind them by degrees: first the broken parapet, then the low entrance to the communication trench, then the earthworks obscured by the bank. Will did not turn to make a final picture of it. There was no single view that could contain the days spent there, and he did not want to pretend otherwise. He had carried too many things by naming them once and believing the name had settled them.

The notebook was still under his tunic. Inside it were the two entries, the first left as it had been written, the second set beside it without correcting the first. The paper held what it held. Alfie was there, though not as a complete account, and neither was Will. He did not need to settle what the pages meant before he could keep walking.

Rain gathered along the wire beside the track. As the men moved on, the drops trembled when the wind passed, and the thin morning light broke and joined again along each strand. Will kept his eyes on the ground to avoid a rut, then raised them toward the wire. Behind him, the young soldier’s steps had steadied. Ahead, Price was counting again.

Will went on with the section, carrying the book and Alfie’s name, and kept noticing the light on the wet wire.
