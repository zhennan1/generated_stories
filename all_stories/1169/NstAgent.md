## Chapter 1: The Recruit

The first frost silvered the schoolhouse windows on the morning Thomas Merrick decided to go to war.

He was seventeen, though he had learned to stand with his shoulders arranged so that strangers guessed eighteen. His father taught standards three through six in the village school, and Thomas had spent the last year helping him after lessons—marking sums, sweeping chalk dust from the floor, and reading aloud from the histories in which young men rode beneath banners toward certain glory.

The war had made those histories seem newly alive. In the evenings Thomas sat by the range with a book open on his knees while his father mended his cuffs. He read poems about England, sacrifice, dawn, and fields made holy by blood. The words entered him like music. Beyond the window, the village lay quiet under its autumn mist: the smithy dark, the church tower pale, the hedges black with rain. Nothing happened there. Thomas was hungry for something that would give shape to his life.

His father looked up from the needle. “You’ve read that page three times.”

“I was thinking.”

“That is what worries me.”

Thomas shut the book. “I’m going to enlist.”

The needle paused in the cloth. For a moment only the clock spoke, counting out the silence.

“Your mother’s brother said the same thing,” his father said at last. “He came home from South Africa with a cough and no taste for parades.”

“This is different.”

“Every war is different until it begins.”

Thomas had expected anger, and the absence of it left him defenseless. His father’s face, usually mild and distracted, seemed suddenly older than the schoolhouse. There were white threads in his brown hair that Thomas had never noticed.

“I can’t stay here while everyone else goes,” Thomas said.

“Everyone else?”

“Men from the next village. The butcher’s sons. Mr. Bell’s eldest.”

“Other people’s foolishness is not an argument for yours.”

Yet three days later his father walked with him to the recruiting office in the market town. He did not speak on the road. Fallen leaves scraped along the lane, and Thomas carried the small leather valise his father had packed, though it held nothing but two shirts, a Bible, and a photograph of his mother taken before illness took her.

The recruiting sergeant measured Thomas, asked his age, and looked twice at his birth certificate. Thomas stood taller. When the sergeant said, “You understand you may be sent anywhere?” he answered yes with a voice that sounded to him like another person’s.

Outside, his father gave him a paper parcel. “Bread and cheese.”

“I won’t need it.”

“You will.”

They stood beneath the office’s painted sign while carts rattled through the square. Thomas wanted his father to say he was proud. Instead, the older man reached up and straightened the collar of Thomas’s coat.

“Write,” he said. “Even if there is nothing to write.”

Thomas promised.

The weeks that followed were made of farewells. The village women pressed cakes into his hands. Children asked whether he had killed anyone yet. At the station, his father shook him by the hand, then, embarrassed by the formality, embraced him hard enough to make the buttons of his coat bite.

“Remember,” his father whispered, “a uniform does not make a man.”

Thomas watched him diminish beyond the steam and waving hats. He wanted to call back that he knew. He did not yet know anything.

Training camp stood in a flat, windy stretch of country where the huts smelled of tar, wet wool, and boiled cabbage. The first morning a corporal shouted until Thomas forgot which foot was left. They crawled through mud, fired at paper men, polished boots until their fingers cracked, and learned that a rifle had a weight and temperament of its own. At night, boys who had been shop clerks or farmhands groaned in their sleep.

Thomas was assigned a bunk beside a wiry Welshman with coal-black hair and a grin that appeared even when he was exhausted.

“Billy Preece,” he said. “From Merthyr. You?”

“Thomas Merrick.”

“Schoolboy?”

“My father’s a schoolmaster.”

“Then you can write my letters.”

“I can.”

“Good. I can’t write much beyond my name, and even that looks like a spider drowning.”

Billy had worked underground since fourteen. He spoke of the mine as though it were a troublesome animal: dangerous, familiar, and sometimes generous. He taught Thomas how to fold a blanket tightly enough to pass inspection and how to eat cabbage without tasting it. Thomas read poems aloud by candle-end; Billy listened with his head tilted, then asked whether the poets had ever slept in a barrack hut.

“Perhaps they had officers who allowed better blankets,” Thomas said.

“They had better sense, more likely.”

Still, Billy shared his contraband tobacco. In return Thomas wrote a letter to Billy’s sister, describing the camp as “brisk and healthy,” while Billy dictated, “Tell Mam I’m not frightened,” and then crossed it out.

Autumn deepened. Trains moved constantly beyond the camp, their windows glowing at dusk. Rumors passed from hut to hut: a new offensive, a desperate need for men, France by Christmas. Thomas polished his buttons until they shone like coins. He imagined a ridge, a charge, a sunrise breaking through smoke while he and Billy advanced beneath the colors.

On the evening before embarkation, he opened the little diary his father had given him. The pages were white and waiting. Thomas held his pencil over the first line, listening to the barracks breathe around him.

What could he write? That his hands trembled? That he was afraid of being afraid? That somewhere beyond the sea a great poem was beginning, and he had been invited into it?

He closed the diary without making a mark.

Before dawn they marched to the station. Billy stumbled beside him, yawning, and muttered that France had better provide decent beer. The train carried them east through a gray morning. At the port, gulls wheeled over the crowded decks, crying like creatures already warning them away.

Thomas stood at the rail as England receded into rain. He felt the diary in his breast pocket, blank as untouched snow, and his heart beat with nervous excitement.

Ahead lay France, the trenches, and the chance—so the poems had promised—to become someone worth remembering.

## Chapter 2: The Maze

By the time Thomas reached the war, he had ceased to look for it out of railway windows.

France had passed in pieces: a quay slippery with rain, women selling oranges through the bars of a gate, bare orchards beside the railway. Then came a lorry ride and a march with all his equipment dragging at his shoulders. The guns were a disturbance beneath the sound of their boots. He had mistaken them at first for wagons crossing a bridge.

Now each concussion moved inside his chest.

They halted beside a broken wall at dusk. Ahead, a man disappeared down steps cut into the earth. The next man followed, and the next.

“Palace entrance,” Billy said.

Thomas tried to smile. His lips were cold.

Below the steps lay a passage scarcely wide enough for his shoulders and pack. Water shone between strips of wood underfoot. The walls rose above him in bulging sacks and slick clay, and the sky became a crooked ribbon. He followed the pack ahead round one corner, then another, losing all sense of the road behind.

Something brushed his ankle. A rat ran along the duckboards, its pale tail trailing through the water. It did not hurry until a boot struck beside it.

The smell came in layers: wet sacking, latrines, old smoke, and beneath them a sweetness that caught at the back of his throat. He knew it without being told, and wished he did not.

A man coming the other way flattened himself against the wall. His face was so coated with dirt that his eyes seemed naked.

“Fresh lot?”

Nobody answered.

“Mind the third board.”

Thomas stepped where he thought the man meant. The board tipped, and his right boot sank into mud almost to the knee. He pulled; the mud held him. Panic flared absurdly hot. He imagined the whole trench swallowing him while the file continued without noticing.

“Leave off hauling like that.” Billy braced a hand beneath his arm. “Slowly. Give it a chance to let go.”

Together they worked the boot free. Thomas stumbled against him, breathing hard.

“Same as a bad-tempered pit pony,” Billy said, though he was breathing hard too.

Their guide brought them into a bay where a short corporal stood beneath a shelf of corrugated iron. Gray bristles covered his jaw. One sleeve had been neatly repaired with darker cloth. He looked at the recruits without curiosity.

“Haskett. You belong to my section now. Names?”

Thomas gave his. Billy followed.

Haskett pointed to a recess. “Packs there for the moment. Rifles stay with you. Anything dropped in the water, you fetch it. Nobody else will.”

He moved a tin clear of a leak with his boot, a small exact movement, then checked Thomas’s rifle bolt.

“Keep this clean. Mud’s got plenty of time. You haven’t.”

A young officer appeared round the traverse carrying a hooded torch. He looked hardly older than the boys who had taught Thomas cricket at school, but the skin beneath his eyes was bruised purple. The eyes themselves were extraordinarily bright.

“The replacements, Corporal?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Good. Very good.” He looked from Thomas to Billy as though trying to place them from some previous meeting. “Lieutenant Allingham. You’ll learn the way about soon enough. Always seems impossible at first.”

He consulted a folded paper, murmuring something Thomas could barely hear.

“‘Where are the songs of Spring?’”

Thomas recognized the line. For an instant he felt the relief of hearing his own language in a foreign country.

Then a heavy report interrupted them. Allingham stopped speaking and listened with his head bent. After a moment he went on down the trench without finishing the poem.

Haskett showed them the fire step, a narrow ledge raised above the trench floor.

“Up here when ordered. Keep low getting into place. Don’t stroll along it showing your head. Down off it before you move to the next bay.”

Thomas climbed. Beyond the parapet he glimpsed a waste of stakes, torn earth, and wire. He had expected distances, a view opening grandly toward the enemy. Instead there was a darkness close enough to touch.

“Down,” Haskett said, and Thomas obeyed too quickly, knocking his elbow against the wall.

They were told about stand-to: rifles ready on the step before dawn, again at dusk. Sentry turns were given out. No smoking where a glow could show; no looking over to satisfy curiosity; no leaving a post until properly relieved.

Thomas repeated his orders when asked. Remembering them seemed desperately important, like remembering a lesson while the schoolmaster waited.

Later he sat beside Billy beneath a scrap of tarpaulin. Rain tapped above their heads. Somewhere a man laughed, and another asked him to shut his mouth.

“Wonder what they’re having for supper at home,” Thomas said.

“Something without this much weather in it.” Billy held out a piece of bread.

Thomas took it. A grinding whine grew over the trench, unlike the distant crashes, drawing nearer even as he listened.

He looked up.

A hand caught the back of his neck and drove him down.

The world struck him through the ground. His teeth clicked together. Something battered the tarpaulin; earth poured down his collar. For a moment there was no sound except a high thin singing inside his skull.

He found himself on his knees with his cheek against Billy’s boot.

Haskett crouched beside them. His hand was still on Thomas’s neck.

“Stay.”

Thomas stayed. He could not have done otherwise.

When the corporal released him, he tried to wipe his face and discovered that both hands were shaking. He thrust them beneath his arms.

“Close one,” Billy said. A little cut beneath his eye had begun to bleed.

Thomas wanted to ask whether it would happen again. He bit the question back.

Haskett inspected the bay, then sent a man to check beyond the traverse. A broken mug lay in the mud where the man had been sitting.

At dawn Thomas stood his first watch beside Billy. Mist filled the wire. His knees trembled against the sandbags, and he prayed Billy could not feel them through the boards.

A drop of water hung from a loose thread on the parapet. As the light strengthened, it shone briefly pink. Thomas watched it until Haskett touched his shoulder and told him to watch the ground ahead.

## Chapter 3: Eve of Battle

The order came at noon, carried along the trench by a runner who looked too young to be carrying anything more serious than a message from school. He stopped at Haskett’s bay, saluted badly, and handed over a folded paper.

“Raid tonight,” Haskett said after reading it. “Small party. We go over at two.”

Billy, who had been trying to dry his sock against his stomach, looked up. “We?”

“Our section provides four.” Haskett’s eyes moved over them. “Merrick. Preece. Evans. Miller.”

Thomas felt the trench tilt, though the duckboards remained beneath him.

The word had always belonged to newspapers and maps: raid. It was a clean word, almost bright, with a swift little sound at the end. He had imagined men crossing a field beneath a moon, rifles held ready, each motion purposeful. He had not imagined his own hands.

“Do we have to volunteer?” Billy asked.

“No.”

“Then I’m volunteering.”

Thomas looked at him.

Billy shrugged. “You’d only go and make a mess of it without me.”

Haskett gave no sign that he had heard the joke. “Both of you report to the dugout after stand-to. Bring rifle, bayonet, two bombs, and your wits. If you’ve got any.”

The afternoon narrowed around preparation. Thomas sat on an ammunition box with his rifle across his knees and worked a rag through the barrel until the cloth came out gray. He stripped the bolt, cleaned each piece, and laid them in a row. The oil smelled sharp and familiar, like the workshop behind his father’s schoolroom. His fingers were steadier over the rifle than they had been over his breakfast.

Billy watched him. “You missed a bit.”

“Where?”

“Here.” Billy pointed to the receiver, then grinned. “No, there. Or perhaps there. I’ve never cleaned one in my life.”

Thomas threw the rag at him.

Haskett appeared with a coil of sandbag string. He caught Thomas trying to knot it around his ankle.

“Your puttee’s coming loose.”

Thomas glanced down. The wool bandage had sagged in a dirty fold above his boot.

“Wrap it again.”

“I know how.”

“Then why are you doing it like you’re strangling a pudding?”

Haskett sat on the edge of the box and unwound the puttee. He showed Thomas how to begin at the ankle, how to keep the pressure even, how to overlap the cloth without cutting off the blood. His hands were broad and scarred, yet the work was careful.

“Too tight and your foot goes numb. Too loose and it catches on wire. War’s mostly choosing which trouble you prefer.”

Thomas copied him. Haskett inspected the result and grunted.

“Better. Keep your boot tied high. Mud gets in, dry it out as soon as you can.”

There was no comfort in the instruction, but there was something near it: the promise that details might still matter.

At dusk the four raiders gathered in the dugout. The timber roof trembled whenever the guns spoke. A map was pinned to the wall, its blue and red pencil marks crossing a strip of no-man’s-land that looked absurdly small. Haskett explained the wire-cutting party, the gaps, the signal rockets, the route home. Thomas listened until the German trench became a series of instructions rather than a place where men slept.

Billy produced a small tin cup. “Found this in a haversack.”

“Found or stole?” Thomas asked.

“Borrowed permanently.”

From inside his tunic he drew a dented tin of rum. There was barely enough for two mouthfuls. He poured it between them.

The liquor burned down Thomas’s throat and settled hotly in his empty stomach.

“To getting back,” Billy said.

“To getting back,” Thomas echoed.

Billy held the cup a moment longer. “And if you see a German, don’t stop to admire him.”

Thomas tried to laugh. It came out thin.

Lieutenant Allingham entered with his papers pressed beneath one arm. He had removed his cap, and his fair hair stood damply from his forehead. He paced the narrow dugout while Haskett repeated the timings. At the first heavy shell he stopped, listening.

“‘The moving waters at their priestlike task,’” he murmured.

Thomas knew that was Keats too, though he could not place the poem. Allingham continued to pace, reciting under his breath as the bombardment thickened.

“Sir?” Thomas said.

The lieutenant turned. “Yes?”

“Does the poetry help?”

Allingham’s bright eyes fixed on him. For a second he seemed offended; then he smiled with weary kindness.

“It gives the fear a shape, Merrick. Sometimes that is the best one can do.”

Another shell landed close. Dust sifted from the beams. The lantern swung, throwing every face into light and shadow.

When night came, Thomas climbed to the fire step for a final look. The cloud had broken. Stars filled the sky above the parapet, innumerable and cold. Between the two lines, the shell holes held strips of reflected starlight. The wire made black lace against the heavens. Far off, a flare rose and opened, washing the ruined earth white; then it faded, and the stars returned more beautiful for having been threatened.

Thomas thought of his father locking the schoolroom door. He thought of his mother’s photograph in the breast pocket of his tunic. He thought of poems that promised glory as if glory were a country a boy might enter by courage alone.

In the dugout, he opened the diary his father had given him. The first page waited beneath his hand.

*12 October 1916*

*Dear Father,*

*Tomorrow we go out on a raid. I am afraid, though I hope you will not think less of me for writing it. The men say fear is ordinary, and Corporal Haskett says a man who claims not to feel it is either a liar or a fool. I mean to be neither.*

*Still, I believe this is what I came for. Tomorrow I may prove myself a man. I may do something brave and worthy of the uniform, and when I return I shall understand what all the speeches meant. Billy is beside me, and we have promised to look after one another. The lieutenant recites Keats while the guns fire. It is strange, but the stars over the enemy’s wire are very beautiful tonight.*

*I wish you could see them. I wish I were at home. I hope to return. I am ready to go, though I am not ready at all.*

*Your loving son, Thomas.*

He closed the diary before the ink had fully dried. Outside, Haskett called the men to their places. Billy nudged his shoulder.

“Come on, schoolmaster.”

Thomas put the diary away, lifted his rifle, and followed him toward the dark. Hope walked beside dread, neither willing to yield the path.

## Chapter 4: The Raid

At ten minutes before two, the guns began.

There was no warning. One moment Thomas crouched in the dugout with his rifle between his knees, listening to Billy breathe through his nose; the next, the earth gave a tremendous cough. Dust sifted from the timber. The candle went out. Shells struck the German parapet, the wire, the fields behind them, and the whole world became concussion.

Thomas pressed his palms to his ears, though the noise entered through his boots and teeth. His diary lay in the inner pocket of his tunic, his father’s gift warm against his ribs. He thought of the words he had written: Tomorrow I may prove myself a man.

The phrase seemed to belong to somebody else.

“Keep your head down, Merrick,” Haskett said. His voice came oddly clear in the pauses. “Your head is no more valuable for being attached to you.”

Billy gave a short laugh. Dust whitened his face, and the little cut beneath his eye had opened again. Blood ran along his cheek.

Allingham appeared at the dugout entrance, bent nearly double. His bright eyes caught the flare of a runner’s candle.

“Two minutes. Check your bayonets. No firing until you’re in the trench.”

He began reciting under his breath. Thomas caught only fragments—some nightingale, some easeful death—and wondered whether the lieutenant knew which poem he was saying. Haskett checked them with quick hands, tugging straps and touching pouches.

Then the whistle blew.

Thomas climbed onto the fire step and pushed upward into a sky without colour. The parapet had become a jagged ridge of dirt and smoke. He followed Billy over it, and for several seconds there was nowhere to go but forward.

The machine guns began at once.

Their bullets came in a hard, tearing stream, as though an invisible saw were being drawn through the dark. Mud leapt before Thomas. A man to his left folded without a cry. Someone seized Thomas’s sleeve and was gone before he could turn.

He ran because Billy ran. The ground was a chain of craters joined by slime. His boots plunged ankle-deep, then knee-deep. The air smelled of wet clay, cordite, and something sweet he refused to name. Flares rose from the German line and opened in green-white blossoms. Under each one, dead men, wire, and running soldiers appeared with the sharpness of nightmare, then vanished.

Barbed wire caught Thomas by the tunic. He jerked forward; the cloth tore. Another barb seized his sleeve.

“Don’t fight it!” Billy shouted. “Back up!”

Thomas could not. He wrenched himself loose, ripping his forearm. Billy seized his webbing and dragged him through a gap.

They reached the trench with Haskett and one other man. The German position was narrower than Thomas had imagined: sandbags, broken boards, smoke, bodies. Men shouted in a language he did not understand. A bomb burst at the traverse and knocked him against the wall.

Then a German soldier came around the corner.

He could not have been more than seventeen. His helmet sat crookedly, his face pale beneath the grime, and one boot was untied. He held a bayonet, but the point wavered.

They stared at each other.

The German’s eyes were blue. Fear widened them, and Thomas saw not an enemy but a boy taken from some quiet place, perhaps carrying a mother’s photograph against his heart.

Thomas lowered his rifle.

The boy raised his.

There was no time to choose. Thomas thrust forward. The bayonet entered with a resistance both slight and terrible. The German made a small, surprised sound. His hands closed around the rifle. Their faces were close enough for Thomas to see mud at the corner of the boy’s mouth.

Thomas pulled the blade free.

The boy slid down the wall. He looked at Thomas once more, not with hatred but with astonishment so naked that Thomas felt it enter him like another wound.

“Move!” Haskett roared.

The raid became fragments: a bomb thrown into a dugout; a flare whitening smoke; Billy firing from the hip; Haskett shouting that they had found the wire party’s position. Thomas stumbled over a body and fired at a shadow without knowing whether he had struck anyone. Everywhere there was mud, shouting, and the metallic taste of fear.

Then the signal came to withdraw.

They climbed out beneath the German guns. The retreat across no-man’s-land seemed longer than the advance. Thomas crawled where he had run, dragging one leg through mud. Billy came behind him, swearing softly, and pushed his shoulder whenever he slowed. A man cried for help from a crater. Haskett turned, but fire struck the earth between them, and the cry stopped.

The guns suddenly ceased—not all, but enough. In the stunned quiet, Thomas heard his own breathing and the wet click of Billy’s boots.

A skylark rose from somewhere near the shattered wire.

It climbed invisibly into the paling dark, pouring out a song so pure that Thomas stopped crawling. The notes trembled above the craters and the dead, above the torn earth where men had tried to kill one another. He saw green flare smoke turning silver, rain on a broken stalk of grass. Beauty had not come to reward them or make their suffering noble. It was simply there, indifferent and astonishing.

“Thomas!” Billy called. “Get down!”

He moved again.

They reached the trench before dawn. Hands caught Thomas under the arms and hauled him over the parapet. He landed on the duckboards, shaking so violently that his rifle clattered from his fingers. Billy dropped beside him. Haskett counted them once, then again. Of the four who had gone out, three had returned.

No one said the missing man’s name.

Thomas leaned against the wall. His bayonet was dark. He stared until Haskett took the rifle and wiped the blade with a rag.

“You did what you had to,” Haskett said.

Thomas wanted to ask what that meant. He wanted to say the German boy had been afraid, that his boot had been untied, that he had looked no different from Billy or himself. Instead he vomited into the mud.

Billy crouched beside him and placed a hand between his shoulders. He made no joke and did not tell Thomas to be a man.

At sunrise the trench filled with smoke and men too tired to speak. Thomas touched the diary through his tunic. The words inside had not changed, but their meaning had. He no longer believed a man was proved by killing. He knew only that he had crossed a strip of earth as a boy and returned carrying someone else’s terror inside him.

## Chapter 5: Duckboards and Rain

For two days after the raid, Thomas spoke only when someone asked him a question.

He answered then, but his voice seemed to come from the far end of a tunnel. Yes, Corporal. No, Corporal. Tea? No, thank you. He sat on the fire step with his back against wet sandbags and watched water collect in the ruts between the duckboards. Each drop made a little ring, as if the trench were trying to remember what a pond was.

The company counted itself at dawn. The missing man was Private Lomas, a broad-shouldered farmhand from Norfolk whom Thomas had barely known. Two others had been brought in wounded: one with a shattered wrist, one with iron in his thigh. The rest stood in a crooked line while the sergeant called names. Men answered from the darkness, some plainly, some with the dazed obedience of sleepers.

Lomas’s body was found after breakfast.

They carried him back wrapped in a groundsheet. His face was not shown. Thomas watched four men lift him over the parapet and carry him to higher ground behind the support trench, where the chaplain waited with a book open.

There was no proper grave. The earth was too wet and the guns were beginning again. They laid Lomas among three strangers and marked the place with a board bearing his name in pencil. The chaplain spoke of resurrection. A shell burst beyond the hedge and covered the last words with black soil.

Back in the trench, Corporal Haskett found Thomas trying to scrape German mud from his boots with a ration tin.

“Stop that.”

Thomas looked up.

“You’ll wear the leather thin.” Haskett took the tin. “Boots aren’t china. They’re meant to be dirty.”

The corporal crouched and pulled Thomas’s left boot loose. His fingers were blunt, cracked, and quick.

“Show me your feet.”

“I’m all right.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

Thomas removed both socks. His feet were white and wrinkled, the skin soft as soaked paper. Haskett examined them like a doctor.

“Dry them whenever you can. Rub them, don’t just hold them near a brazier. Change socks. At night, loosen the boots but don’t leave them off. Lace them like this.”

He crossed the laces firmly around the ankle so the boot held without strangling the foot.

“Trench foot starts quiet,” he said. “Numbness first. Then blisters. Then you lose what you thought was part of you. War takes things in installments, Merrick.”

Thomas pulled on a dry pair from his kit. They were not his own; Haskett had found them somewhere.

“Thank you.”

“Don’t thank me. Remember it.”

That afternoon Haskett taught him how to sleep.

“Anywhere,” he said. “Five minutes is enough. Ten is luxury.”

Thomas sat with his head against the revetment while Haskett described the method: keep the rifle between the knees, tuck the hands under the armpits, breathe slowly, and let the noise pass around you without following it. Thomas tried. A shell landed behind the line. His eyes opened at once.

“Again,” Haskett said.

“I can’t.”

“You can. You’re just choosing to listen.”

Thomas tried again. He slept perhaps three minutes and woke with his cheek against the sandbag. Haskett nodded as if he had passed an examination.

Billy came back from the ration party with a tin mug, two cigarettes, and a grin that held more weariness than amusement. The cut beneath his eye was crusted darkly.

“Found these,” he said, offering Thomas one cigarette. “The mug was guarded by a man who had no teeth. I outwitted him.”

“You stole it.”

“Outwitted.”

They shared the cigarette beneath corrugated iron. Smoke tasted sharp and comforting. Around them, men traded tobacco, biscuit, rumors, and insults. A private named Simmonds claimed he had seen a German officer run away wearing only one boot.

“Good sense,” Haskett said. “The boot was probably full of war.”

The men laughed. It was an ugly, grateful sound. Thomas found himself laughing too, though the laugh broke in the middle. No one looked at him. That kindness was greater than attention would have been.

Lieutenant Allingham returned near dusk, his greatcoat spattered with clay. He had been at battalion headquarters during the raid, helping sort reports, though he offered no explanation. His eyes seemed brighter than ever.

“The world’s indifferent,” he murmured, pacing before the dugout. “And still the nightingale sings.”

A bombardment began. The timber above them shuddered. Allingham raised his voice over the thunder, reciting Keats in broken pieces. He lost the lines, started again, and gripped a table until his knuckles blanched.

Thomas watched him and understood that poetry had not saved the lieutenant. It merely gave his fear a shape it could wear in public.

“Do you remember the next part?” Allingham asked suddenly.

Thomas did not. Billy shook his head.

Haskett said, “I remember the tea’s gone cold.”

The lieutenant stared at him, then laughed too loudly. The shelling went on.

By the fourth day, Thomas knew the trench’s small geography: the plank that rocked under the heel, the niche where dry matches were kept, the corner occupied by a rat with a torn ear. He knew who snored, who lied about letters, who saved sugar for Sundays. He began sharing his bread without deciding to. Billy slept with one shoulder against his. When a replacement soldier arrived trembling at the fire step, Thomas showed him where to put his feet.

“Not there,” he said. “That board moves.”

The rain came in the night.

It began as tapping on the corrugated roofs, then thickened until the trench walls streamed. Water ran down the steps and poured through the dugout entrance. The duckboards lifted and knocked together. Men bailed with helmets, tins, anything that would hold water for a second.

Thomas stood ankle-deep in the cold flood, his rifle above his head. Haskett shouted orders. Billy cursed the weather, the Germans, and the ancestor who had invented mud. Somewhere nearby a man began to sing, softly and without tune.

Thomas looked along the trench. Faces appeared and vanished in the rain: Billy’s, Haskett’s, the frightened replacement’s, Allingham’s pale one. They were not heroes in a battle. They were men waiting, working, enduring the same wet darkness.

The thought came without drama, and once it came he could not dislodge it.

The war was not a battle.

A battle had a beginning and an end, a charge and a withdrawal, a name that could be printed in a newspaper. This was different. This was the mud in his boots, the damp in his blanket, the taste of smoke, the habit of counting living men. It was a condition they inhabited, like weather or sickness.

The rain kept falling. Thomas tightened his grip on the rifle and passed a sandbag to Billy. Together they built the wall higher while the guns muttered beyond the darkness.

## Chapter 6: Gas

The alarm came at two in the morning, not as a shout but as a bell struck somewhere in the dark.

“Gas!”

The word ran along the trench faster than any order. Men woke beneath their greatcoats. Someone knocked over a brazier. Someone else began praying in Welsh. Thomas came upright with his heart already beating hard, though he could see nothing beyond the black ribs of the dugout entrance.

Haskett was on his feet.

“Box respirators! Move!”

The wind had changed. A yellow-green cloud was spilling over the parapet, low and thick, creeping down into the trench as if the earth itself had begun to breathe. It shone faintly where the moon touched it. The sight was almost beautiful, and that was the first terrible thing about it.

Thomas fumbled at the satchel on his chest. His fingers found the buckles, slipped, found them again. Around him men cursed and gagged. Haskett had his mask on already, the rubber face staring blankly from beneath its hood.

“Thomas! Get it sealed!”

“I’m trying.”

The cloud reached him.

He drew one breath before the mask came over his face.

It was as though he had swallowed a coal. Fire spread through his throat and down into his lungs. He coughed once, violently, and the second breath would not come. His eyes flooded. He tore at the bag, dropping it into the mud, and for a moment stood in the green haze with his mouth open, unable to understand why the world had become pain.

Billy seized him by the collar.

“Mask, you bloody fool! Mask!”

Thomas found the mask again. Billy slapped the rubber into his hands and pulled the straps tight while Thomas choked. The small glass eyepieces filled with blurred faces and yellow light. Air came through the filter, hot and sour, but air came.

Men stumbled past them. One fell against the fire step and did not rise. Another clawed at his throat though his mask was secure. The gas poured onward, indifferent to rank, rifle, prayer, or courage. It entered every hollow the trench offered. It slid beneath the dugout door. It settled in the low places.

Thomas’s lungs would not accept the mask’s thin breath. He gagged inside it. His knees buckled.

“Up!” Billy shouted. “Stay up!”

He dragged Thomas toward the rear of the trench, one arm hooked around his chest. Thomas tried to walk, but the duckboards tilted and lengthened under his boots. The walls breathed. A rat passed over his hand, and he thought it was a child with a wet face.

At the communication trench, cold night air touched the gap beneath his mask. He tore it away and vomited into the mud. The spasm seemed to wrench his ribs apart. Billy held him upright while the cloud behind them glowed like a poisoned sunrise.

“Don’t lie down,” Billy said. “Keep moving.”

“I can’t.”

“You can. You’ve got to.”

They went on. Thomas saw the village church at home, white in summer sunlight. His mother stood beside the gate, though she had been dead four years. She held out a cup and told him it was time for school. Then the cup became a shell, and the church bell became the alarm.

At the aid post a medical orderly cut away the straps and forced him onto a trestle. Men crowded the little shelter, coughing behind their masks. Lamps swung overhead. The doctor’s hands moved quickly over Thomas’s chest.

“Breathe in.”

Thomas tried. His lungs caught and rattled.

“Again.”

He vomited onto the floor.

Billy stood at his head, mud dried yellow on his face. “He got a lungful before he sealed up.”

“So did half the section,” the doctor said. “Hold him still.”

There was a basin, a syringe, a blanket that smelled of carbolic. Thomas drifted in and out beneath them. Each time he surfaced, the pain had changed its shape. It clawed at his throat, then pressed beneath his breastbone, then became a flock of small birds beating against his ribs.

Once he woke convinced he was back in the German trench, looking into the dead boy’s frightened eyes. The boy wore a mask. Thomas tried to tell him he was sorry, but the rubber mouthpiece swallowed the words.

Billy’s hand closed around his wrist.

“You’re here, Tom.”

Thomas turned toward the voice. “Where?”

“Aid post. You’re alive.”

The statement sounded less like comfort than an accusation.

Near dawn the doctor gave him a cup of water. Thomas held it with both hands but could not drink. His fingers trembled so badly that the surface quivered. Billy took the cup and touched it to his lips.

“Small sips.”

“You should sleep.”

“I have been.”

“You haven’t.”

Billy shrugged. “I’m Welsh. We’re difficult to kill.”

Thomas managed something like a laugh, which turned at once into coughing. Billy waited until it passed, then tucked the blanket beneath his chin.

For two days Thomas remained at the aid post. The gas had not killed him, the doctor said, but his lungs were inflamed and might trouble him for weeks. The words were spoken in the same tone used for damaged equipment: a thing assessed, recorded, and returned if it still functioned.

On the second afternoon, an orderly led Thomas to a washroom. There was a small mirror above the basin, clouded at the edges. He leaned toward it.

A gaunt face stared back. The cheeks were hollow. The eyes seemed too large, ringed in purple, and held the fixed astonishment of someone who had opened a door onto death and found it empty on the other side.

Thomas touched the glass.

He looked older than his father.

Behind him Billy appeared in the mirror, carrying two mugs of tea and wearing an expression of offended suspicion.

“Don’t believe it,” Billy said.

“Believe what?”

“That face. It’s a gas trick. You’ll look worse after the tea, but that’ll be the tea’s fault.”

Thomas turned. Billy set one mug down and sat beside him.

“What happened to the others?” Thomas asked.

Billy looked at the floor. “Some went back. Some went to the dressing station. Sergeant Vale didn’t make it.”

Thomas closed his eyes. The gas had no face, no uniform, no age. It had not looked at Vale before taking him. It had not hated him. That seemed worse than hatred.

A bullet at least had a direction. A shell had a sound before it arrived. The gas came without a shape a man could fight, and survival depended on the speed of frightened fingers and the turn of an invisible wind.

That night Billy slept in a chair beside the cot, his head tilted against the wall. Thomas woke choking, certain the yellow cloud was filling the room. He struck out blindly. Billy caught his hand before it reached the floor.

“Easy,” he murmured. “It’s only air.”

Thomas listened. His breath rasped in and out. Beyond the canvas walls, guns muttered over the dark.

He had once believed surviving meant being spared for some purpose. Now he knew it might mean nothing more than the wind shifting at the right moment, a strap caught, a friend refusing to let go.

He looked at Billy’s sleeping face and held the thought close, though it frightened him.

Luck had saved him.

Luck could change its mind.

## Chapter 7: The Lull

The guns did not stop so much as forget them.

For three days after the gas attack, the line remained strangely quiet. There were still shots at dawn, a scattered shell beyond the ruined village, the occasional crack of a rifle answered from the German parapet, but no barrage came to tear the earth open. The silence between these noises seemed deliberate, as if both armies had agreed to breathe carefully.

Thomas returned from the aid post with a cough that caught him by the ribs. The doctor had called it a convalescence and the sergeant had called it luck. Haskett called it no excuse for neglecting his boots.

“Dry them,” the corporal said, pushing a rag into Thomas’s hands. “Lungs or no lungs, trench foot’s still got your name on it.”

Thomas sat beneath the duckboards, rubbing mud from his leather. His breath rasped. Every few minutes Billy glanced over, pretending not to.

“I’m not going to vanish,” Thomas said.

“Didn’t say you were.”

“You keep looking.”

“I’m looking at the boots. They’re a disgrace.”

Billy’s own boots were worse. His hands were blistered from dragging Thomas through the gas, and the skin across his palms shone pink and raw. Yet he used those hands to mend a torn sandbag, roll cigarettes, and steal the best portion of bully beef whenever the cookhouse sent it forward.

The men began to make use of the quiet. Private Naylor sang songs from the music hall in a thin, tuneless voice, and was pelted with lumps of clay until he changed to hymns. Someone taught Thomas a Welsh chorus whose words he could not understand. He sang anyway, following Billy’s broad mouth through the verses. The sound rose from the trench, uneven and brave, and for a moment the men were not soldiers but a badly assembled choir in a church with no roof.

Letters arrived in bundles, softened by rain and passed from hand to hand. A wife sent a lock of hair. A brother wrote that the baby had begun to walk. Naylor received a page covered in his mother’s cramped handwriting and read it three times before folding it into the lining of his tunic. Haskett got no letter and claimed this was because his family had the good sense to know he was uninteresting.

Thomas had one from his father. It contained news of the school, the price of coal, and a complaint about mice in the pantry. At the bottom, Mr. Merrick had written: You promised to write even when there is nothing to report.

Thomas took out his diary, then put it away again. There was nothing he could make true on a page. He wrote instead that he was well, that the weather was tolerable, and that Billy continued to be impossible. The lie was small enough to carry.

On the fourth morning, while they were filling sandbags, Thomas saw a spot of blue above the parapet.

At first he thought it was cloth caught on the wire. Then the wind moved it.

“Billy.”

Billy followed his gaze. A single wildflower had rooted in a crack between the sandbags, its stem impossibly fine, its petals blue as a scrap of summer sky. Around it lay splintered wood, rusted tins, and the blackened stumps of grass. The flower had chosen the worst place in the world and opened there without permission.

Billy climbed onto the fire step.

“Get down,” Haskett snapped.

“Just looking.”

“Looking gets men shot.”

“Then I’ll look quickly.”

He reached through the gap and cupped the flower in both hands. His blistered palms made a careful shelter around it. Thomas watched him, suddenly afraid that Billy’s touch would break the stem.

“Don’t pull it,” Thomas said.

“I know.”

They stared at the tiny bloom. Neither laughed. The noise of the trench fell away: the coughs, the boots, the muttered curses, even the distant guns. Thomas thought of his mother’s photograph in the leather valise, of the village lane in spring, and of the German boy’s eyes before the rifle butt struck him. Beauty did not erase any of it. That was what made it terrible. It stood beside the horror and refused to explain itself.

Billy lowered his hands.

“Could press it,” he said.

“In what?”

“Your diary. You never use the thing.”

Thomas almost told him he had used it once. Instead he took the flower when Billy offered it. The stem trembled against his fingers.

That evening he laid it between two blank pages, wrapped in a scrap of clean paper. He closed the diary and put it in his breast pocket, close to the place where his heart still sometimes raced for no reason.

At night the moon rose over no-man’s-land. It silvered the shell holes and the torn wire until the desolation looked almost peaceful. Mist rested in the craters. Somewhere beyond the German line a flare climbed, pale and slow, then went out.

Lieutenant Allingham stood beneath the shelter of the traverse, his greatcoat buttoned to the throat. He had been quiet all day. Now he began to recite.

“Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird!”

His voice was low, but the men heard him. A shell whined far overhead and burst beyond the village. Dust sifted from the sandbags. Allingham did not stop.

“Not so, my heart; thou hast no lore—”

He lost the line. His mouth remained open. Thomas saw the lieutenant’s hand tighten around the pages he carried, though the poem was memorized.

“—no, no, not this,” Allingham murmured, then began again from the beginning.

Billy leaned beside Thomas. “He’ll get there.”

“Will he?”

Billy looked at him, then at the moonlit waste. “Not tonight, perhaps.”

The lieutenant continued, giving the words to the darkness. Thomas listened without trying to find glory in them. He heard instead the fear beneath the music, the stubborn human wish to make a shape from what could not be endured.

The guns muttered. The flower lay pressed against his chest.

For the first time since the gas, Thomas drew a full breath. It hurt, but it was his. Above the parapet, the moon shone on the wire, the mud, and the small hidden lives that had survived them all. He understood that the world’s beauty was not a promise of safety. It was only beauty—fragile, indifferent, and therefore precious.

He kept the diary closed. The flower, he thought, had said enough.

## Chapter 8: No Man's Land

The wire had been cut in three places by shells, and battalion wanted it mended before dawn. Allingham brought the order down the trench with a folded paper in his hand. Four men, stakes, coils, muffled mallets. No firing unless discovered.

Thomas said he would go.

Haskett looked at him. “You can barely cough without folding in half.”

“I can crawl.”

He wanted something to do whose end could be seen: a gap closed, a stake driven, a length of wire made fast. He was tired of waiting for his own breath.

Billy put down the sock he was darning. “Then I’m coming.”

“You’re not obliged,” Thomas said.

“Neither are you. Seems we’re both fools.”

Haskett inspected the cloth wrapped around the tools. He made Thomas cough into his sleeve, listened, and said nothing more about it. At midnight he led them up the ladder. Naylor followed with the spare stakes.

Beyond the parapet, mist lay so low that Thomas could see the broken posts above it but not his hands. He crawled after Billy’s boots, pushing the coil before him. Water entered at his cuffs. Once his knee found something soft beneath the mud, and he shifted his weight without looking.

Haskett halted beside the first gap. They worked by touch. Billy held the stake while Thomas eased a strand round it, feeling for the barbs through his gloves. A strand caught Billy’s sleeve.

“Even the fences want a bit of me,” he whispered.

Thomas freed him. Behind the mist, the German line was invisible. A shovel struck earth somewhere over there; then a man coughed. The ordinary sound made Thomas stop with the wire between his fingers.

Haskett touched his heel. Work.

They had reached the second gap when the flare rose.

Its hiss came first. Billy lowered himself, but the light opened before he was flat, whitening the mist, drawing each barb sharp against the ground. Thomas lay with his face in the mud. Beside him Billy’s shoulder moved once.

The rifle cracked.

It was one shot, picked out from all that whiteness. Billy made a small, surprised noise and rolled against the stake.

Thomas waited for him to swear. The flare swung overhead. A thread of saliva stretched from Billy’s mouth to his sleeve.

“Billy?”

Haskett was already crawling towards them. He pressed a hand below Billy’s collarbone, then took Thomas by the wrist.

“Under his arms. Keep low.”

Naylor gathered the loose wire away from their legs. Thomas pulled Billy backwards into a shallow crater while Haskett covered them with his rifle. The mist closed over their heads. Billy’s boots left two dark furrows in its silver floor.

In the crater Thomas tried to put on the field dressing. Blood came between his fingers, warm enough to feel through the wet wool. Billy watched his hands with a puzzled frown.

“Hold that,” Thomas said. “There. Press.”

Billy’s hand slipped. Thomas put it back.

“We’re nearly in.”

They were not. Beyond the crater lay a smashed belt of wire and thirty yards of open ground. Thomas lifted him again, bracing Billy’s back against his chest. His lungs tightened; he swallowed a cough and dragged. Each pull brought a faint breath from Billy, as though Thomas were hurting him deliberately.

“Sorry,” he whispered. “Sorry. Just a little.”

Billy turned his face towards him. His lips moved.

“What?”

But there was only breath, moist and terribly slight. Thomas bent his ear to Billy’s mouth. For a moment he thought he felt the beginning of a word.

Then Billy’s weight changed.

Thomas kept his arms locked around him. He waited, counting without deciding to count. At ten he began again. Haskett crawled back from the wire and crouched beside them, his hand briefly at Billy’s throat.

“Merrick.”

“We can carry him.”

“Yes,” Haskett said. “We can.”

They brought him through the gap feet first. Men reached down from the parapet, and Thomas would not release him until Haskett separated his fingers from the tunic.

In the trench, under a hooded lamp, Billy looked unaccountably untidy. One puttee had unwound; mud filled the cut beneath his eye where the old scar lay. Thomas straightened the puttee. He could do nothing about the rest.

They buried him the next morning behind the support line, where a ruined orchard gave a little shelter from observation. Thomas helped dig. The spade jarred against buried brick. Someone had found boards for a cross, and Haskett wrote the name in pencil, going over each letter twice.

Before they covered him, Thomas took out his diary.

The flower had flattened to a thin blue stain with a stem. He unfolded its scrap of paper carefully. Billy’s hands were bare; someone had cleaned them, exposing the raw patches on his palms. Thomas placed the flower between them and brought the fingers together.

He had meant to say something. With the others waiting beside the grave, he could think only that the hands ought to be warmer.

The first earth fell on the blanket. Thomas took up his spade.

Later, a shell burst near the communication trench. Naylor ducked against him. Thomas remained standing until Haskett hauled him down by the belt.

“Still got to take cover.”

Thomas nodded. There was grit between his teeth. He had heard the shell coming and understood perfectly what it was, but the familiar shrinking inside him had not happened. Haskett watched him for a moment before moving on.

That evening Thomas sat where Billy had darned his sock. The needle was still stuck through the heel. He put it away so nobody would sit on it.

From Billy’s effects Haskett had given him an envelope bearing his mother’s address. Thomas copied it onto a fresh one. He set a sheet against his knee and held the pencil until the tremor diminished.

Dear Mrs Preece,

I am writing as a friend of your son. Billy was the best friend I had here.

That much he could leave. He read it again, then bent over the paper. What could he send her of the crater, of the mouth trying to speak? His own mother had once waited at a window when he was late from school.

He wrote slowly, making the letters clear, in the hand his father had taught him.

He did not suffer. He was very brave, and he died peacefully.

The words lay neatly beneath his hand. They looked like words he had believed before he came to France.

## Chapter 9: The Long Dark

The months did not pass so much as accumulate.

Rain found them in October and remained through November, working into wool, bread, rifle bolts, and the hollows beneath the eyes. The trench changed shape daily. A parapet collapsed; a duckboard vanished; a sump filled and was emptied and filled again. Men slept wherever the mud permitted them to lie. They woke with their mouths tasting of metal and went on.

Thomas learned to go on before he learned that he was doing it.

His cough had settled into a dry rasp, no longer violent enough to earn the doctor’s attention. He could lift ammunition, crawl a communication trench, and stand watch through a night of rain. When the corporal above him was sent to hospital with a fever, the sergeant put the stripe on Thomas’s sleeve.

“Lance corporal Merrick,” Haskett said, examining it. “Try not to let it improve your character.”

Thomas looked at the narrow band of cloth. “What does it improve?”

“Your chances of being blamed.”

The men laughed. Thomas did too, because it was expected. The promotion gave him no authority he had not already been forced to possess. He counted blankets, found missing boots, divided rum, and told new men when to keep their heads down. At night he checked the sentries by touch, placing a hand on a shoulder before speaking. He knew what fear did to a man’s hearing. He knew that a quiet trench could be more dangerous than a loud one.

The new recruits arrived with clean faces and boots too stiff to bend. They asked how far away the German trench was, whether the rations would improve, and when they might see the famous guns. One boy from Kent showed Thomas a photograph of his sisters and said he had promised to bring them back a helmet.

Thomas showed him how to wrap a scarf around the rifle stock against the cold.

“Keep your spare socks inside your tunic,” he said. “And don’t stand under the sandbags when the shelling starts.”

“Where should I stand, then?”

“Somewhere else.”

The boy smiled, grateful for the joke. Three nights later a shell came through the traverse. Thomas found the photograph in the boy’s breast pocket, its corners burned black. There was no helmet to send home.

After that, Thomas stopped learning the recruits’ names until he had to write them in the company book.

Allingham lasted until December. He had grown thinner, his bright eyes sunk in bruised hollows, but still carried his poetry into the dugout as another man might carry a flask. During a bombardment he stood with one hand against the wall and recited Keats over the thunder.

“Where palsy shakes a few, sad, last grey hairs,” he said, and then the dugout door blew inward.

The blast threw him against the table. For one instant Thomas saw him sitting upright in the dust, looking surprised at the blood spreading through his greatcoat. Then the men were lifting him. Allingham gripped Thomas’s sleeve.

“Don’t let them lose my book,” he said.

“It’s here.”

“No. The other one.”

There was no other one. Thomas found a small volume of Keats beneath the overturned table and placed it in the officer’s hand as they carried him away. Allingham’s fingers closed around it. He was conscious, but his face had the blank, astonished expression of a child being taken from school.

They said he would be sent home. Thomas never learned whether that meant recovery or merely a different sort of waiting.

Haskett’s turn came in January.

A shell struck at stand-to, when the men were still blinking sleep from their eyes. The section disappeared beneath a brown eruption. Thomas woke on his back with the taste of mud in his mouth and a ringing that erased every other sound. Someone was calling his name.

Haskett lay against the fire step. Below the knee, his left leg had gone.

For a moment Thomas’s mind refused the shape of it. Haskett was the man who had taught him to dry his feet, to sleep in five-minute pieces, to duck without shame. The leg was an impossible absence beneath the torn trousers.

“Get the stretcher,” Haskett said.

Thomas knelt, pressing a dressing where the blood came fastest. His hands worked without trembling. Haskett watched him with a grim approval.

“You’ve got the knack now.”

“Save your breath.”

“Never did know what to do with it.”

The stretcher-bearers arrived. Before they lifted him, Haskett caught Thomas’s hand. His grip was hard and dry, the same grip with which he had once dragged Thomas beneath a shellburst.

“Keep them alive, Merrick.”

“I’ll try.”

“Don’t try. Do.”

Then they carried him away through the rain, and Thomas stood with Haskett’s blood on his cuffs until someone ordered him back to work.

Rumours came more regularly than letters. Germany was finished. The war would end by Christmas. The Kaiser had abdicated. The Kaiser had not abdicated. An armistice had been requested, rejected, signed, broken. Each rumour moved along the trench in a whisper and died beneath the next shell.

Thomas listened without hope. Hope had become another thing that made a man careless.

He kept the diary in the inner pocket of his tunic. Its first entry remained there, the page softened at the corners from being handled. He had thought, before the raid, that a man proved himself in a single act. Since then he had killed, carried, buried, obeyed, and survived. None of it had supplied an answer. The blank pages seemed less empty than honest.

He did not write. There was nothing to say that the mud did not already know.

On the morning of 11 November, the guns were firing as usual. Thomas was distributing tea when a runner appeared, pale and breathless.

“At eleven,” he said. “Ceasefire at eleven.”

Nobody cheered. The men looked at one another as if he had announced a change in the weather.

The bombardment continued. Then, at the appointed hour, it stopped.

Silence entered the trench in stages. First the great guns ceased. Then the machine guns. Then the rifles along the line. The air, suddenly released from its labour, seemed too large. Water dripped from the sandbags. Somewhere a rat moved beneath the duckboards.

Thomas stood on the fire step. Across the torn fields, the German parapet remained where it had always been. No flag rose. No music played. The dead did not speak.

A man nearby began to laugh, then covered his face. Another whispered the names of people who were not there.

Thomas touched the stripe on his sleeve. It was wet and dark with mud. He thought of Billy’s blue flower, Haskett’s vanished leg, Allingham’s book clasped in a bloody hand, and the boy from Kent with his sisters’ photograph. The silence did not feel like peace. It felt like the absence of pain, and he did not yet know how to live inside it.

For the first time in years, nothing was coming toward them.

Thomas waited for relief.

What came was fear.

## Chapter 10: The Silence and the Sound

For several minutes after eleven, Thomas remained on the fire step, waiting for the sky to change its mind.

The guns had stopped, but the war had not yet left his body. His ears rang in the great vacancy. His lungs rasped when he breathed. Every few seconds his shoulders tightened, expecting the next concussion, the next shower of earth and splinters. Around him, men moved cautiously, as if silence were a wire they might trip.

“Is that it?” Private Naylor asked.

Thomas looked across no-man’s-land. “For now.”

Naylor gave a short, uncertain laugh. “You’re meant to say it’s over.”

Thomas had learned not to say what he did not know.

By afternoon, word came down in writing. The armistice was signed. The men were to remain at their posts, weapons cleaned, sentries maintained. The army had discovered that even peace required orders.

Someone found a bottle. Someone else produced a tin of bully beef and divided it with a solemnity usually reserved for funerals. There was talk of trains, leave, mothers, wives, pubs, and beds. One man said he would sleep for a week. Another said he would never sleep again. They spoke loudly, filling the trench with the sound that had been taken from it.

Thomas went into the dugout and took out his diary.

The old entry faced him, its ink faded but legible. He could still see his younger hand declaring that tomorrow he might prove himself a man. The boy who had written it believed manhood was a gate, and courage the key. He had not understood that a gate could open onto a country from which there was no return.

He turned to a clean page and wrote:

*11 November 1918*

*The guns are quiet and I cannot trust it. I hear the voices of the dead in every wind.*

*Billy is still beside me when I close my eyes. He would have made some remark about the army finding a way to spoil even a holiday. I remember his hands, blistered and careful around the little blue flower. It is buried with him in the orchard, though sometimes I feel it pressed against my heart.*

*Haskett told me to keep the men alive. I do not know whether I have done so, only that I am still here. Allingham went away with Keats in his hand. I hope he found a room where the walls do not shake.*

*I remember the German boy. I remember his eyes before I remember his name, and I do not know his name. He was my age. I have carried him farther than I carried some of our own.*

*The war has ended. I am afraid of what is left.*

Thomas stopped there. He did not sign the entry. His father had given him the diary to hold the things he could not say aloud, but the page already seemed crowded with ghosts.

Before dawn the next morning, he walked the trench alone. The men slept in heaps beneath their blankets, their faces slack with exhaustion. He passed the fire step where Haskett had taught him to watch the dark, the bend where Billy had once shared his tobacco, and the dugout wall against which Allingham had recited Keats while shells came down. Water glimmered in the communication trench. A dead rat floated in it, turning slowly as if searching for a current.

At the front of the line, Thomas climbed out.

No-man’s-land was smaller than he remembered. The craters had softened at their edges, and thin grass showed through the churned mud. Wire lay rusting in loops. A helmet filled with rainwater rested beside a shattered rifle. In the pale morning, the battlefield had a terrible gentleness. Frost silvered the splinters. A spider had spun a net between two stakes, each bead of dew holding the colourless sky.

Thomas stood before the German trench. There was no enemy there now, only an empty line and a broken door hanging from its hinges. He thought of the boy beneath the earth and felt no forgiveness arrive. Beauty did not mend anything. It merely appeared, exact and indifferent, among the ruins.

Then he turned back.

Demobilisation took weeks. There were lists, examinations, signatures, queues, and a final exchange of kit. Thomas surrendered the rifle he had cleaned through two winters. The clerk glanced at his papers and called him nineteen, though Thomas felt older than the men who had taught him to shave. He boarded a train with a bundle, his father’s leather valise, the Bible, his mother’s photograph, and the diary tucked safely inside his tunic.

The journey home moved through a country learning how to look ordinary again. Stations filled with uniforms and embraces. At one platform a woman searched every arriving face until she found the wrong man and folded against him anyway. Thomas kept his eyes on the window.

He did not write to his father. He had promised letters when he had nothing to report; now he had too much, and none of it could be made useful. Instead he walked from the station along the country road toward the village, his boots sinking into clean brown earth.

Hedges leaned over the lane, green and wet. A horse cropped grass in a field. Somewhere beyond the elms, a church bell struck the hour. The sounds entered him without warning: not as replacements for the guns, but as things that had existed before them and would continue after him.

A skylark rose from the hedgerow.

It climbed into the pale sky, invisible almost at once, and poured its song over the road. Thomas stopped. He had heard that song above shellfire, when the world was breaking and the bird seemed to know nothing of it. He had thought then that beauty might be a promise. Now he understood it was not a promise, nor an answer. It was simply there, carrying the weight men placed upon it.

His knees weakened. He covered his face and wept.

He wept for Billy’s jokes and Haskett’s hand, for Allingham’s frightened eyes, for a nameless German boy, for the recruits whose names he had refused to learn. He wept for the boy who had written that tomorrow he might prove himself a man. The tears did not make him clean. They only proved that something in him had survived besides duty.

At the edge of the village, Mr. Merrick stood beside the gate. He had grown thinner. His hair was whiter than Thomas remembered. For a moment neither moved.

Then his father crossed the road and took him in his arms.

Thomas held him tightly, smelling wool, chalk dust, and the familiar soap of home. He wanted to say that he was safe. He wanted to say that he was sorry. Instead he said, “I’m here.”

His father’s hand pressed the back of his head. “Yes,” he whispered. “You are.”

Behind them, the skylark continued singing. Thomas listened without trying to turn the sound into poetry. He knew now that the truest beauty and the deepest horror could inhabit the same world, sometimes the same breath. He walked through the gate beside his father, carrying the dead with him, and did not pretend to be the boy who had left.
