The trench was narrower than Thomas had imagined, and there was no room in it for the way he had practiced walking. At the depot he had learned to carry his shoulders square, his rifle upright, his eyes on a point beyond the next man’s head. Here he went sideways, dragging his pack against the wet timber, while water poured from somebody’s abandoned coat into his collar. The guide ahead held a shaded lantern low. In its moving circle, the duckboards appeared one at a time, like steps being laid over a well.

“Keep up,” the guide said.

Thomas tried. His right boot slipped between two boards, and the mud took hold above his ankle. He pulled; the boot stayed. The men behind stopped without speaking. Heat climbed his neck. He had wanted, more than anything, to arrive without making a fool of himself.

A hand reached past him and caught his pack strap.

“Don’t fight it standing straight. Bend your knee.”

The speaker was a broad man with a pale line through one eyebrow. He braced Thomas against the revetment while Thomas worked his foot backward. The boot came free with a sucking noise.

“There. Ground’s keen to enlist you.”

Someone behind them laughed. Thomas laughed too, a fraction late.

They reached a recess roofed with corrugated iron and earth. A candle burned in a bottle beside three tin mugs. The broad man took Thomas’s rifle before he could object, checked its muzzle, and handed it back.

“Clay in there can kill you. Name?”

“Thomas Vale.”

“Mercer. Put your things on that shelf, Vale. Not on the floor unless you want them swimming.”

Thomas bent beneath the roof. His orders, folded inside his tunic, had brought him through a railway siding, a ruined village, and a communication trench that seemed to double back on itself forever. Now he had reached his section, and all he could think about was the relief of removing his pack. When he lifted it onto the shelf, his arms continued to feel its weight.

A smaller soldier sat against the wall repairing a sock. He introduced himself as Finch, then moved his knees to make room.

“Tea?”

“Yes, please.”

“Civilized, this one.”

The mug was hot enough to hurt. Thomas held it with both hands. Beneath the tea he could taste smoke and something metallic, but it was the first thing anyone had given him here that was not an instruction.

Outside, a gun sounded. The candle flame leaned, recovered. Thomas looked toward the entrance.

“Ours,” Mercer said.

Thomas nodded as though he had known. He waited for another report, trying to discover what made that sound different from an enemy gun. At home he had listened to thunder from his bedroom and counted the seconds until the rain. Here the earth carried the noise through his boot soles.

Mercer asked him to help with a leaking seam above the shelf. They pushed a folded sack between the iron and a roof beam, then wedged it with a splinter of wood. Water ran down Thomas’s wrist. When they finished, the drip had shifted away from the blankets.

“Good,” Mercer said. “Now you can say you’ve improved the place.”

For a moment Thomas felt absurdly pleased.

A sergeant stooped into the entrance and asked Mercer whether the new man had arrived. Thomas stood too quickly and struck his head on the roof.

“Stay down,” the sergeant said. He inspected Thomas’s face, then his rifle. “You’ll be with Mercer tomorrow. Listen to him. Rations shortly. Get whatever sleep you can.”

When he had gone, Thomas rubbed his scalp.

“What happens tomorrow?”

Finch stopped drawing his needle through the sock. Mercer rested his hand on the edge of the shelf.

“We go across before light.”

“An attack?”

“Yes.”

Thomas looked at the mug between his knees. He had known there would be an attack sometime. During training, sometime had stretched comfortably beyond every meal and inspection. Now it had become the dark interval on the other side of sleep.

“Stay near me,” Mercer said. “If you lose me, find Finch. Don’t run after somebody just because he’s running.”

Thomas wanted to ask whether they were frightened. Instead he asked where to put his blanket.

Later, with the others settling around him, he took out the little diary his sister had bought at the station. Its first pages contained distances traveled and descriptions of meals. He shaded the candle with his palm and wrote.

*Eve of my first engagement.*

*We attack before daylight. I thought I should have more to say about it. Mercer helped me out of the mud and Finch gave me tea. Finch has lent me dry socks while mine hang up. I must remember to return them.*

*I hope I shall do properly. I hope nobody at home supposes I am already brave.*

He closed the book before he could scratch out the last sentence.

Beyond the dugout, a flare rose over the parapet. Thomas watched its light fall through the entrance, illuminating droplets caught on a strand of wire. Each shone briefly, perfectly round. Then darkness returned, and Mercer reached past him to turn the hanging socks nearer the candle.


Thomas woke with Mercer’s hand on his shoulder and the certainty that he had forgotten something. The candle had burned into the bottle neck. Above the shelf, the sack they had wedged into the leaking seam sagged with water, but the blankets beneath it remained dry. He stared at this small success while Mercer shook him again.

“Boots on. Bring everything you’ll need.”

For a moment Thomas could not remember how to tie a lace. His fingers pulled one end through, lost it, began again. Finch sat opposite, chewing something from a ration tin. He offered Thomas a piece of biscuit, and Thomas accepted because refusing would require speech. It broke against his teeth with a noise that seemed disgracefully loud.

“Your socks,” Thomas said.

“Keep wearing them. Easier than carrying you.”

Thomas tried to smile. He folded his own damp pair into his pack, then checked that the diary was inside his tunic. His sister had told him to keep it out of the rain. She was at home, beyond the railways and the ruined village, probably sleeping beneath the quilt he remembered from her room. He pressed the book flat against his chest and fastened the button over it.

Mercer ducked through the entrance. Thomas followed him up the slippery steps and out into the trench, where the air smelled of wet sacks and extinguished fires. Men stood shoulder to shoulder along the duckboards. Somewhere ahead, a voice passed an instruction backward; each man repeated it to the next, quietly, as though the dark itself must not hear. Thomas moved after Mercer around a timbered corner and stopped beneath a ladder leaning against the parapet.

The sergeant passed close enough for Thomas to see rain caught in his moustache. He touched a man’s equipment here, shifted another man out of the passage there. Nothing in his movements suggested a charge across open ground. He might have been arranging passengers for a crowded train. Thomas found this reassuring until he saw the sergeant glance at his watch.

“Bayonet,” Mercer said.

Thomas obeyed. The metal would not seat properly, and Mercer steadied the rifle while he tried again. This time it caught. Thomas wanted to apologize, but Mercer had turned toward the ladder.

Then the guns began.

The first concussion struck through Thomas’s ribs. Others followed so closely that he could no longer separate them. Earth sifted from the trench wall onto his sleeve. Across the opening overhead, the darkness flashed pale and red, showing a tangle of roots protruding from the parapet. He had imagined that battle would make him want to shout. Instead he wanted everybody to stop moving so that he could understand what was happening.

Mercer bent close to his ear.

“When I go, follow. Watch your footing.”

Thomas nodded. He had been told this already; now it was the only instruction he could hold.

The whistle scarcely reached him through the bombardment. Mercer climbed. Thomas put a boot on the bottom rung, found the next with his knee, and scrambled upward with his rifle awkward across his arms. A hand shoved beneath his pack. He came over the sandbags onto ground that yielded under both boots.

There was no wide, level field. There were ridges of thrown earth, holes holding scraps of sky, stakes bent like broken fingers. Mercer was several paces ahead. Thomas followed the pale patch of mud on his coat.

A flare opened above them. For an instant the wire ahead glittered with drops, just as it had outside the dugout. Thomas saw each strand distinctly, saw a strip of cloth stirring from one barb. The light was beautiful enough to make him stare. Mercer caught his sleeve and pulled him down into a shallow depression.

Something snapped past. Dirt struck Thomas’s cheek.

“Not there,” Mercer said, pointing. “We go round.”

Thomas could not tell whether he had nodded. His mouth was full of biscuit dust though he had swallowed it long ago. To their left a man stumbled, recovered, and vanished behind a rising sheet of earth. Thomas flinched so hard his helmet slipped over one eyebrow.

Mercer was moving again.

Thomas pushed up, took three steps, and caught his boot in a loop of buried wire. He fell against the rim of a shell hole. His rifle slid forward; he seized its sling and felt the wire tighten around his ankle. Panic made him pull harder.

Mercer crouched beside him.

“Back first. Like the mud.”

The words reached him. Thomas bent his knee, worked his heel backward, and freed the boot. Mercer gripped his coat while he found ground firm enough to stand on. Neither spoke. Thomas stayed close behind him as they skirted the hole and descended into another, deeper crater.

Finch was already there, pressed against its muddy wall.

“Still got my socks?”

Thomas let out a breath that shook all the way through him. He nodded, then crouched beside Finch as Mercer peered over the rim. His hands were trembling, but he kept hold of the rifle. When Mercer reached back, Thomas took his wrist and helped brace him against the slippery slope.


Mercer’s weight drew Thomas forward until his knees slid in the mud. He dug his heels against a buried stone and tightened his grip. Above Mercer’s shoulder, the rim of the crater brightened and went black again. Thomas could see nothing of the ground beyond it.

“Can’t go straight,” Mercer said, lowering himself. “Wire’s still up.”

Finch wiped mud from his mouth. “Left?”

“Looks broken there. There’s a ditch beyond.”

Thomas waited for more: how far, how they would recognize it, what they should do when they arrived. Mercer only eased his wrist out of Thomas’s grasp and checked the slope behind Finch. The attack, which had begun with a whistle and a ladder, now depended on finding somewhere to put a boot.

A shell burst close enough to throw loose earth over the rim. Thomas hunched beneath his rifle. Something heavy struck the water at the bottom of the hole, splashing his trousers, and he remained bent after the noise had passed.

Finch touched his shoulder. “Still here.”

Thomas lifted his head. Finch had a cut beneath his lip, a bright thread of blood slipping through the dirt. He dabbed it with his sleeve.

“You’re bleeding.”

“Bit my lip. Should have kept my mouth shut.”

Mercer pointed toward a low place in the crater’s side. “We climb there. Left round the broken post, then down. Stay close.”

Thomas looked where he pointed. The slope seemed too steep, but Finch went first, driving his toes into the wet earth. Mercer followed and reached back. Thomas passed his rifle up, then caught Mercer’s hand. For a moment he hung with one knee scrabbling uselessly. Finch seized the back of his coat, and together they hauled him onto the lip.

He flattened himself beside them. Ahead, smoke dragged low across the ground. A post leaned out of it, trailing wire. Farther away, men moved in fragments: a bent back, a lifted arm, boots disappearing behind a mound. Thomas could not tell whether they were advancing or returning.

Mercer crawled. Thomas followed so closely that he nearly struck Mercer’s boot with his chin. The mud smelled newly opened, like the ditch behind his sister’s garden after heavy rain. He tried to keep his rifle clear of it. His elbows sank; each time he pulled one free, cold water filled the hollow.

Beside the broken post lay a man on his side.

Thomas saw the hand first. It rested palm upward in a little pool, the fingers curled as though sheltering something. The sleeve was the same color as his own.

He stopped. Mercer turned, caught his gaze, then crawled back far enough to reach the man. He touched his neck beneath the jaw and waited.

“Can we—” Thomas began.

Mercer shook his head.

Thomas looked for a wound and could not see one. That made it harder to leave him. There ought to have been something unmistakable, something that explained why this man remained while they went on.

“Thomas.” Mercer’s hand closed briefly on his sleeve. “Come.”

He crawled past the outstretched fingers, keeping his knee clear of them.

Beyond the post, the ground fell into a narrow drainage cut, its sides broken by shellfire. Finch slipped down first. Thomas lowered himself after him and landed awkwardly in water above his ankles. He turned at once, reaching up before Mercer asked. Mercer’s boot skidded on the edge; Thomas caught his forearm while Finch steadied his pack. They brought him down between them.

For several breaths they stood pressed together in the ditch. Thomas’s hands ached from gripping. Mercer squeezed his arm once, then moved along the cut, crouching beneath its ragged bank.

The ditch brought them to torn sandbags and a gap in a trench wall. Finch peered through, called something Thomas could not distinguish, then beckoned. Thomas stepped over a splintered board and followed him inside.

Men from their own attack crowded the traverse. One knelt with a bandage between his teeth; another leaned against the wall, working the bolt of his rifle. A voice farther along called for ammunition. Thomas stared at a discarded mug lying on its side beneath a firing step. Someone had been drinking here.

“Keep the opening clear,” Mercer told him.

Thomas moved aside. When Finch stumbled back toward the gap as Mercer came through, Thomas caught his elbow and held him until he found his feet.

“Obliged,” Finch said. He looked down at the water streaming from Thomas’s boots. “Those socks will need washing.”

“I’ll wash them.”

“Hold you to it.”

A faint grayness had begun to gather above the trench. In it, Thomas noticed a strand of grass growing from a collapsed sandbag, bowed beneath three drops of water. The drops trembled at every concussion and held.

Mercer crouched beside him. “We’re across the wire. Stay here while I see who’s in charge.”

Thomas nodded. He set his rifle against his shoulder and watched the gap they had entered through. When another man appeared there, struggling with the broken board, Thomas reached out his hand.


The man at the gap had no helmet, and his face was gray with mud. Thomas caught his sleeve before he fell against the broken board. The man’s rifle dragged behind him, its bayonet buried in the earth.

“Easy,” Thomas said, though his own voice sounded no steadier. “There’s room.”

The man stared at him as if Thomas had made the trench wider by speaking. Thomas pulled him through and guided him against the wall. Another soldier followed, then another, each arriving bent nearly double. The opening could admit only one at a time, and the men behind pressed forward with the blind urgency of people who had been running from something larger than themselves.

“Not all at once,” Thomas called. “Let him through.”

He put a hand against the next man’s chest and held him back until the first had found a place on the firing step. It was not an order he had been taught to give, but the man obeyed. Thomas felt the first small change in himself there: fear had not gone, but it had been joined by a task. For three breaths he could think of nothing except keeping the passage open.

Finch came to his side, one hand pressed to his cut lip. “You’ve got the manner of a railway porter.”

“Then stop blocking the platform.”

Finch gave a crooked smile and shifted down the traverse. Together they made a channel between the men crowding in and those trying to reach the forward end. Finch tapped shoulders, pointed to gaps, and caught a rifle before its owner dropped it. He and Thomas did not need to discuss where each should stand. When one moved, the other made space.

Mercer returned through the smoke with a sergeant behind him. The sergeant’s sleeve was torn and his whistle hung by a thread, but his voice carried through the concussions.

“Who came in with you?”

“Second platoon, mostly,” Mercer said. “We crossed at the broken post. There may be more in the ditch.”

“Then we bring them in if they can walk. The line ahead is holding, but the left has no cartridges.”

The sergeant pointed along the traverse. A narrow communication trench led away beneath a sagging timber, its floor hidden under brown water and scattered equipment. Beyond it, men were passing ammunition from hand to hand.

“Mercer, take two and get boxes from the reserve bay. Find out whether the left section is still occupied.”

Mercer looked at Thomas and Finch. “These two.”

Thomas felt his stomach contract. He had expected Mercer to remain beside him, not to give him a direction that carried him away from the shelter of the traverse. Yet the order was simple. He checked his rifle, tightened the strap of his pack, and looked at Finch’s injured mouth.

“You able?”

Finch spat a thread of blood into the mud. “I’m able enough to owe you a pair of socks.”

“Then stay behind me where the boards are sound.”

They followed Mercer into the communication trench. The timber above them shuddered as a shell landed somewhere beyond the parapet. Water leaped from the floor and struck Thomas’s knees. He put one hand against the wall and moved carefully, testing each plank before trusting it. Behind him Finch’s boots made a dragging sound, but he did not stop.

At the first bend, a stretcher blocked half the passage. Two men carrying it backed toward them, faces pinched with effort. Thomas flattened himself against the wall and pulled Finch close by the sleeve. The stretcher passed so near that its canvas brushed Thomas’s coat. Beneath the covering, a hand dangled and swung with each step. Thomas watched it until the bend swallowed it.

“Keep going,” Mercer said.

The trench climbed toward a dugout whose entrance had been partly buried. Men crouched outside it, sorting ammunition boxes from a heap of fallen sandbags. Mercer seized two boxes and passed one to Thomas. It was heavier than he expected. The handles cut into his fingers.

“Left section,” Mercer said. “We take these forward, then find whoever’s there.”

Thomas lifted the box against his hip. The weight pulled him sideways, but Finch caught the other handle.

“I’ve got this end,” Finch said.

They carried it together, stepping through the water while Mercer led them beneath the low timber. The passage opened briefly to the sky. Smoke thinned above the parapet, and dawn showed itself as a color rather than a light: a pale green-gray behind torn clouds. On the broken sandbags, tiny flowers had survived the bombardment, their heads powdered with dust. Thomas saw them as he passed and thought of the grass by his sister’s gate, where she used to leave a cup of water for bees.

Then the guns spoke again, and the flowers vanished beneath flying earth.

Mercer dropped behind the parapet and waved them down. Thomas lowered the box beside him. Across the churned ground, a line of rifles rose and disappeared through smoke.

“Left section!” Mercer shouted.

For a moment there was no answer. Then someone called from beyond a collapsed traverse, “Here!”

Mercer pointed. “We go to them. Stay low.”

Thomas gripped the box handles again. Finch took the other side. They climbed over the broken sandbags and moved along the trench, carrying ammunition toward the voice, while behind them the opening remained clear for those still coming in.


The voice came again, nearer this time, though the collapsed traverse made it seem to rise from beneath the earth. “Ammunition! For God’s sake, ammunition!”

Thomas and Finch lowered the box long enough to crawl beneath a slant of timber. The passage had narrowed to the width of their shoulders. Thomas went first, dragging the box by its rope handle. Splinters caught in his sleeve. Behind him, Finch breathed through his teeth, and the box bumped against his knees each time the trench shook. Mercer followed with his rifle held crosswise, scraping mud from the wall.

They emerged into the left section and found it occupied after all. Six men crouched along the firing step, their rifles laid across the sandbags, while two others worked at a machine gun whose tripod had sunk into the mire. The parapet had been torn open in three places. Through the gaps Thomas saw a pale stretch of blasted ground and, beyond it, the wavering black line of the enemy wire. No officer stood among the men. A corporal with a strip of cloth tied around his arm looked up from an empty ammunition tin.

“You took your time,” he said.

“Line ahead was holding,” Mercer answered. “We were told this section had gone quiet.”

“It went quiet because we ran out.” The corporal pointed to the ammunition box. “Set it there.”

Thomas and Finch hauled the box onto the firing step. The corporal knelt and broke the lid with the butt of his revolver. Inside, the cartridges shone in their paper packets, absurdly clean after the mud. The men reached for them at once, and Thomas passed packets from hand to hand until his fingers moved faster than his thoughts. One soldier tore the paper with his teeth. Another began loading magazines against his knee.

A shell struck somewhere beyond the traverse. The concussion pressed Thomas down until his cheek met the wet sandbag. Dust sifted over his helmet. For an instant the section disappeared in a brown cloud, and he heard Finch shouting his name from close beside him. Thomas answered, then reached toward the sound. His hand found Finch’s wrist, slick and trembling.

“Still here,” Finch said.

Thomas pulled him upright. “Stay with Mercer.”

“I’m not going anywhere without the socks.”

The joke was thin, but it held them together. Thomas saw that Finch’s cut had opened again, blood bright against the gray mud, yet Finch was already feeding cartridges to the nearest rifleman. Mercer had moved to the corporal and was studying the broken parapet.

“How many on the left?” Mercer asked.

“Those you see, and three in the shell hole beyond the traverse. One’s hurt bad. We’ve been waiting for a runner.”

“No runner will get through that gap.” Mercer pointed toward the rear, where the collapsed timber partly blocked the communication trench. “We need the section linked to the center.”

The corporal glanced at Thomas, then at the open passage. “Can you get back?”

Thomas looked behind him. Smoke poured through the gap, turning the trench into a gray throat. He remembered the men still arriving at the entry opening, the stretcher, the flooded boards, and the sergeant’s order to keep the line supplied. The route was dangerous, but it existed. More importantly, the left section was not lost; it was merely cut off and nearly empty.

“I can,” Thomas said.

Mercer turned sharply. “You’ll take word to the sergeant. Tell him the left is occupied, six men here, three beyond the traverse, and the ammunition has arrived. Ask for a carrying party and a stretcher.”

Thomas felt fear rise before the order had finished. He wanted Mercer to send someone older, someone who knew how to move through a trench without imagining every shadow as a rifle muzzle. Instead, Mercer pressed a packet of cartridges into his palm.

“Give this to the sergeant so he knows you came from here.”

Thomas closed his fingers around it. The small packet was warm from Mercer’s hand. He nodded, then crouched beside Finch.

“You coming?”

Finch’s face tightened as another explosion rolled overhead. “You think I’m letting you have all the work?”

Together they ducked into the smoke-filled passage. Thomas went first, carrying the message and keeping one hand against the wall to guide him. Behind him Finch followed with his rifle and the promise of borrowed socks still hanging between them like a thread. Thomas no longer thought of performing properly or proving that he belonged. There was only the occupied section behind him, the sergeant ahead, and the narrow route that had to remain open. He moved toward the rear as the guns thundered, advancing the small, necessary distance on which the rest of the line depended.


The smoke-filled passage narrowed where the wall had slumped inward. Thomas turned sideways and pushed through, keeping the cartridge packet tucked beneath his coat. The earth was warm against his knuckles. Each concussion shook loose grains that pattered over his helmet and filled his collar. Behind him, Finch coughed once, then caught the wall with his free hand.

“Still there?” Thomas called.

“Unfortunately.”

The answer came close enough to steady him. Thomas felt along the trench with his boots, finding the boards by pressure rather than sight. Twice his foot sank into water up to the ankle. Once it struck something hollow, and a voice beneath the floor groaned. He stopped, but Finch seized the back of his coat.

“Don’t,” Finch whispered. “We can’t open that.”

Thomas knew what he meant. The voice belonged to a man trapped under the duckboards or buried in the mud, and any attempt to reach him would cost minutes the left section did not have. Thomas moved on, carrying the groan with him. It seemed to travel beneath the boards, keeping pace.

At the next bend, a blast blew smoke toward them so thickly that the world became the taste of charcoal. Thomas crouched and waited until the air thinned. Through it he saw a figure crawling toward them, helmetless, dragging a rifle by its sling. The soldier stopped when he saw Thomas and tried to rise.

“Can you walk?” Thomas asked.

The man nodded, though his knees folded when he tried. Finch leaned his rifle against the wall and caught the soldier under one arm. Thomas took the other. Together they hauled him as far as the bend, where two men from the center trench reached out and pulled him away.

“Left section,” Thomas said. “They need a stretcher.”

One of the men shouted that he had heard him. The other was already calling for a medic. Thomas did not wait to see whether anyone answered. He and Finch pushed onward, boots slipping as the passage began to climb. The air changed near the rear: less smoke, more cordite, wet wool, and the sour smell of men who had been awake too long. Somewhere ahead, a whistle shrilled three times, and voices answered from the dugouts.

The communication trench opened into the front-line trench beneath a sagging timber frame. Thomas climbed over a fallen sandbag and nearly collided with the sergeant. He was kneeling beside a crate, speaking to a runner whose cheek was wrapped in a blood-darkened bandage. The sergeant’s gaze went first to Thomas’s face, then to Finch’s reopened cut, and finally to the hand Thomas held against his chest.

“Where’s Mercer?”

“Left section.” Thomas bent, trying to draw breath without coughing. “Occupied. Six men in the position, three beyond the collapsed traverse. One badly wounded. They have ammunition now, but the passage is blocked and the section is cut off from the center.”

The sergeant’s expression tightened. “Mercer sent you?”

Thomas brought out the cartridge packet. “He said this would prove it. He wants a carrying party and a stretcher.”

The sergeant took the packet, turned it over once, and thrust it into his pocket. Then he stood and raised his voice above the guns.

“Two men with a stretcher! Four carriers for the left! Find the medic and tell him there’s a wounded man beyond the traverse!”

A corporal near the dugout repeated the orders. The words moved down the trench, changing hands as they went. Four soldiers began pulling equipment from a stack of abandoned packs. One unfolded a stretcher whose canvas was stiff with mud. Thomas watched them for a moment, relieved by the sight of something being made ready, then realized the sergeant was looking at him.

“You’re going back.”

It was not a question. Thomas glanced at Finch. His friend had retrieved his rifle and was pressing a strip of cloth to his mouth.

“I can take him,” Finch said.

“You can take yourself,” the sergeant replied. “Stay here and get that wound tied.”

Finch opened his mouth, but Thomas shook his head. “You heard him.”

The words sounded strange coming from him. Earlier, he might have apologized for them. Now he was already turning toward the passage.

The sergeant caught his sleeve. “You know the route?”

“By the damaged traverse, then under the leaning timber. The left section is past the collapsed boards. The corporal there is holding the men together.”

“And Mercer?”

“With them.”

“Tell him the party is coming. If the passage is blocked, mark the turn with a strip of white cloth.”

Thomas nodded. He took a torn bandage from the sergeant’s hand and shoved it at Finch. “Wrap your mouth.”

Finch accepted it, eyes narrowed with irritation but not argument. Behind them, the stretcher party formed in the trench, one man carrying a coil of rope, another a lantern shuttered beneath his greatcoat. The sight of them made the distance ahead seem possible, though the guns continued to hammer at the earth between.

Thomas stepped back into the communication trench. This time he did not grope blindly. He pointed out the flooded boards, the sunken patch, and the place where the trapped voice had sounded. Finch followed close behind, warning the carriers where to duck. Ahead, the smoke gathered under the timbers, hiding the route to Mercer and the wounded man. Thomas tightened his grip on his rifle and went forward to lead them through.


The stretcher struck the wall behind Thomas with a wooden knock. He stopped and raised his hand, though he could scarcely see whether the men behind him noticed. A shell passed overhead with a tearing sound; everyone folded against the trench sides. Earth showered into the water around their boots. When Thomas lifted his head, Finch was beside him, holding the loose end of the bandage against his cheek.

“You’re meant to be back there,” Thomas said.

“Only came to show them the boards.”

“You’ve shown them.”

Finch looked past him into the smoke. Thomas understood the hesitation because he felt it himself: leaving a friend seemed different from obeying an order, even when both required the same steps. He touched Finch’s sleeve.

“Get it tied properly. We’ll need you carrying when we come back.”

Finch pulled the bandage away long enough to answer. “You’d better come back, then.” He tore a loose tail from the cloth and pushed it into Thomas’s hand. “For your turn.”

Thomas watched until Finch had passed the last carrier and disappeared toward the center. Then he tucked the strip beneath his cuff and moved forward. Behind him came the two stretcher-bearers, four carriers, and the medic, who kept one hand over his satchel as though someone might steal it. The sergeant had sent them. Thomas had only to get them there. He repeated that distinction whenever their waiting footsteps made him feel responsible for more than he knew how to do.

At the sunken patch he slowed. The water lay almost still, filmed with dust, and for a moment he could not distinguish the broken boards from their reflections. He tested the edge with his boot.

“Against this wall,” he said. “One at a time.”

A faint scraping answered beneath him.

Thomas crouched before he could decide not to. Between two displaced planks, muddy water trembled around something pale. Fingers, he thought; then they moved, and he knew.

“There’s a man under here.”

The medic squeezed past a bearer and knelt. He put his face close to the opening. “Can you hear me?”

The fingers closed against a plank. Thomas felt relief so sudden that it hurt, followed by shame: he had passed this place before and left him there. He wanted to explain about Mercer and the ammunition, but the medic was working a hand through the gap.

“Don’t pull the boards,” the medic said. “That timber’s resting on them.”

One carrier lowered himself beside him. Together they scraped mud from around the opening until Thomas could see part of a sleeve. The man beneath made a breathless, broken noise.

“We need a lever and room to lift,” the medic said. He looked up at Thomas. “How far to the other casualty?”

“Past the leaning timber. Through the left section.”

“Then get the stretcher there. I’ll stay long enough to see what’s pinning this one.”

Thomas stared at him. The party had been fetched for the man beyond the traverse; now the passage itself was taking it apart. The medic caught his wrist.

“I can’t treat someone I can’t reach. Bring Mercer word where I am.”

Thomas nodded. One carrier remained to help him. The others lifted their loads again, and Thomas stepped carefully past the opening, leaving a voice where there had been only a groan.

At the damaged turn, fresh earth had fallen across the direct passage. He remembered squeezing through it with Finch, remembered the scrape against his shoulder; now a splintered support lay where his head had been. He stood still, trying to fit the changed ground to the route in his memory.

“Which way?” a bearer asked.

Thomas lifted the shutter of the lantern. A weak yellow light revealed a narrow drainage cut beside the collapse, its bottom lost under water. He edged into it sideways. Three steps brought him around the fallen support and back onto boards.

“Here. Keep the stretcher upright.”

He tied Finch’s strip to a projecting timber above the blocked turn. In the lantern light the cloth showed astonishingly white. It stirred in the disturbed air, and for an instant he remembered washing on the line at home, his sister reaching up on her toes to catch a sleeve. Then a carrier brushed past, and Thomas lowered the shutter.

They worked the stretcher through lengthwise. Thomas took its forward handle while the bearer climbed over a buried sandbag, then surrendered it and crawled beneath the leaning timber. Rifle fire sharpened ahead. He emerged into the left section with mud down his neck and called for Mercer.

Mercer turned from the parapet. “Where’s Finch?”

“Back getting bandaged. The sergeant sent relief. Stretcher, carriers. The medic’s stopped at a trapped man in the passage.”

“And the route?”

“Blocked at the turn. I marked it. There’s a way beside it.”

Mercer gripped his shoulder, briefly and hard, then beckoned the bearers forward. The corporal pointed beyond the traverse toward the shell hole. Thomas pressed against the wall to let the stretcher pass. He had delivered the message and brought the men through, but when Mercer ducked toward the wounded man, Thomas followed, reaching out to steady the rear handle over the broken boards.


The rear handle bucked against Thomas’s palm as the bearer ahead stepped down beyond the traverse. He tightened his grip before the wood could strike the wounded man’s boot. Below them, the shell hole held a shallow skin of water, trembling with each concussion. Mercer crouched beside the casualty, one knee planted in the mud, and pulled back a coat that someone had spread over him.

“Bring it level,” Mercer said. “Not down into the water.”

Thomas shifted his feet along the broken boards. The wounded man lay wedged against the slope, his face turned toward the trench as though he had been listening for their arrival. Mud obscured his collar and one side of his mouth. His eyes followed the stretcher, then settled on Thomas.

“Have you got room?”

“Yes,” Thomas said.

It was the first answer that came, and he was grateful it was true.

They laid the stretcher across the driest ground they could find. One bearer knelt to examine the dressing beneath the man’s coat while Mercer supported his shoulders. Thomas waited with his hands open, uncertain where they could safely touch. The bandage was dark through its middle. He remembered how carefully he had once handled a bird with a broken wing, afraid that the pressure of a finger might be enough to kill it. This man was heavier than any kindness Thomas knew how to offer.

“Under his hips,” the bearer told him. “Keep him straight. Let us do the lifting.”

Thomas slid his hands beneath sodden cloth. At the count they moved together. The casualty cried out, a short, furious sound that stopped when his breath failed. Thomas nearly loosened his hold, but Mercer said, “Steady,” and he held until the bearer told him to withdraw. They settled the man onto the canvas. Thomas remained bent over him a moment longer, fingers curled around nothing.

A shell burst beyond the parapet. Something struck the outer face of the traverse with a heavy slap. Thomas ducked over the stretcher without thinking; when he straightened, the wounded man had caught his sleeve.

“Don’t leave that.”

Thomas followed his gaze to a cap half buried beside the water. He retrieved it and tucked it under the man’s hand. Inside its torn lining, someone had stitched a small square of blue cloth. He looked away before he could begin wondering who had sewn it.

Mercer checked the straps. “Take him back to the medic. Tell him we need this passage kept open.”

Thomas nodded. “He’s still at the man under the boards.”

“Then he’ll see you coming.”

They lifted. Thomas took the forward bearer’s rifle so that both the man’s hands were free, slinging it awkwardly beside his own. He climbed out ahead of them, turned beneath the leaning timber, and held back a hanging length of wire while the stretcher passed. Behind him, Mercer was already calling to the corporal. Thomas could no longer distinguish the words once they rounded the traverse.

The return seemed narrower. Where he had led men carrying an empty frame, he now had to judge the width of a shoulder, the rise of a boot, the space a dangling hand might need. At the drainage cut he stopped them before the forward bearer entered.

“We turned it on its edge coming through,” the man said.

Thomas looked at the casualty, then at the flooded slot beside the fallen support. Finch’s strip hung above it, streaked now with dirt.

“We’ll have to clear more.”

One of the carriers set down his load and joined him. They pulled loose sandbags away from the mouth of the cut, dragging rather than lifting them. A split plank caught beneath the last bag. Thomas worked it free, tearing his thumbnail against a nail. He put the thumb into his mouth, tasted mud and blood, then bent to the plank again.

Together they laid it over the softest stretch. The bearers tested it with their boots before lifting the stretcher. Thomas went backward through the cut, watching the forward handle, warning them when the casualty’s elbow came close to the wall. White cloth brushed his helmet. He heard himself saying, “A little higher. That’s it,” in a voice almost as ordinary as if they were moving furniture through a doorway.

Beyond the blockage, the medic’s lantern showed beneath a coat. The man left to help him was still kneeling at the displaced boards. Beside his boots, a muddy hand gripped the edge of the opening.

“Here,” Thomas called. “We’ve brought him.”

The medic looked up. “Set him against the wall. Leave me space.”

The bearers lowered their burden where he pointed. Thomas delivered Mercer’s message while the medic opened his satchel. Then he crouched beside the carrier at the broken floor.

“Still pinned?” he asked.

The carrier nodded toward the timber. Thomas put down the rifles and reached for the lantern. Its light caught water dripping from the boards, each drop briefly bright before it fell into darkness. Beneath them, the trapped man tightened his grip.

“We’ve got the stretcher through,” Thomas told him. “We’re here.”

He held the light steady while the carrier searched for somewhere sound to place the lever.


I held the lantern while the carrier crawled along the broken floor, testing the boards with the butt of his rifle. Each touch sent a soft complaint through the timber. Beneath us, the trapped man breathed in short, wet pulls. I could hear him trying not to cry out.

“Can you move your legs?” I asked.

“Not my left,” he answered. His voice sounded close enough to touch, though the boards between us made it seem to come from the earth itself. “My arm’s free.”

“Keep it there. We’ll lift on three.”

The carrier found a length of iron half buried in the mud and wedged it beneath the fallen timber. I set the lantern on a dry ledge, then dropped to my knees beside him. The medic shouted from behind us that the wounded man needed pressure held against his side, but I could not turn to look. The trapped soldier’s fingers were wrapped around one splintered edge, and I had begun to think of that hand as a promise we had made.

“Not yet,” the carrier said. “The lever’ll slip.”

I swept mud away with both hands. The passage stank of wet earth, smoke, and the sharp copper smell of blood. My fingers struck something solid beneath the timber—a crate, perhaps, or a length of buried support. I dug around it until I could see the corner of a wooden ammunition box. Its lid had split, and cartridges glimmered in the mud like dull little teeth.

“That’s what’s holding it,” I said. “We need it out.”

The carrier shook his head. “It’s under the weight.”

“Then we take the weight first.”

I crawled farther into the drainage cut and pressed my shoulder against the timber. The wood was cold through my tunic. I remembered Finch telling me that a man did not have to be strong if he could find where the strength belonged. At the time I had thought it was one of his jokes. Now I shifted lower, braced my boots against the wall, and called for the carrier to lift the lever.

The timber rose a fraction. Mud squeezed into the gap. Beneath it, the trapped man gave a strangled gasp.

“Hold it!”

I reached in and dragged at the ammunition box. It caught on something below. I pulled again, feeling the wood tear my palms. The box slid free with a sucking sound and struck my chest. Loose cartridges rolled everywhere, clicking against the boards. The pressure above changed at once.

“Now!” the carrier shouted.

We lifted together. The beam came up far enough for the trapped man to pull his arm toward himself. I seized his wrist, and another hand closed around mine. His grip was astonishingly hard. I hooked my other arm beneath his shoulder and dragged while the carrier levered the timber higher. The man’s coat snagged on a nail. For one terrible second he hung between us and the wreckage.

“Leave the coat,” I said.

He did. We pulled him through with a tearing sound, and he collapsed against my knees. One boot remained beneath the timber, twisted at an angle that made my stomach turn. The carrier lowered the beam only after I had shifted the man clear. Dust fell from the ceiling and settled across his hair.

The medic came crawling toward us, his sleeves dark to the elbows. He examined the trapped man’s leg, then pressed two fingers against his throat.

“Alive,” he said. “That’s a beginning.”

“Can you move him?”

“Not without a splint. And not through that cut unless you widen it.”

I looked toward the passage behind us. The wounded casualty lay against the wall, the medic’s earlier dressing pressed beneath a carrier’s hands. Beyond him, the way to the center connection was marked by the white strip I had tied there, though it now hung grey at the edges. Through that opening came a confused roar of voices and the distant hammering of guns.

“We can widen it,” I said.

The carrier stared at me. “With what?”

I picked up the ammunition box and turned it over. Its boards were thin but sound. Nearby, the broken lid had left a jagged plank with a nail protruding from one end. I used the nail to pry loose a second piece, then shoved both beneath the softest part of the drainage wall. The earth gave way in heavy clumps. Water rushed around my wrists, cold enough to numb them.

“Keep the lantern low,” I told the carrier. “There’s a support behind this mud.”

We worked without speaking. Each handful made a little more room: first for a shoulder, then for the stretcher’s forward handle, then for the wounded man’s dangling boot. The trapped soldier watched us from the boards, his face pale beneath the mud.

“What’s your name?” I asked him.

He swallowed. “Hale.”

“I’m Thomas. We’re taking you out, Hale.”

He glanced at the wreckage behind him. “You already did.”

The words struck me harder than the shelling. I had thought rescue meant carrying a man into safety, some clean passage between danger and relief. Here it meant keeping a lantern lit, finding a box beneath a beam, and refusing to let a living hand disappear. I widened the cut until my shoulders scraped both sides, then crawled through to the other end and called back.

“Bring the stretcher sideways. The turn is open.”

The bearers lifted the first casualty and carried him toward the center connection. I remained behind with Hale and the carrier. When the stretcher had passed, I returned through the cut, took Hale beneath the arms, and pulled him inch by inch over the boards. He cried out once when his leg moved. I apologized, though there was nothing gentler to offer.

At the blocked turn, Mercer appeared through the smoke, his face streaked with the same mud as ours. He looked from the wounded man to Hale, then at the widened passage.

“You found him.”

“We found the room around him,” I said.

Mercer nodded toward the center. “Get him to the medic. The relief party is waiting beyond the traverse.”

Together we carried Hale beneath the white cloth and into the communication trench, stepping over the loose cartridges scattered like seeds across the floor. Behind us, the broken boards settled with a low groan. Ahead, lanterns moved through the smoke, and the men made space without being told.


Hale’s good foot dragged between mine as we reached the medic. I could no longer feel my hands beneath his arms. The carrier took more of his weight, and together we lowered him onto a groundsheet spread over the duckboards. Above us, a shell passed with the sound of cloth tearing slowly.

“Not there—under cover,” the medic said.

We lifted again. Three paces brought us beneath the reinforced lip of a dugout, where the lantern flame scarcely moved. Those three paces seemed the longest part of the journey. When we set Hale down, he caught my wrist.

“Tell me before you move it.”

“I will.”

The medic cut away the trouser leg. I watched Hale’s face instead of the injury while the carrier fetched splinting boards. Across the entrance, the first casualty lay on his stretcher, a bearer still holding the dressing against his side. His cap rested beneath his hand. Whenever the guns sounded close, his fingers tightened around its brim.

“Keep that pressure,” the medic told the bearer. “You’re doing right.”

I knelt at Hale’s shoulder while they secured his leg. Before each movement I warned him, and each time he nodded as though granting permission for something neither of us could prevent. When it was done, he loosened his grip. Four pale marks remained above my cuff.

“Thomas,” he said, testing whether he remembered correctly.

“Yes.”

“Don’t lose my boot.”

I looked at his stockinged foot, then at the dark passage behind us.

“I can’t get it now.”

“Later, then.”

I said nothing. He closed his eyes, and the medic drew a blanket over him. It was the first promise that day I had stopped myself from making.

Once the bearers had both men ready for the aid post, I followed the communication trench back toward Mercer. At the damaged turn, I retied the white cloth higher on its splintered stake. Its loose end flickered against the earth. Beyond the traverse the relief party waited, bent beneath their equipment, faces turned toward every sound.

Mercer stood where I had left him.

“Both with the medic,” I reported. “Hale’s splinted. The other man’s being carried back with pressure on the wound. The relief party is coming through now.”

He made me repeat the condition of the passage, then sent me onward with the report for the sergeant. I went ahead of the first men, pointing out the weakened boards. At the narrowest place, one of them put a hand on my shoulder to steady himself. I waited until he had found his footing.

***

When I walked that communication trench after the armistice, I still tested each board before trusting it. Rain had washed the white strip almost clean. It hung stiffly from the stake, and I left it there; men were still bringing equipment and wounded through the turn.

I climbed the dugout steps into a pale afternoon. Beyond the parapet, water filled the shell holes to their broken rims. A length of wire held drops so evenly spaced that it looked threaded with glass. I had seen it shining like that under flares, too. Looking at it now, I remembered crouching while a wounded man tried to crawl beneath it.

No gun interrupted the thought. Somewhere behind me a shovel scraped, stopped, scraped again.

Finch sat on an ammunition crate near the dugout entrance, his bandaged hand tucked inside his coat. He shifted along to make room.

“Well, Thomas?”

I sat beside him. “I keep waiting.”

“So do I.”

We listened together. After a while he produced half a biscuit from his pocket and broke it against the crate. He gave me the larger piece. I nearly protested, then took it. We had argued about smaller kindnesses when I first arrived.

My diary had swollen so badly that the covers would not meet. I worked the pages apart with my thumbnail and found the entry written before my first engagement.

*Eve of the attack*

*We go before daylight. I am to stay near Mercer, or find Finch if we become separated. I hope I shall do properly. I wish my sister could see me in my equipment, though I would rather she did not know how frightened I am. By tomorrow I ought to know whether I am brave.*

There was a blot beneath the last sentence. I remembered being annoyed with it.

Finch glanced at the book. “Writing home?”

“In a minute.”

I turned to a dry corner near the back.

*After the armistice*

*The firing has stopped. I am still frightened. Hale was alive when they carried him away, with his leg splinted, and the other man had his cap. I wish I had asked his name.*

*I once thought I should have something splendid to tell my sister. I want to sit in the kitchen while she talks. I want to hear someone walking upstairs and know they will come down again.*

*Finch shared his biscuit. We sat outside.*

My pencil stopped. There were men I could not fit onto that small patch of paper. I left space beneath the words, closed the book carefully, and took out my sister’s unfinished letter. Beside me, Finch brushed crumbs from his coat. I asked him to lend me his knee for a writing desk.
