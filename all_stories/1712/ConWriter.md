London arrived at the university observatory with rain in her shoes and a summons folded inside her overdue electricity bill. She had expected an accusation about her scholarship application. Instead, Professor Show opened the door carrying a kettle with no lid.

“Mind the cable,” he said.

She stepped over an orange extension lead. Beyond him, beneath the observatory’s shuttered dome, a metal sphere rested in a cradle of white foam. It was smaller than the washing machine she shared with six other students. Someone had stuck a handwritten notice on it: NOT A BIN.

“That,” Show said, “is why you’re here.”

London kept her coat on. “Have I damaged it?”

“Not yet.”

He put the kettle beside two cups and offered her a chair whose stuffing showed through its split seat. Through the high windows she could see the river, brown and swollen between its walls. On the opposite embankment, a screen displayed the blue sunburst of the Meridian Union. Beneath it, cargo ships moved through an animation of open sea. The Union had begun by rebuilding drowned ports; now its loans paid for half the city’s flood defences, and its emblem appeared even on her scholarship letters.

Show tapped the sphere with one fingernail.

“A prototype housing. The flight vessel leaves on a long solar orbit, with stellar encounters accounted for and correction systems designed to sleep between adjustments. Its homecoming is eight million years away.”

She waited for him to smile.

“Years?”

“Yes.”

“Will there still be a London?”

“Almost certainly not.”

Her own name sounded suddenly absurd: a borrowed city she might outlast by seventy years, attached to a student who had missed breakfast.

Show handed her a thin folder. Inside was a commission bearing the university seal and the Union sunburst.

“We need a message,” he said. “Something engraved, redundant, with a decoding primer. The technical teams handle the primer. You write what we say.”

London read the first line twice. “I study literature.”

“We have sufficient engineers.”

“You have poets. Famous ones.”

“We invited them. Most submitted statements about the generosity of the sponsors.”

“And you want a cheaper statement?”

Show’s hand stopped above the cups. He set one down without pouring.

“I read your essay about the evacuation memorial.”

Her stomach tightened. The essay had cost her a departmental prize. She had written that the bronze rescuers all had faces, while the people they carried were smooth-headed bundles.

“I was told it was ungrateful.”

“You wrote that gratitude becomes dishonest when it forbids grief.”

“Is that why you chose me?”

“Yes.”

From behind a partition came the scrape of a chair. London had assumed they were alone. A man wearing a visitor’s badge emerged, fastening his coat.

“This is precisely the problem,” he said. “You confuse a communications project with a confessional.”

Show’s shoulders stiffened. “We haven’t finished.”

“The launch council has. Nobody funds a monument to human inadequacy.”

London looked down at the commission. A paragraph near the bottom required approval from the Union’s Cultural Secretariat.

“You haven’t actually entrusted me with anything,” she said. “You’ve invited me to audition.”

The visitor gave her a small, almost relieved smile. “A sensible distinction.”

Show took the folder from her, found the paragraph, and drew a line through it.

The man’s smile vanished. “You cannot amend that.”

“I control the university’s contribution.”

“You control a workshop with a leaking roof. The council gave your impossible clockwork a purpose. Try not to mistake indulgence for respect.”

London heard the rain ticking into a bucket behind the partition. Show stood very still.

Then he said, “You called the flood models impossible too.”

The visitor pulled his sleeve straight. “And now the Union builds the barriers. Some of us learned to be practical.”

He left before London could decide whether that was an answer.

Show poured the water. Most of it reached the cups.

“Did they dismiss your models?” she asked.

“Several governments did. The Union bought them later.”

“So they saved your work.”

“Yes.” He pushed a cup towards her. “That doesn’t entitle them to every word you write.”

London studied the crossed-out clause. The Union’s barriers protected her mother’s flat. Its repayment terms had closed the public library where her mother used to work. She could not make either fact cancel the other.

“If I accept,” she said, “I want your amendment countersigned before I give you a draft.”

Show nodded.

“And I want to see the decoding plan. There’s no point writing beautifully to someone who can’t read it.”

For the first time, he smiled. “That can be arranged.”

She took off her wet coat and pulled her notebook from her bag. Show cleared a space beside the sphere, moving washers, a screwdriver, and a plate containing one hardened biscuit.

London wrote: *We were here.*

She stared at it, then crossed it out. It sounded like something scratched into a desk.

Below it she tried again.

*We damaged things we loved. Some of us tried to mend them.*

Show read over her shoulder.

“Some?” he asked.

She held out the pen. “You’re allowed to argue. You’re not allowed to improve our record.”

He left the pen in her hand and sat down beside her.


Show kept his hands on his knees. He had wanted to question the word *some* because it sounded like an acquittal for everyone else, but London’s answer had caught him in a familiar habit: correcting the smallest available thing when the larger thing frightened him. Across the table lay his signature beside a cancellation he had no certainty the council would honour. He could still hear the visitor calling his work impossible clockwork.

London closed her notebook.

“The decoding plan,” she said. “Can I see it now?”

“Yes.” He rose too quickly and knocked his knee against the table. The tea shivered in its cups. “It’s downstairs. Bring your coat. The stairwell isn’t heated.”

He took the amended commission, leaving the notebook untouched. London put it into her bag before following him.

They passed through the partition into the observatory’s narrow service corridor. Show held the fire door while she stepped over the sandbags stacked against its threshold. Below, the stairs smelled of wet plaster. A Union emergency lamp cast a blue sunburst onto the landing, where an old university crest had been removed for rewiring. He had approved that expenditure himself.

“Does everything come with a logo?” London asked.

“Only everything we can afford.”

She glanced back at him. He wished he had not made it sound like a joke.

In the instrument room beneath the dome, Show switched on a bench lamp. Cabinets crowded the walls; on the central table stood a magnifier and a grey sample plate, its surface patterned with grooves. He pulled out a stool for London, then opened the decoding file on the terminal.

“This is the current proposal. Not the publicity version.”

“Good.”

“The first marks establish quantities, then relationships. Repetition, counting, ratios. After that, diagrams of the vessel and the system it left.”

“And after that?”

“Images, paired with vocabulary.”

He heard the weakness as he said it. London leaned towards the screen without touching the plate.

“What tells them an image is an image?”

“The sequence builds towards spatial representation.”

“Show me.”

He scrolled. She watched him pass two diagrams, then raised a finger.

“That one. Why is the person standing beside the circle?”

“To establish scale.”

“How do they know the circle isn’t another kind of person?”

Show stopped scrolling. Above them a pipe clicked as water moved through it. He had sat through three technical reviews in which nobody had asked that question. There had been arguments over engraving tolerances, shielding, storage density: problems whose answers could be entered neatly into a budget.

“They might not,” he said.

London drew the stool closer. “Have you tested this on anyone who wasn’t told what it meant?”

“Not that version.”

“Then we shouldn’t call it a decoding plan yet.”

His first impulse was to defend the team. He opened his mouth, then shut it. He heard no dismissal of their ambition in her questions. She was asking whether their work did what they claimed.

His terminal chimed. A message from the visitor filled the corner of the screen.

*Your deletion has no standing. Supply the student’s text for Secretariat review before further expenditure is authorised.*

London read it from beside him. She took her hand off the table.

Show moved the message into a separate window and turned the screen so she could see his reply.

*No student draft will be supplied under the disputed clause. Please send the amendment to the authorised countersignatory. Technical review will continue.*

He attached the marked commission.

“You could lose the launch,” London said.

“Yes.”

“Don’t say that as though it’s nothing.”

He looked at her then. Rainwater had darkened the cuffs of her jumper; she was holding them away from the sample plate.

“It isn’t nothing. Nor is asking you for honesty and sending your work to someone employed to remove it.”

He sent the message before he could make it more diplomatic. No answer appeared. He printed the correspondence and handed it to her.

“That isn’t the countersignature,” she said.

“No. You still keep your draft.”

She folded the sheet carefully into her bag.

At the terminal, London asked for a blank document. He opened one and surrendered the chair. She typed a heading: *Things we assume the reader already knows.*

Under it she entered: *Pictures. Reading direction. Human bodies. That we mean to communicate.*

“May I add one?” Show asked.

She shifted the keyboard towards him.

*That the vessel has returned where we intended.*

For a moment neither spoke. Eight million years was easy to pronounce beneath a model orbit projected on a lecture-room wall. Here, with rain finding its way through a building barely older than his grandfather, he could not make it feel like a promise.

“We can include what we intended,” London said. “Separate from what we knew.”

Show found an unused sample plate and set it beside her. “We can test a primer without showing anyone your message.”

“Students from other departments. No hints.”

“And record where they get stuck.”

She nodded and opened the first diagram again. This time Show sat beside her with a pencil, waiting while she decided where they ought to begin.


The first diagram looked less like a message than a set of instructions for assembling a cupboard. Lines converged on a bright point, then broke apart into smaller marks. Beneath it, a sequence of repeated bars established quantity, according to the notes in Show’s margin. London read the notes twice, then took the pencil from his fingers.

“You’ve assumed the reader knows which way to turn the plate.”

“The marks are directional.”

“They’re marks. Direction is something we’ve taught ourselves to see.”

Show leaned over the table, close enough that she could smell rain and the bitter coffee he had not finished. He did not reach for the pencil. That small restraint mattered. The visitor had treated her words as material awaiting correction; Show was allowing them to remain inconvenient.

She drew an arrow beside the diagram, then crossed it out.

“An arrow is a picture,” she said. “We’re back where we started.”

“We could define rotation mathematically.”

“Before we define rotation, we need to prove that the reader understands repetition as evidence rather than decoration.”

He nodded once. “Then the primer must demonstrate its own method.”

“Not merely state it.”

London opened a new page in the document. The terminal’s blue light made the room look submerged. Around them, the instrument cabinets stood in rows, their glass doors clouded with age. Somewhere under the floor, a pump came on and laboured against the rising water. The observatory had survived wars, budget cuts, and the Union’s renovations. It might survive their argument. That did not mean the message would.

She typed: *Every claim must be earned by an earlier example.*

Show read it and added: *No symbol should depend on a human convention left unexplained.*

“Good,” London said. “Now the uncomfortable part.”

He waited.

“If the reader is not human, why are we drawing a person at all?”

“Because the message is from us.”

“That answers who speaks, not what the reader needs to know. A stick figure could mean a body, a tool, a species, or a warning.”

“It gives scale.”

“It gives our vanity a shape.”

The words came out sharper than she intended. Show’s face tightened, though he did not defend the figure. He brought up the technical annotations instead and enlarged a section of the plate.

“The figure is paired with measurements,” he said. “Height, limb proportion, atmospheric requirements.”

“And what if the reader has no limbs? What if it thinks in fields or pressure changes? We keep designing a welcome mat for an intelligence that may not have feet.”

“We can’t make the message universal.”

“No. But we can stop calling our preferences universal.”

A message indicator blinked in the corner of the screen. London expected another demand from the Secretariat. Show clicked it open. It was only a system notice: expenditure authorisation remained suspended pending receipt of the countersigned amendment.

The line seemed almost comic beside the eight-million-year schedule. A project intended to outlast continents was halted by a missing signature. Yet London understood the logic. People who could not trust one another with a paragraph were being asked to trust one another with time itself.

“Will they sign it?” she asked.

“I don’t know.”

“And if they don’t?”

“I keep refusing to send your draft.”

“That could end the launch.”

“Yes.”

She looked at him, searching for the usual institutional evasion—the phrase *regrettable consequences*, the claim that someone higher up would decide. Instead he sat with both hands flat on the table, beside the sample plate and the printed correspondence.

“Then we need a version of the work that can continue without pretending the dispute is settled,” she said.

Show’s eyebrows lifted slightly.

“We separate the primer from my message. We test the primer on people who don’t know what it is supposed to say. We record every failure. If the council asks what we’ve achieved, you give them evidence, not reassurance.”

“And if they ask for the draft?”

“They still don’t get it without the countersignature.”

He turned the printed amendment over and wrote a heading on the back: *Interim protocol*. London watched him form the letters. His handwriting was cramped but steady.

“Students from other departments,” she said. “Not astronomers. Not linguists who’ve been briefed. People who will bring their own assumptions.”

“We can recruit from the library.”

“No invitations explaining the purpose.”

“They’ll need consent.”

“Consent, yes. Hints, no. Tell them they’re evaluating an unfamiliar symbolic sequence. Nothing about a vessel, a return, or a message from Earth.”

Show added the conditions beneath his heading. “And we don’t show them any wording from your draft.”

“Not even a sentence.”

“Agreed.”

The agreement settled between them more firmly than the first one had. London felt the change—not friendship exactly, and not forgiveness, but a practical trust built from boundaries observed. Show had not surrendered her work to preserve the launch. She could now help him preserve the work without surrendering it herself.

He printed the revised protocol. The machine shuddered and produced three pages, each marked with the university seal and the Union sunburst. London took a pen and drew a line through the sponsor’s emblem on the first page.

Show glanced at it.

“They’ll say the Union paid for the paper.”

“Then let them invoice me.”

His mouth twitched. “That may be the first financially irresponsible thing you’ve said tonight.”

“Give me time.”

She carried the pages to the far end of the instrument room, where a corkboard hung beneath a dead clock. On it were old orbit calculations, a photograph of the dome before the flood barriers, and a faded notice about a lecture on impossible machines. London pinned the protocol beside the notice.

For a moment the two papers touched: one promise of a future test, one accusation from the past. Outside, water moved along the gutters and the Meridian screens shone over the river, announcing another completed barrier as though completion were the same as redemption.

London returned to the terminal and reopened the first diagram.

“Start with the simplest sequence,” she said. “No person. No vessel. No Earth.”

Show brought up a blank plate template.

“What do we teach them first?”

“That we may be wrong.”

He looked at her.

“Put it at the top,” London said. “If the primer cannot admit uncertainty, it isn’t teaching a reader. It’s giving orders.”


Show typed *We may be wrong* above the empty plate template, then stopped with his fingers resting on the keys. The sentence looked reassuringly modest in English. Engraved without explanation, it would be as inaccessible as the claims it was meant to qualify.

“That belongs in our instructions to ourselves,” he said. “Until we can demonstrate uncertainty.”

London pulled the protocol towards her. “Then don’t engrave an apology and call it a method.”

He moved the sentence into the working notes. Under it he drew two sequences: one repeated faithfully, one interrupted, then resumed. Already he could feel himself inventing an obedient reader who would understand the interruption exactly as he intended. He saved both as candidates rather than examples approved for testing.

Behind them, the printer stirred. Show turned sharply, but it was only completing a delayed copy of the expenditure notice. He fetched the sheet before it fell into a tray of metal offcuts. The suspension remained unchanged. Below it, in smaller type, the Secretariat required a revised schedule identifying work that could proceed without additional expenditure.

“They want a schedule,” he said.

“Not my draft?”

“Not in this notice.”

“Then we can answer this one.”

Her distinction steadied him. He had begun treating every communication from the Union as another attack. That would make him careless, and carelessness would give the visitor something better than wounded authority to use against them.

He opened the project timetable. Its coloured bars assumed that London would deliver a draft before the primer trials, allowing the team to use extracts as test material. He remembered approving that arrangement. It had seemed efficient.

London read the relevant line over his shoulder.

“No.”

“I see it.”

“No extracts, no paraphrases, no little sample that happens to contain the first thing I wrote.”

He deleted the dependency. “The primer gets an unrelated test sequence. Something made only for the trial.”

“And I keep the draft until the amendment has an authorised countersignature.”

“Yes.”

“Put that in the schedule too.”

Show did. Beneath the blocked delivery date he entered: *Author retains all message material pending authorised countersignature of the approval-clause amendment.* It was less elegant than a coloured bar. It was also the first accurate thing on that part of the screen.

London set her bag between her feet and drew the stool closer.

“I still need access to the decoding plan. The whole working file, not whichever pages we happen to open together.”

He felt an unexpected resistance. The file contained abandoned approaches, impatient annotations, diagrams he would have preferred to explain before anyone saw them. Then he looked at her bag. She had been asked to entrust her words to a machine whose designers had not yet earned her confidence.

“You’re right,” he said.

He created a read-only copy of the complete current plan, including the assumptions register and the unresolved questions. Then he enabled annotations in a separate layer and turned the terminal towards her. “This account can inspect everything here. Changes to the encoding method will appear in the revision record. You’ll be able to see what changed.”

“Can I keep a copy?”

He found a blank storage card in the instrument drawer. London watched him transfer the files, then opened the directory herself and checked that the technical appendices were present. He left his hands away from the keyboard.

At the corkboard, the crossed-out sunburst caught the lamplight. Beyond the high window, a Union announcement moved across the opposite embankment: another barrier completed, another district protected. Show had signed requisitions for those emergency lamps because they worked when the old wiring failed. He could not dismiss the rising power as nothing but a machine for putting its emblem on things. Nor could he accept that keeping the river out entitled it to decide what humanity should confess.

“They called the orbit impossible clockwork,” he said. “Now they want a delivery date they can announce.”

London looked up from the appendices. “Was it impossible?”

“The first version? Yes. Its return missed the system entirely.”

She waited.

“We corrected it.”

“Then keep that version in the record.”

He almost said that it would confuse the reviewers. Instead he added the early trajectory failure to the assumptions register, with a link to the correction. Eight million years did not become credible because he concealed the occasions on which he had miscalculated.

Together they reduced the immediate schedule to work they could actually do: prepare primer-only sheets on existing supplies, invite uninformed volunteers from other departments, obtain consent without supplying interpretive hints, record mistakes without coaching. No new engraving. No paid recruitment. No message material.

Show printed one clean test sheet and held it up against the bench lamp. Nothing showed through from an old page; no forgotten footer identified the vessel. London checked the reverse before placing it in a fresh folder.

He attached the revised schedule and the amendment to his reply, explicitly requesting referral to the authorised countersignatory. The signature space remained empty.

“Will you send the test invitation?” he asked.

London took the keyboard. “Read it before I do.”

He moved his chair aside for her. While she typed, he labelled a second folder *Failures and revisions* and placed it beside the first.


London read the invitation aloud from the screen. “You are asked to evaluate an unfamiliar symbolic sequence. The exercise concerns legibility, repetition, and inference. You will not be told what the sequence represents. Your responses will be recorded for methodological review. Participation is voluntary, and you may withdraw without explanation.”

She stopped at the last line. “Add that refusal will not affect marks, funding, or access to the library.”

Show inserted it without argument.

“And say that the sequence is not taken from the commission.”

His hands paused above the keyboard. “That may make them curious.”

“Good. Curiosity is not the same as coaching. They should know what they are not being shown.”

He added the sentence in plain type, then turned the screen towards her. Beneath it he placed a box headed *Materials withheld from participants*: the draft, all paraphrases, the vessel specifications, the return trajectory, and every image associated with the commission. London felt some tightness leave her chest. The list was not a lock, but it made the boundary visible. Boundaries survived best when someone had taken the trouble to write them down.

Show opened the primer folder. Five sheets lay inside, each bearing only geometric marks: paired bars, a sequence interrupted by a blank, and a small cluster repeated at different scales. London lifted the first sheet by its corners and held it beneath the bench lamp. The paper showed no watermark except the university seal, which she crossed out on the copy intended for testing.

“Why remove the seal?” Show asked.

“Because authority changes interpretation. If they think the institution expects a correct answer, they’ll search for one.”

“It’s only the university mark.”

“Nothing is only a mark.”

He read the sentence again, then printed another set on unmarked stock from an old supply drawer. The sheets emerged warm and faintly curled. London compared them with the earlier set, checking for smudges and accidental alignment. On one page, a toner fleck made two separate bars appear joined.

“That one goes into the failure folder,” she said.

“It’s a printing defect.”

“Exactly. If the reader mistakes the defect for part of the system, we need to know whether the system is robust enough to survive ordinary human folly.”

Show labelled the page and set it aside. He did not call the mistake irrelevant. That mattered more than she expected. People spoke about error as though it were an intruder from outside themselves, something that happened to a plan rather than something plans continually produced. The Union had built flood barriers on that faith, raising higher walls whenever the river disproved a prediction. The water had receded, but the habit remained.

She opened the separate annotations layer of the decoding plan. Show had left every unresolved question visible: orientation, scale, the meaning of absence, the risk that repetition would be read as emphasis rather than number. She added a note beside the blank interval.

*Test whether participants infer missing quantity, pause, damage, or choice. Do not correct them during the trial.*

Then, beneath it: *A wrong answer is evidence only if the participant is free to give it.*

Show leaned closer, but kept his hands on his own side of the bench. “Would you accept a clarification if someone asks what kind of answer we want?”

“No. We can clarify the procedure, not the symbol. Tell them there is no expected interpretation.”

“That may frustrate them.”

“Then record the frustration.”

He gave a quiet breath that might have been a laugh. “You’re designing a test for human beings.”

“I’m trying not to design a test for obedient instruments.”

The terminal chimed. A delivery confirmation appeared for the revised schedule and the amendment. London scanned the attached record. The message had gone to the Secretariat’s general review channel and had been marked for referral to the authorised countersignatory. The signature field remained empty. For one brief, foolish second she imagined it filled: a name, a seal, permission to release the pages in her bag. She shut the image down before it could become hope mistaken for fact.

Show looked at the confirmation. “Nothing has changed.”

“Something has,” London said. “They now have a schedule that doesn’t depend on my surrender.”

He looked at her, and she saw no irritation in his face, only the strain of carrying a project whose authority had been divided into too many hands. “If the countersignature is refused?”

“Then the record will show that the primer was tested without the message, and that the message was withheld for a stated reason.”

“And if they say that makes the launch impossible?”

“Then they can explain why a launch requires an unauthorised draft.”

The sentence hung between them, sharper than she intended. Show did not retreat behind procedure. He opened the amendment instead and enlarged the clause they had altered: the author retained control of the message until an authorised countersignature confirmed the conditions of approval.

“I’ll place this clause at the top of the trial file,” he said.

“Not at the top. In the access log. People skip introductions when they’re impatient.”

He moved it there. London watched the revision appear with his name, the time of entry, and a note explaining that no message material had been included. The record was almost painfully ordinary. That was its strength. Redemption, she thought, might begin in such ordinary corrections: a person noticing where power had made a shortcut and closing it before anyone was harmed.

Show gathered the primer sheets into two identical envelopes. “I’ll distribute these through the library desk.”

“No. We should not let anyone summarise the task for us.”

“What do you suggest?”

“Place the invitations in the common notice system and let participants contact the project account. We answer with the same script. No conversation about the commission.”

He nodded. “And the first trial?”

“Not here.”

The instrument room seemed suddenly full of listening surfaces: cabinets, pipes, the old clock, the sealed windows. London pointed to the corridor beyond the door. “Use a room without the observatory emblem. Somewhere they won’t assume astronomy is the answer.”

Show collected the envelopes, then stopped. “Will you attend?”

“To observe the procedure.”

“Not to explain the sequence?”

“Not unless the procedure fails.”

“And if it fails because of us?”

She looked at the blank folder holding her draft. It remained closed, untouched, waiting for the authorised countersignature that might permit its future or merely certify its imprisonment.

“Then we record that too,” she said.

Together they left the test sheets in the locked cabinet, separate from every file bearing her words. Show carried the invitation packet; London carried the access log and the sealed draft. At the instrument-room threshold, she checked the lock twice. Only then did she follow him into the corridor, where the first small, honest experiment could begin.


The corridor outside the instrument room smelled of hot dust and rain carried in from the stairwell. Show locked the door behind them and waited for the bolt to settle. London stood beside him with the access log under one arm and the sealed draft held against her coat. He had seen her check the lock twice; he did not tease her for it. A careless door could undo weeks of careful argument.

“Library desk first,” he said.

“Common notice system first,” London replied. “The desk comes after the invitation is visible to everyone.”

He glanced at the packet in his hand. Each envelope contained the same plain instruction sheet, the same project-account address, and no observatory seal. He had removed even the small diagram that might have suggested the exercise belonged to astronomy. “We can post it from the terminal near the reading room.”

“And record the wording before it goes live.”

Show opened the access log. The corridor’s lamps made the screen difficult to read, so he angled it against the wall. The revised entry remained intact: primer material only, no extracts from the commission, no coaching, participation voluntary. Beneath it, he added the location of the first trial—an unoccupied seminar room in the library annex—and noted that the room had been checked for observatory emblems. London read the line, then pointed to the phrase *unoccupied*.

“Is it actually unoccupied?”

“It is scheduled for a staff meeting later.”

“Then call it unoccupied during the trial. Words matter when someone audits the record.”

He changed it. The alteration appeared in the revision history, beside the earlier wording. Show felt the familiar irritation of being made to slow down, followed by the more useful recognition that she had caught an assumption before it became a fact. “You’re going to inspect every adjective.”

“Only the dangerous ones.”

They moved along the corridor. Through the tall windows, the river showed strips of reflected light between the buildings. On the opposite embankment, the Union’s new flood barrier rose in stepped sections, its floodlamps shining through the damp. The structure had become a second horizon. People trusted it because it was large, and because the water had not yet found a way over it. Show had once admired that kind of confidence. Now he saw how easily confidence turned into permission: permission to dismiss the people who had warned that the foundations were wrong, permission to call every objection delay.

At the notice terminal, he signed into the common system and selected the standard category for voluntary academic exercises. London stood close enough to read but did not touch the controls. He pasted the invitation exactly as approved:

*You are asked to evaluate an unfamiliar symbolic sequence. The exercise concerns legibility, repetition, and inference. You will not be told what the sequence represents.*

He added the safeguards about withdrawal, marks, funding, and library access. The final sentence stated that the sequence was not taken from the commission.

Show hesitated over the publish key.

“If you want to remove that,” London said, “say so before you do it.”

“I don’t want to remove it.”

“Then publish it.”

He pressed the key. The notice appeared without the university crest. Beneath it, the system displayed a warning that unattributed research exercises might be mistaken for recruitment. Show opened the classification menu and selected *methodological evaluation*. He attached the project account and saved the record. The system generated a reference number, which he copied into the access log.

“There,” he said. “No personal invitations.”

“And no replies from personal accounts.”

“I’ll set an automatic response.”

“Read it first.”

He opened the draft response. It thanked the sender, repeated the voluntary nature of participation, gave the room and procedure without identifying the symbols, and stated that questions about the commission would not be answered during recruitment. London marked one sentence.

“‘The exercise is unrelated to the launch project.’ That is too strong. It is procedurally separate, not unrelated.”

Show changed the wording. “You’re right.”

The admission came easily now. That surprised him more than the correction itself. Earlier, he would have defended the sentence as harmless shorthand, then spent ten minutes proving that shorthand did not count as deception. London’s method left less room for vanity. She did not ask him to be infallible; she asked him to make the error visible and remove it.

They printed the notice for the library board and walked toward the annex. The packet made a soft, regular sound under his arm as the envelopes struck against one another. At the stairwell, London stopped beside a brass plaque bearing the observatory’s sunburst.

“This is exactly what we’re avoiding.”

“The annex is on the other side of the library.”

“Then we use the service passage.”

The service passage ran behind the stacks, narrow and painted the colour of old paper. Show led the way, pushing open a fire door that had been wedged with a wooden block. Beyond it, the library annex waited in a quieter wing. He checked the seminar room before unlocking it. No sunburst, no star charts, no instrument diagrams—only tables, folding chairs, a blank wall, and a radiator ticking as it cooled.

London entered and inspected the corners. She lifted a wastebasket lid, checked beneath the table, and examined the room’s single framed notice about fire evacuation.

“Nothing that tells them what to think,” she said.

Show set the invitation packet on the nearest table. “We’ll use the same opening script for everyone. Read the instructions. Confirm consent. Give them the sheet. No interpretation prompts.”

“And if they ask whether the marks are mathematical?”

“You say the exercise does not provide that information.”

“If they ask what an answer should look like?”

“There is no expected interpretation.”

London nodded. “And if they refuse?”

“We record refusal only as refusal, not as failure.”

He placed the access log on the table between them. The room felt different from the instrument room: less charged, less like a place where authority had gathered and hardened. Yet the blank wall seemed to demand a verdict. Show pinned the standard script beside it, then positioned the failure folder beneath the spare forms.

London touched the sealed draft through her coat. “The message remains with me.”

“Yes.”

“Until the authorised countersignature.”

“Yes.”

She looked at the room, the untouched sheets, and the empty chairs. “Then begin with what we can know.”

Show opened the door to the annex corridor and set the first invitation on the public board. The notice stayed there, plain and unadorned, while the barrier lights continued to burn beyond the library windows. He returned to the seminar room and left the door open, ready for whoever came without being told what to see.


London remained by the open door until the corridor’s last footsteps faded. The notice on the public board was visible from where she stood: a rectangle of plain paper among university announcements, room bookings, and a colourful appeal for volunteers to repair the garden roof. Nothing about it announced the launch, the observatory, or the authority behind the project. That smallness pleased her. A message intended for the distant future had begun with a sheet that could be mistaken for rubbish.

Show returned from the board and closed the seminar-room door to its first stop, leaving it unlatched. “The notice is live,” he said. “The project account is the only contact.”

London checked the terminal on the table. The reference number matched the access log. “And the automatic reply?”

“Published with the notice.”

“Read it once more.”

He opened the response. She listened without looking at him, following each sentence against the approved script: thanks for the inquiry, voluntary participation, no effect on marks or funding, withdrawal without explanation, no information about the symbols, no questions about the commission answered during recruitment. The wording was careful, but one phrase caught against her attention.

“‘Responses may contribute to future communication research,’” she said. “That implies a benefit.”

“It says may.”

“May is still a promise when people want to be useful.”

Show selected the phrase. “What should it say?”

“‘Responses will be recorded for methodological review.’ Nothing beyond that.”

He replaced it, then read the whole paragraph aloud again. London watched the revision history populate beneath the text. His acceptance no longer felt like surrender. His revisions showed that he was beginning to treat procedure not as a wall placed between people and knowledge, but as a way of preventing the powerful from deciding what counted as knowledge after the fact.

She took the numbered envelopes from the cabinet and laid them in a row. Each held an identical primer sheet, a consent form, and a response page with no institutional crest. “We should rehearse the opening before anyone comes.”

“We already agreed on the script.”

“Agreement is not performance.”

He pulled a chair into the marked position opposite the table. London stood behind it, where an observer would sit, and held the instruction sheet at the same angle she had used for every copy. “Begin as though I know nothing.”

Show read the first sentence. His voice was steady, though the room gave it a slight echo. “You are invited to examine a sequence of unfamiliar symbols. We are studying how people describe visual patterns when no interpretation is supplied.”

London raised a hand. “That says we are studying people. It may make someone feel examined rather than consulted.”

He looked down at the sheet. “The approved wording was meant to be neutral.”

“Neutral to us. Not necessarily to them.”

He crossed out the sentence and wrote a replacement in the margin: *The exercise asks you to describe what you notice in the material provided.* London considered it, then nodded. “Keep the original in the revision record. Don’t erase the path.”

He entered the change on the terminal. The old wording remained visible beneath the new one, with the reason attached: *avoid implying evaluation of the participant rather than evaluation of the material.* London felt the local objective move by inches, not toward a result but toward a condition in which a result could be trusted.

Show continued the rehearsal. He asked for consent without mentioning the launch, then explained that the participant could stop at any point. When he reached the instruction, “Please tell us what the sequence means,” London stopped him again.

“We cannot say that.”

“It’s in the old draft.”

“It is a coaching prompt. Say, ‘Please describe what you notice, if you wish.’”

He corrected the script. “And if they say they see nothing?”

“We thank them and record the response.”

“What if they ask whether they’re supposed to find a pattern?”

“We say there is no expected interpretation.”

“What if they become embarrassed?”

“Offer a pause. Do not reassure them that they’re close.”

Show’s pen rested above the form. “That last part sounds obvious.”

“People reassure others with their faces before they speak.”

He glanced at her, then set the pen down. “Then I’ll sit where the participant can see my hands, not just my face.”

The consideration moved her more than an apology would have been. He was no longer merely correcting text; his position showed that he was examining his own presence as an instrument capable of altering the result. London placed the sealed draft in the locked drawer beneath the access log, keeping its key on the chain inside her coat. The amendment still awaited an authorised countersignature. Nothing in the trial could be allowed to make that absence seem ceremonial.

A notification chimed from the terminal. An inquiry had arrived through the public account. London and Show leaned over the screen, careful not to read more than the message required.

*Can participants bring a friend to help understand the symbols?*

Show looked to her.

“Answer with the standard procedure,” London said. “Observers aren’t part of the exercise unless approved in advance, and no one may interpret for a participant.”

He typed. She checked every line before he sent it. The reply offered no hint about what the symbols represented and no suggestion that attendance would be useful. When the message disappeared into the outgoing queue, the room settled around them again.

Outside, someone paused at the public board. London saw only a shadow pass across the wired glass. She did not move toward the door. The invitation had to work without her persuasion. Show stood beside the table, waiting, and she saw him resist the impulse to fill the silence.

“Leave the door open,” she said.

“It is open.”

“Then let the room make the first claim.”

He opened it wider. The corridor entered as a strip of pale light, touching the envelopes but not the locked drawer. London took her seat beside the access log, ready to record consent, refusal, confusion, or nothing at all. For once, not knowing was not a failure of preparation. It was the boundary they had prepared to defend.


The silence lasted long enough for Show to hear the radiator click twice. He kept his hands flat on the table, palms visible, as he had promised. Beyond the open door, the annex corridor held its pale strip of light. Anyone approaching would see the room before seeing him. London sat beside the access log with her pen uncapped, the sealed draft secured in the locked drawer beneath them. The arrangement made the room feel less like a laboratory than a public place in which even refusal required room to exist.

A shadow crossed the wired glass again. This time it stopped.

Show rose, then checked himself before taking a step toward the door. “Good afternoon,” he called. “You’re welcome to come in, or to read the notice from where you are.”

A young man appeared in the corridor, carrying a folded umbrella and a library book pressed against his chest. He looked from Show to London, then to the blank wall. “Is this the symbols exercise?”

Show felt the old impulse to explain: the commission, the transmission, the vessel that would carry a human message into a darkness so distant that the word *return* became almost meaningless. The explanation waited behind his teeth, brilliant and useless.

“It is an exercise involving unfamiliar visual material,” he said. “The notice gives the full procedure.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the answer we’re authorised to give during recruitment.”

The man’s mouth tightened. Show recognised the expression from committee meetings, from visitors who had decided that caution was merely cowardice wearing a tie. “So you want people to guess without knowing what they’re looking at?”

“We want to record what people do when no interpretation is supplied.”

“That sounds like a trick.”

“It may sound that way. You can decline without giving a reason.”

London’s pen moved across the form. She did not look up or intervene. Show was grateful for the silence, though it made each sentence his responsibility. The man studied the notice through the open door.

“Do I get paid?”

“No.”

“Does it affect marks or funding?”

“No.”

“And if I bring someone who understands this sort of thing?”

“Observers and interpretation assistance aren’t part of the exercise unless approved in advance. You would complete it yourself, and you could stop at any point.”

The man gave a short, humourless laugh. “Then you’ve designed it so nobody can tell whether they’re doing it right.”

“There is no expected interpretation.”

“That’s convenient.”

Show felt heat rise behind his collar. In the instrument room, he would have argued about the difference between convenience and control. Here, the argument would become persuasion, and persuasion would contaminate the response before it began. He pointed to the plain chair opposite the table.

“You can decide not to participate. If you do participate, I’ll read the consent statement first.”

The man remained in the corridor. “What’s the point?”

Show glanced at the locked drawer. The launch project had already made a grand promise on behalf of the species: that eight million years from now, someone might find evidence that human beings had tried to speak honestly. Yet every administrative shortcut they prevented was evidence of a different human tendency—the wish to control the answer before asking the question.

“The point,” he said carefully, “is to learn what can be recorded without telling you what to see.”

The man looked at him for a long moment. Then he stepped inside and set the umbrella beside the chair.

Show did not smile. He took the first numbered envelope from the row and checked that its seal was unbroken. “Before we begin, I need to explain your rights. Participation is voluntary. You may stop without giving a reason. Your response will be recorded for methodological review. It will not affect marks, funding, or access to university services.”

The man nodded, though Show did not treat the movement as consent. He placed the form on the table and waited until the man had read it. London turned the log toward him so that he could record the time without asking the participant to announce anything personal.

“Do you consent to take part?” Show asked.

“Yes.”

He marked the response, then slid the primer across the table. It contained only the instructions and the unfamiliar sequence, nothing from the commission, no diagram of the vessel, no suggestion that the marks belonged to the stars. The man bent over it. His umbrella dripped onto the floor.

For several seconds he said nothing.

Show kept his hands still.

“They look like a failed alphabet,” the man said.

Show wrote the words exactly. “Would you like to describe what you notice?”

“They repeat, but not regularly. This one seems to face the other way.” He touched the page, then withdrew his finger. “Is that what you wanted?”

“There is no expected interpretation.”

The man looked up sharply. “You keep saying that.”

“Because it remains true.”

He returned to the sheet. “Then I don’t know what else to say.”

“Your response can be that you don’t know.”

The man considered this, visibly irritated by the permission. “I don’t know what else to say.”

Show recorded it without softening the phrase. The result seemed small, almost absurdly small, but the record held no claim that the participant had failed or discovered anything. It held only what had been offered.

When the man finished, Show asked whether he wished to withdraw his response. He said no. Show gave him the copy of the procedure and pointed out the project account for later questions, repeating that questions about the symbols or the commission would not be answered during recruitment.

At the door, the man paused. “You really expect someone to make sense of this eventually?”

Show looked toward the locked drawer, then at the unadorned page. “I expect us not to pretend we already have.”

The man left. His footsteps receded toward the public board.

London closed the access log and added the response number to the envelope. “You didn’t reassure him.”

“I nearly did.”

“But you didn’t.”

Show looked at the damp mark his umbrella had left on the floor. “I thought that was what he needed.”

“Perhaps. It wasn’t what the procedure needed.”

He accepted the correction. Together they replaced the used materials, marked the chair as available, and left the door open. The corridor no longer looked empty. It contained the record of a refusal to be coached, a partial description, and an honest admission of uncertainty. Show returned to his chair and positioned his hands where the next participant would see them.


London waited until the corridor swallowed the participant’s footsteps before she touched the used envelope. Show had already placed the response page inside it, but he had left the access log open beneath his hand. The participant’s words remained visible in his handwriting: *failed alphabet*. Beneath them, in the same measured script: *I don’t know what else to say.*

“Read it back,” she said.

Show did not object. He read the two statements, then the recorded consent and the note that the participant had declined to withdraw his response. London listened for additions that were not there: no description of embarrassment, no guess about intention, no inference from the way the man had touched the page. The record was thin, but its thinness was honest.

“Now check the envelope number against the log.”

He did. “Seven.”

“And the primer?”

“Seven.”

“The procedure copy?”

“Seven.”

London drew a line through the final item on the materials checklist. “Good. The next person must not receive anything that has been handled during the previous exercise.”

Show looked at the sealed draft’s drawer, then at the row of fresh envelopes. “You think contamination is possible from paper alone?”

“I think people are inventive when they believe an answer matters. A smudge becomes a clue. A bent corner becomes a hint. If we can prevent an accident, we prevent it.”

He nodded and began preparing the next set. His movements were slower than before the first participant entered. He separated the pages, checked the response number, and placed each sheet squarely beneath the next. London watched him pause over the instruction page.

“The phrase about ‘unfamiliar visual material’,” he said. “Should we keep it?”

“Why?”

“It may be too broad. Someone could think the exercise concerns an image, or a map, or a test of eyesight.”

“They can ask before consenting.”

“And if they ask what kind of material?”

“We give the approved answer.”

He turned the page toward her. “The approved answer says the material is not explained in advance.”

“That is not the same as saying what it is.”

“Would you add ‘symbolic’?”

London considered the word. It seemed harmless, but it carried a direction. Symbolic meant that the marks stood for something; it invited the participant to search for a code rather than describe the marks themselves. “No. We are not going to plant a category in their mind because it makes our work easier.”

Show crossed out the suggestion in the revision record. “Accepted.”

The word settled between them with more weight than it should have had. In the old committee room, correction had been treated as an attack on authority. Here, Show gave it a place in the procedure and moved on. London felt her trust in him become less personal and more useful: he could be relied upon to let the record outrank his pride.

A notification appeared on the terminal. Another public inquiry had arrived.

Show reached for the keyboard, but London raised a finger. “Read it aloud before you answer.”

He did. *If a person recognises the sequence from somewhere else, should they say so?*

London looked at the response page marked with the participant’s admission of uncertainty. Recognition was exactly the sort of event they could not manufacture and could not reward. “Yes,” she said. “They should report whatever they notice, including recognition. But the instruction must not suggest that recognition is expected.”

Show drafted the reply. They examined it together, removing a phrase that said *useful discovery* and replacing it with *relevant observation*. The difference was small enough to escape anyone rushing through an inbox, yet large enough to keep the participant from imagining a prize.

“Send it,” London said.

He did. Then he added the inquiry to the public correspondence log, noting the time without inventing an identity for the sender. London checked the entry and initialled the margin.

The room had grown close. The radiator clicked, and rainwater from the participant’s umbrella still darkened a patch of linoleum near the chair. London took the cleaning cloth from the cabinet.

“I’ll deal with that,” Show said.

“You prepare the envelopes.”

“I can do both.”

“You can, but then you’ll hurry one of them.”

He accepted the cloth from her and wiped the floor in slow strokes while she inspected the remaining materials. The ordinary labour steadied the room. The project had once been described in language of vessels, transmissions, and deep time, as if human folly could be overcome by altitude and distance. Yet the work before them was this: a wet floor, a numbered envelope, a reply that refused to flatter its recipient. Hope, London thought, might depend on such humiliating exactness.

When the materials were ready, she closed the log and locked the drawer containing the sealed draft. “We need the countersignature request sent again,” she said. “Not as a reminder that assumes approval. As a request for review.”

Show opened the relevant form. “The previous version is still pending.”

“Then the new one must refer to the amendment, not imply that the amendment has been accepted.”

He changed the wording while she watched. The request identified the sealed draft, described the procedural revision, and stated plainly that no use of it was authorised until the countersignature arrived. London checked each sentence. Only then did she approve its dispatch.

Show pressed send. “That advances nothing visibly.”

“It advances the part we can control.”

She lifted the fresh envelopes and walked to the door. “We should replace the public notice with the corrected reply about observers.”

Show followed, carrying the access log. London stepped from the annex seminar room into the corridor, making sure the door remained open behind them. Together they crossed to the public board, where the invitation sat among ordinary university papers. She pinned the clarification beneath it, then returned to the room and recorded the posting in the log.

No one had been persuaded. No symbol had been interpreted. But the next participant would encounter a cleaner boundary than the first, and Show would meet that participant with a procedure he trusted enough to follow—and trusted London enough to correct.


Show remained by the public board until London had pressed the last corner of the clarification flat against the cork. The paper beneath it advertised a lecture on planetary migration, its title half-hidden by the new notice. A draft from the recruitment account was still visible on the terminal in his hand, and he checked it against the version pinned before stepping away. No phrase had changed in transit. No eager volunteer had been called a witness, no uncertainty had been dressed as discovery.

“Read the first line,” London said.

He did. “Participation is voluntary.”

“And the second.”

“Descriptions are recorded without an expected interpretation.”

She nodded. “Good.”

They walked back along the corridor together. The annex seminar room stood open, its table visible through the doorway, with the fresh materials arranged in numbered order. Show carried the access log against his chest. The sealed drawer remained locked. He had stopped noticing the key’s weight in his pocket, but now he felt it and was grateful for the physical reminder. Whatever happened next, the draft would remain unused until the countersignature arrived.

Inside, London inspected the chair, the table surface, and the waste tray. Show watched her check the underside of the desk for stray paper. A ridiculous precaution, perhaps, but he no longer felt the urge to defend the word ridiculous. The commission had been endangered less by impossible distances than by small permissions granted too casually. A copied diagram, an unmarked suggestion, a discarded page in the wrong bin: human beings could turn any gap into an invitation.

“Set seven aside,” she said.

He looked at the envelope. “For the next participant?”

“For audit. We have the response, but the materials must remain identifiable until the review is complete.”

He placed the envelope in the marked tray. “Then number eight.”

“Number eight.”

He laid out the primer, response sheet, consent form, and procedure copy. London checked each number aloud while he answered. The exchange had the plain rhythm of a rehearsal, but it steadied him. Their earlier disagreements had not vanished; they had become useful edges. He could feel where his own certainty would carry him too quickly, and he trusted her to stop him before momentum became instruction.

A figure appeared beyond the wired glass. Show straightened, then deliberately let his shoulders fall. The visitor raised one hand, not quite a knock.

“Come in,” Show said. “You may also read the notice first.”

The door opened. A student in a dark coat stepped over the threshold and glanced at the blank wall, the table, and the two of them. “Is this connected to the launch project?”

Show felt the answer forming—the vessel, the journey into deep time, the hope that a message might survive long enough to be mistaken for a beginning. He kept it behind his teeth.

“It is a voluntary study involving unfamiliar visual material,” he said. “The procedure explains what participation involves. We can answer questions about your rights, but not interpret the material for you.”

The student looked at London. “And you?”

“I check that the procedure is followed,” London said. “You can leave without participating.”

The student paused, then entered fully and read the notice, one hand resting on the back of the chair. Show waited until the page had been returned to the table.

“Do I need to know anything about astronomy?” the student asked.

“No.”

“Does the project have a correct answer?”

“We are not asking you to find one.”

The student gave him a sceptical look. “That sounds like something said when there is a correct answer.”

Show felt the old pressure to persuade. Instead, he turned the consent form so its conditions faced the visitor. “You may describe what you notice, say that you notice nothing, or stop. We will record your response rather than grade it.”

London’s pen hovered over the log, but she did not fill the silence for him. Show took the restraint as confidence, not abandonment. He read the consent statement, pausing where the form required, and waited while the student signed.

When he opened envelope eight, the primer made a faint paper crackle. The sequence lay beneath the room’s fluorescent light: marks without a title, repetitions without a promised key. The student bent over them. Show could hear the radiator clicking in the corridor and the distant lift stopping on another floor.

“These look deliberate,” the student said.

Show recorded the phrase exactly. “What makes you say that?”

“They’re too balanced to be random.” The student traced the air above the page without touching it. “But maybe that’s me imposing balance.”

London’s gaze met Show’s for an instant. He felt no need to rescue the response from its uncertainty.

“You can record both observations,” he said.

The student wrote for several minutes. Show kept his hands visible and his voice level. When the student finally set down the pencil, the page contained a description of repeated forms, a question about orientation, and a final sentence: *I may be seeing intention because I was told to look.*

Show read it back without emphasis. The student listened, then nodded.

“Keep it?” Show asked.

“Yes.”

He marked the decision and placed the response in the envelope. London checked the number, the seal, and the access entry. This time Show did not reach for the log before she did. He let her correction govern the sequence, and the small surrender felt cleaner than any defence of his authority.

At the door, the student glanced once more at the marks. “Will anyone ever know what they mean?”

Show looked at the untouched drawer, the fresh materials waiting for another stranger, and the honest page between them. “If they do,” he said, “it should be because the record allowed them to ask—not because we told them what to find.”

The student left. Show heard the footsteps move toward the public board, then fade. London sealed the envelope and wrote the response number into the log. He carried the used materials to the marked tray while she prepared the next clean set. Beyond the open door, the corrected notice remained visible to anyone passing through the corridor. The work had moved one person farther—not toward an answer, but toward a record that might survive their mistakes.
