Three days before the clemency hearing, Mara asked Tomas Reed to move his chair.

“Against the light,” she said. “I want to see what you’re writing.”

He shifted it without answering. Beyond the barred window, rain ran down a wall so close that it seemed to seal the room. There was a table, two chairs, and an iron ring through which the warder had passed her chain. Someone had scratched a small house into the tabletop. Mara kept putting her thumb over its door.

Tomas laid out paper. His coat smelled of wet wool, and one cuff had been repaired with thread a shade too pale.

“Your statement,” he said. “Whenever you’re ready.”

She had prepared its opening through most of the night.

“The Exchange burned because Greyhaven had made it a place that deserved to burn. For years, this city fed families into its accounts and called the result prosperity. What I did was the act it wanted done but lacked the courage to claim.”

His pen stopped.

“Wanted?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“You can speak to what you intended. You cannot speak for everyone in Greyhaven.”

“There were songs about burning it.”

“There are songs about drowning tax collectors.”

“Write what I said.”

He looked at her, then continued. The scratch of the nib steadied her. While he wrote, her words existed somewhere outside her body, acquiring the orderly appearance of facts.

“The same citizens who cheered the ruin now demand my execution,” she dictated. “They wish to hang one person so they need not examine what they themselves desired.”

A voice rose in the passage, followed by the clatter of a bucket. Mara waited until it had passed.

Tomas set down his pen.

“Which citizens cheered?”

“You heard them.”

“I heard people welcome the destruction of debts. I also heard people calling for water.”

“The city cannot pretend it had no part in this.”

“Some people may have wanted the registers destroyed. That is not the same as wanting a fire with people inside.”

She pulled her hands together. The chain rasped through the ring.

“They weren’t supposed to die.”

“That belongs in the statement.”

“It makes it sound like an accident.”

“Was their dying part of your intention?”

“No.”

“Then say what happened to them.”

She looked toward the window. Water gathered beneath a flaw in the glass, swelled, and dropped.

Tomas opened the folder beside his elbow.

“We could begin with their names.”

“The tribunal has their names.”

“This is not for the tribunal’s information.”

“Then what is it for?”

“For you to acknowledge that the people who died were not the Exchange.”

She knew where the list lay in his folder. She had seen it during the trial, each name followed by an age and an occupation, as though a few neat particulars could account for a person. There had been an apprentice younger than she had expected. She remembered being angry with the clerk for speaking so softly that everyone leaned forward.

“They’ve had weeks of testimony,” she said. “My statement should explain why.”

Tomas closed the folder, but did not pick up his pen.

Beyond the door, someone laughed. The sound offended her with its ease. In three days, strangers would sit comfortably while deciding whether she should live, and beyond those three days she could imagine nothing without feeling the skin beneath her jaw tighten.

“Iona Pell,” she said.

“What about her?”

“She must testify.”

“I can approach her.”

“You’ve seen what she said.”

“I have.”

The weekly paper had reached Mara folded inside a letter. Iona had been asked whether she would attend the execution. Her answer occupied four lines beneath an account of the prison preparations: *My husband is dead. I do not ask that another person be killed for me.*

Mara had read those lines until the paper softened at the crease.

“If she says that before the tribunal, they’ll have to commute.”

“They will have to listen. That is all I can promise.”

“She survived it. She lost someone. They cannot dismiss her as sentimental.”

Tomas rubbed a spot of ink from his finger.

“Her husband’s name was Elias.”

“I know.”

“You haven’t said it.”

“It isn’t necessary every time.”

He waited. She hated the patience of it, the way it left her responsible for the silence.

“People say I don’t care because I won’t perform grief for them,” she said. “Iona understands that killing me won’t restore anything. Let her tell them.”

“Opposing your execution does not mean she understands you as you wish to be understood.”

“It means she knows this sentence is wrong.”

“It may. I’ll ask her what she means.”

Mara leaned forward as far as the chain allowed.

“Tell her that if she stays away, they’ll take her silence for consent.”

“No.”

The word was quiet enough that she initially mistook it for hesitation.

“You must make her understand the consequence.”

“I will explain why her testimony might matter. I will not tell a woman who survived your fire that your death will be her responsibility.”

Heat climbed Mara’s neck.

“That isn’t what I said.”

“It is what you asked me to carry to her.”

For a moment she considered demanding another advocate. There were three days left. Another advocate would need the papers, permissions, time. Tomas knew it too, although nothing in his face suggested satisfaction.

“What if she refuses?” she asked.

“We present the petition without her.”

“And they hang me.”

“I don’t know what they will decide.”

“You’re supposed to help me.”

“I am helping you. But this is a clemency hearing, not a second trial. The tribunal can leave the sentence standing. Iona can decline to attend. If she attends, she can speak against execution and still say things you do not want said.”

Mara looked down at his pages. Her opening filled almost a sheet. The last sentence ended at *desired*. Beneath it lay clean paper, enough space for everything she had meant to make inevitable.

A key turned in the outer lock.

Tomas gathered the statement, then paused.

“Do you want to add anything about the people who died?”

She pressed her thumb over the scratched doorway.

“Not yet.”

The warder entered and reached for the chain.

“You’ll bring Iona,” Mara said.

“I’ll ask her.”

The iron lifted from the table. Mara tried to hold her hands still as Tomas put the pages into his bag.

“Ask properly,” she said, but he had already turned toward the open door.

Before the fire, Mara kept accounts for a collection office in Lowbank, in a room that smelled of damp wool and coal smoke. The office stood above a cobbler’s shop. Its stairs were steep, and those summoned there climbed them one at a time, usually with a notice in hand and their eyes lowered.

Mara sat behind a high desk, copying names and sums into the ledgers in a fine, even hand. Mr. Vale, her employer, said neat figures made people less inclined to argue. He was right, though not as he intended. A number written cleanly looked official. A disputed charge could pass for a debt the moment it appeared in ink.

The fees came in small amounts: a shilling for a missed notice, another for a visit, another for recording the first two. Some were forbidden by the city’s own rules. Mara knew because she had copied those rules into a reference book for the office. When tenants challenged their bills, Vale told her to show them the total, not the parts.

One wet morning, a woman arrived with a boy whose coat sleeves ended above his wrists. She set a folded notice on Mara’s desk and asked why the amount had grown since the previous week.

“Administrative costs,” Mara said, reading from the page.

“I paid the collector.”

Mara found the entry. There was a visit fee, though no visit had been recorded, and a penalty dated two days before the notice had been delivered. The boy stared at the brass clasp on the ledger while his mother waited, rainwater dripping from her hem.

“You’ve been charged twice,” Mara said.

The woman’s face tightened with caution rather than relief. “Will you take it off?”

“I can’t alter the account.”

“Then why tell me?”

Mara had no answer that would help. She folded the notice along its old crease and returned it. The woman went downstairs carrying the same debt and the knowledge that the office had seen it.

Vale called Mara into his office after they had gone. He did not raise his voice, which made his rebuke feel less like anger than routine.

“You are paid to keep the books, Miss Venn, not to interpret them for every tenant who dislikes the sum.”

“I showed her the entry.”

“You made her think she had a claim.”

“She did have a claim.”

He tapped a fingernail on his desk. His cuffs were clean, his collar stiff. “People who borrow must repay. If we waive every fee they object to, the rest will decide objections are a method of payment.”

Mara returned to her desk. Below, the cobbler’s hammer struck leather, steady as a clock. The woman on the stairs had not made it pause.

The office kept formal accounts for the Exchange and working ledgers where charges were copied forward until they hardened into fact. Mara began checking them against the rules. She found a widow charged for a notice delivered to the wrong address, a family billed for inspecting a locked door, and the same fee entered twice under different names. She marked the entries in pencil.

Vale found the marks the next afternoon. He turned the ledger toward her.

“You have been altering the record.”

“Only marking charges that don’t match the schedule.”

“The schedule is not your concern.”

“It is if the books are wrong.”

For a moment she thought he might be embarrassed. Instead, he sighed as if she had made a simple task needlessly difficult.

“Your wages are paid through the Exchange’s account. Their rules govern what is collectible. Our office cannot survive on your private sense of justice.”

“My sense has nothing to do with it. The amounts are unlawful.”

“Then let the tribunal decide.”

“But no one takes them there. They can’t afford to.”

Vale looked at her as though she had proved his point. He told her to collect her coat and not return. He would pay her through the week, he said; she could consider it a kindness.

She left with her purse light and her fingers stained blue-black from ledger ink. Lowbank crowded around her: brick houses, washing strung over narrow lanes, shopkeepers calling prices through open doors. Uphill, Greyhaven’s stone buildings rose above the smoke as if the district below them were only weather.

People in Lowbank spoke of the Exchange as a hand around the neighborhood’s throat. They cursed its registers, which preserved each late payment and penalty long after the original debt was settled. At the market, a fish seller joked that the surest way to balance an account was to burn the book. Someone answered with a rhyme about flames running through columns of figures. The laughter was rough and brief; then the market returned to bargaining.

Mara heard the rhyme again outside a public house. Two men sang it as they finished their drinks, and a woman waiting at the pump joined the last line, changing a word to make the rhythm work. They sang about smoke and ash, about names disappearing from the registers. Their voices were cheerful in the careless way of people who expected the song to end before they went home.

No one asked what would happen if the fire reached the rooms above the records. No one named the clerks, the watchmen, or anyone who might be inside after dark. The song was about the books. The laughter was about the power written in them.

Mara understood the difference. She knew the tune did not amount to a plan, and that anger was not permission. But she had watched families climb the office stairs, trying to keep fear from their children, while the rules she had copied protected the people who broke them. Vale’s dismissal seemed to settle what her careful marks could not.

So she listened to the rhyme and treated the laughter as an answer. The neighborhood hated the Exchange; therefore, she told herself, it would welcome whatever was done to it. Resentment became consent in her mind because consent was what she needed, and no one in the street had thought to offer it.

Two days before the hearing, Iona Pell entered the visiting room with her injured hand held close against her body. The fingers curled inward as if around something small. A pale, glossy scar ran from her wrist beneath her cuff.

Mara had laid out the statement she had prepared. She had meant it to be accurate, and useful: the Exchange’s obstructed routes, the absence of adequate precautions during evening auctions, the fire’s spread through rooms meant to be clear. She had chosen each phrase to make the danger impossible to dismiss.

Iona read the first page, then set it down.

“I won’t say this.”

Mara kept her voice level. “It describes what happened.”

“It describes what you want it to mean.” Iona’s gaze moved over the lines again. “You want me to say the fire was a protest. That we all struck at the Exchange together.”

“I didn’t write that.”

“You wrote ‘a shared anger’ and ‘a common purpose.’” Iona’s good hand tapped the paper once. “I had no part in your purpose.”

The room smelled of wet wool and old stone. Beyond its narrow window, Lowbank’s roofs crowded under a low sky. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled over the paving; the sound passed and left the silence more complete.

Mara gathered the pages, though she had no reason to. “You’ll testify about the routes?”

“Yes. They were blocked. The evening auction left crates in the passage and people packed along the stairs. There was no clear warning, no proper order for getting everyone out. I will say what I saw.”

“And the fire—”

“I will say what I saw.” Iona looked down at her hand. “I will say this one will never close properly. I will say Daniel died. Elias died. I will not turn their deaths into a sentence that makes you feel less alone in what you did.”

Mara felt the words land and tried, reflexively, to sort them. Iona could help prove the Exchange had endangered its workers. That made her an ally. But she wanted Mara confined for life. That made her an enemy. The two labels pressed against each other until neither would hold.

“I need the tribunal to understand the Exchange’s part,” Mara said.

“It should understand the whole of it. The Exchange kept unsafe rooms open. You set the fire. Both things are true.”

The plainness of it left Mara nothing to correct. She had expected anger, perhaps a plea for mercy, perhaps the cold satisfaction of someone who had decided there was nothing to lose. She had not expected Iona to refuse the comfort of choosing one culprit.

“What do you want?” Mara asked.

“An accurate account. Compensation for the households that lost people or their means of living. And for you to be confined for life.”

Iona spoke without raising her voice. Her injured fingers twitched once against her palm. There was no triumph in her face, no attempt to make the demand crueler than it was. That made it harder to meet.

Mara lowered her eyes to the statement. In the margin, in her own careful hand, she had written *we*. The word seemed swollen now, a small lie trying to occupy the page.

Iona would speak against the Exchange. She would speak against Mara, too. There was no way to turn that into an alliance without changing what Iona had said.

The scar on Iona’s wrist brought back the narrow ruled columns of the reconciliation book, and the days before the fire, when Mara had believed a clean copy of the truth could still serve as a weapon.

She had been a bookkeeper at the Exchange long enough to recognize a charge disguised by a change of name. A late fee would reappear as an inspection cost; an auction entry would be adjusted until the debtor owed more than the goods had fetched. Mara had marked the discrepancies and carried them to Vale. He had listened with his hands folded on his desk, then dismissed her for “careless handling of confidential accounts.”

Before she left, she copied the disputed charges and auction entries into a smaller reconciliation book. She worked from memory where she had to, and checked every figure she could against the pages still available to her. The little book went out of the Exchange with her. It survived the fire, beyond the reach of the burning registers, and its entries could support claims from households the Exchange had kept waiting for answers.

She had known the difference between destroying evidence and preserving it. She had preserved the evidence that could expose the Exchange, then planned to destroy the records that gave it power over Lowbank.

The distinction had seemed clean at the time.

She knew the building’s exits. She knew which guards changed posts during the evening auctions and how long the front rooms stayed busy after the last lots were called. She knew the cleaners worked after the buyers left. Clerks stayed late to reconcile the day’s sales and carry the books upstairs. She had watched them pass through the corridors, aprons gray with dust, sleeves rolled back, faces slack with the weariness of people thinking only of the walk home.

She knew they would still be inside.

That knowledge should have stopped her. Instead she put it beside all the other facts she could not bear: her dismissal, the altered accounts, the families whose names remained trapped in the registers. She told herself the Exchange was stone and iron, that the fire would take the records first, that anyone in the rooms would see the danger and leave. The exits were there. The guards knew the building. People were not helpless.

She had not asked anyone who worked the late shift how quickly the corridors cleared. She had not checked whether the auction crowds left every route open. She did not wait to learn how fast a fire could travel through the rooms above the records.

The reassurance arrived before the questions. She accepted it as if it were an answer, and went on.

The day before the hearing, the gaoler brought a chair into Mara’s room and wiped its seat with his sleeve.

He had not done that for Iona.

Councilor Adrian Saye entered carrying his gloves. Rain shone on the shoulders of his coat, but his boots were scarcely marked by the yard. He waited until the gaoler withdrew, then set the gloves on his knee.

“At tomorrow’s hearing,” he said, “there will be opportunities to make matters worse.”

Mara sat on the edge of her narrow bed. Beyond the barred window, water dripped from a broken gutter into the yard.

“I read your speech,” she said. “Exemplary punishment. You were very particular.”

“My public duty concerns the safety of the city.”

“And your private duty?”

For the first time, he looked directly at her.

“That depends on whether you can distinguish a useful concession from a defeat.”

She had once entered figures for one of his companies beneath the heading *Adjustments*. Storage charged after goods had been removed. Interest calculated on fees that had never been agreed. She remembered the cramped space in the ledger margins where she had tried to make the figures reconcile.

Saye leaned forward.

“You have alleged that my company imposed unlawful charges. Withdraw those allegations. State that your copies were incomplete and that you cannot substantiate the conclusions drawn from them.”

“My copies are not incomplete.”

“Every copy is incomplete without its context.”

“The originals burned. That makes context convenient.”

His fingers tightened around one glove.

“You could also undertake not to circulate the book.”

So he knew it had survived. Until that moment, she had wondered how much had reached him: rumor of a few entries, perhaps, or a former clerk’s accusations. He knew enough to come himself.

“And in return?”

“I would support a petition for clemency.”

“You asked them to make an example of me.”

“An example need not be an execution.”

The word left a coldness beneath her ribs. She had tried not to picture the sentence beyond tomorrow’s room: the formal voice, the warrant, the arrangements made by people who would go home afterward. Now Saye had brought that future within reach and suggested it might be moved.

“Would you say so before the tribunal?”

“I would make my position known through the proper channels.”

“After I withdraw the allegations.”

“Your willingness to correct them would demonstrate remorse.”

She looked at his clean cuffs. He was offering no promise she could hold him to, yet she wanted to ask him to repeat it. Support for clemency. A phrase thin enough to hide behind, wide enough to contain the possibility of living.

For several breaths she imagined agreeing. Tomas could prepare a statement. She could admit that anger had led her beyond what the evidence proved. She might survive to resent herself for it.

Then she remembered Iona resting her damaged hand on the table, moving it carefully with the other.

The copied entries would not restore that hand or bring Daniel home. But beside other testimony, they could establish what households had been made to pay, what goods had been taken, what claims deserved examination. Mara could surrender her own accusation. She could not make the charges cease to have fallen on other people.

“Leave me the terms in writing,” she said.

Saye stood.

“I have offered you a way to be sensible. Do not mistake discretion for weakness.”

He took his gloves and called for the gaoler. The chair remained after he left, its legs planted where there had scarcely been room for his knees.

By the time Tomas arrived that afternoon, Mara had turned the visit into an opening address.

“He threatened me,” she said.

Tomas put his papers on the chair. “Tell me his words.”

She gave him most of them. As she repeated the offer, she began to hear how it would sound before the tribunal: the councilor demanding punishment in public, bargaining for silence in private. People would turn to look at him. Someone would ask about the book. For once, she would not be the only person required to answer.

“We begin with that,” she said. “Before they settle into their account of me.”

“Our account must be accurate.”

“It is accurate.”

“Then it can withstand being examined without embellishment.”

“He came here because he is frightened.”

“Possibly. His visit gives us reason to question his motives. The entries give us grounds to seek scrutiny of the charges. Neither proves every allegation against him.”

“You sound as though you’re defending him.”

“I am trying to keep you from promising yourself what this can do.”

She stood, although there was nowhere to walk except between the bed and the door.

“If they see what the Exchange was doing—”

“They may still convict you.”

“I know that.”

“If Saye supports clemency, they may still refuse it. If we expose him, they may punish him and sentence you severely. There is no arrangement of these facts that guarantees your life.”

She stopped beside the window. In the yard, a man was sweeping rainwater away from a drain. Each stroke pushed a little of it uphill before it returned.

“Saye’s wrongdoing matters,” Tomas said more quietly. “So does the evidence you kept. But neither removes your responsibility for the fire.”

She pressed her thumb into the soft mortar between two stones.

“What will they ask?”

“What you intended. What you knew about the building. Whether you knew people would still be working.”

She could have told him then. The cleaners after the buyers left. The clerks carrying books upstairs. Her certainty that they would see the danger and escape, accepted without a single question put to anyone who worked those hours.

Instead she said, “I knew the evening auctions ran late.”

Tomas waited.

She turned from the window. “Write down Saye’s exact words first.”

He watched her a moment longer, then opened his notebook.

That evening she rehearsed how she would describe the councilor’s visit. She removed nothing that exposed him. When she reached the questions Tomas had asked, she shortened her answers until they could be spoken without hesitation.

Mara rehearsed the answers until their brevity felt like steadiness.

What had she intended? The destruction of the registers.

Had she expected anyone to die? No.

In the silence after that answer, she heard the chain again: one link striking the Exchange’s side door, a small sound beneath the auction bell.

The narrow court had smelled of wet brick and old cabbage. Light from the high auction windows fell across the paving, too far above her to reveal more than shadows passing behind the glass. She had tested the chain once, pulling until it tightened between the door handle and the iron staple in the wall. The door opened only a finger’s width.

She knew about the evening cleaners. She had watched them on other nights, carrying buckets past buyers still arguing over prices. The Exchange did not empty when the bidding ended. Work continued behind the public rooms: floors to scrub, books to carry upstairs, accounts to reconcile.

Daniel came into the court while she was putting away the key.

“What have you done?”

“It will slow the guards.”

He took hold of the chain. “Take it off.”

“If they come out here, they can reach the water steps before I do. This makes them go round.”

“There are still people inside, Mara. Cleaners. I saw them on the landing.”

“I know.”

She remembered saying it. Not misunderstanding him, not failing to hear beneath the bell. She had answered.

“Then leave them a way out.”

“The front doors are open.”

“Have you checked?”

She had seen buyers entering less than an hour before. That was not the same as checking the stairs, the passages, the doors between the working rooms and the street. She had not asked where the cleaners went when the auction finished.

“They’ll have time,” she said.

Daniel looked toward the upper windows. A clerk’s silhouette crossed one of them with a stack of ledgers against his chest.

“We can still stop.”

She could still take out the key. There would be no pursuit if she abandoned the plan. She imagined instead the registers intact the next morning, another day’s charges entered in their ruled columns, and felt her hesitation harden into anger.

She wanted him to speak about the families turned out over invented debts. That was the conversation she knew how to have. Instead he kept his hand on the chain.

“Move away from the door,” she told him.

She did not know that everyone could escape. She knew they might not, and chose to proceed. The reassurance came afterward, something she repeated as she walked toward the other entrance: there would be time.

By morning they had moved her to the interview room. Only a few hours remained before the hearing. Tomas sat beside the table when the gaoler admitted Iona. Her shawl was dark with rain at the shoulders. She remained standing until the door closed, then sat opposite Mara.

The statement Mara had dictated lay between them.

“I need you to tell them why,” Mara said. “What the Exchange did to us.”

“I can tell them what I know.”

“We stood in the same queues. You remember the winter charges, being told we owed more every time we paid. You know what Saye made of our lives.”

Iona loosened her shawl but did not remove it. “I remember.”

“Then support the account. Tell them it was against the registers. That I wasn’t trying to hurt our own people.”

“Did you ask me here to tell me what to say?”

“I’m asking you not to help them hang me.”

“I oppose hanging you. I’ve already said that.”

“But if you speak against me—”

“I will speak truthfully. Where that helps you, and where it doesn’t.”

Mara pushed the statement across the table. Iona did not touch it.

“After everything we’ve both suffered,” Mara said, “surely you owe me that much.”

Iona’s face changed, not into anger but something more tired.

“No. What was done to us does not put me in your debt. I don’t forgive you, Mara. I don’t have to forgive you to say they mustn’t kill you.”

“They’ll use what you say.”

“Then let them use the truth. I’m not giving you different testimony because you’re frightened of it.”

Beyond the door, keys rattled.

Mara had counted on Iona’s opposition to execution as though it were agreement about the fire. She had imagined the words fitted together: what they had suffered, what she had intended, why she should live. Iona had separated them without raising her voice.

When the gaoler returned, Iona rose. Mara almost reached for her sleeve, then drew her hand back.

After she had gone, Mara stared at the untouched statement.

“If I give them everything,” she asked Tomas, “will it make a difference to the sentence?”

“I will ask them to consider your honesty. I cannot promise what they will decide.”

“And Saye?”

“The evidence against him matters. It may establish abuses that deserve punishment and claims that deserve repayment. It does not remove your responsibility for the fire.”

“If I tell them things they haven’t proved?”

“That is a decision you must make without a promise about the sentence.”

She looked at him, waiting for the qualification. None came.

On the paper, her account still looked firm and economical. She had chosen each sentence with an answer in mind: agreement from Iona, recognition from Lowbank, mercy from the tribunal. Now Iona had left, Tomas was waiting, and none of those answers was hers to require.

She silently rehearsed the answer about the deaths again.

No.

It was the answer to whether she had expected them. She had used it to avoid saying what else she had known.

Across the table, Tomas opened his notebook. Mara watched him find a clean page.

Story:

The auction bell rang twice, and the Exchange filled with the scrape of chairs and the soft, watchful murmur of people bidding against one another. Mara waited until the first lot was called. From the passage behind the counting rooms, she could hear the auctioneer’s practiced brightness carrying through the walls, turning ledgers and deeds into objects with clean edges and prices.

She had chosen the hour because the building would be busy. Smoke, she had reasoned, would be mistaken for dust or a chimney fault until the registers were already burning. She had chosen the records room because the books inside could prove how Saye’s clerks had turned unpaid fees into permanent debts, and how those debts had taken homes from Lowbank families. She had chosen the side exit because guards would use it to follow her.

She had secured it herself.

The fastening was meant to buy her time. She had not meant to trap anyone. But she knew the evening cleaning crew sometimes worked through the auctions, and she knew residents came into the Exchange after closing hours to dispute entries or wait for clerks. Daniel had told her that people remained in the building.

She had heard him. She had gone ahead.

At the time, she told herself there would be enough warning. The auction would break up; the workers would leave. The fire would be small until the registers were gone. She arranged those thoughts as neatly as columns in a book, each one covering the space where a question might have appeared. If anyone was still inside when the smoke spread, there would be time to get them out.

Then the first dark ribbon slipped beneath the counting-room door.

For a breath, she watched it curl along the floor, slight and almost delicate. She had expected smoke. She had pictured it rising in a thin veil, something she could manage. This smoke thickened as she watched, blackening the lamplight and swallowing the far end of the passage. The alarm bell began. Beyond the walls, the auctioneer’s voice broke off.

A woman cried out. Someone shouted for the stair.

Mara ran toward the side exit. The passage was already filling. She found the fastening by touch and pulled at it, then struck it with the heel of her hand. The metal held. Her fingers slipped; she tried again, coughing hard enough to fold at the waist. She had brought the key. She had brought every tool she thought she might need to escape. None of it made the fastening yield.

She could hear people beyond the door, or thought she could. The smoke made every sound uncertain: a cough could become a shout; a shout, the groan of timber. She worked the lock until the heat drove her backward. When she reached the main passage, a clerk stumbled through the haze with one sleeve over his mouth. He told her there were people above, that the upper rooms were filling.

Daniel was there.

She remembered his face more clearly than the words. Soot had marked one cheek; his eyes were wet from smoke, fixed on hers with a recognition that seemed to take in everything she had refused to say. He turned toward the stairs. She followed, but they could not reach the upper rooms. The smoke had packed the stairwell and blurred the landings into a single dark throat.

Mara tried another way. She hauled at a door until its frame shuddered. She called for the people she had assumed would have time, though by then she could hardly draw breath. Her rescue was a series of late, useless acts: a door forced too slowly, a stair attempted too late, names spoken into smoke that gave nothing back.

Daniel died. So did the workers and residents who had been inside.

Outside, the crowd was already gathered in the square. At first they cheered as flames showed through the Exchange windows. The registers, they shouted, were burning. Mara had wanted that sound. She had wanted to hear the old accounts consumed, the charges made impossible to collect.

Then someone came out carrying an injured clerk. The cheering stumbled. A woman called for her brother. The crowd began to understand that the fire had not distinguished between paper and people.

For years Mara had remembered the noise as approval. She had held it in her mind as proof that the city had known what the Exchange deserved. Now she could hear what she had edited out: fear in the cries, grief beneath the first shouts, people calling names they would never hear answered. Their anger had not been consent. Their suffering had not made the dead expendable.

In the tribunal chamber, the benches were full. The air held damp wool, old wood, and the dry smell of papers handled too often. Mara stood between Tomas and the clerk’s table. Her dictated statement lay open before her, its sentences polished smooth by repetition.

She had prepared to say that she wanted the registers destroyed, not the building. That she had believed everyone would be out. That Saye’s abuses had left Lowbank with no lawful remedy. Each sentence had been built to carry her toward a conclusion: that the fire had become something beyond her intention, and that the people listening should therefore be merciful.

She looked at the pages and saw how carefully she had arranged the gaps.

“I will not read that,” she said.

The presiding officer waited. Tomas did not speak. Mara felt the room turn its attention toward her, and understood that there was no answer she could make that would restore command over what followed.

“I planned the fire at the Exchange,” she said. “I intended to destroy the registers. I knew the evening cleaning crew might still be inside. I knew residents sometimes remained there after the auction. Daniel warned me that people were in the building. I heard him. I secured the side exit because I wanted to delay anyone who might pursue me, and I set the fire anyway.”

The words sounded smaller than the act. She did not try to make them large enough.

“Saye’s abuses explain what those records contained. They do not explain my decision to burn the building. I cannot speak for Lowbank, and I do not ask anyone here to treat its suffering as my defense.”

She paused. The presiding officer’s face gave her nothing to read.

“There is a reconciliation book that survived. I kept it after the fire. It records the original charges beside the amounts Saye’s clerks added, with dates and names. I can identify the related account rolls and correspondence. Together, they may substantiate unlawful charges and compensation claims.”

She set her hands flat on the table.

“That is what those records can prove. It is not a reason to absolve me.”

The tribunal chamber was cold despite the crowded benches. Pale light fell through its high windows, catching dust above the clerks’ tables. Mara sat with her hands still beside the confession she had refused to read. Its careful gaps remained visible at the edge of her sight.

When Iona Pell was called, she stood without looking at Mara.

“I will testify about the Exchange,” she said. “I came because its routes and conditions belong in the record. Not because Mara asked me.”

Mara felt the urge to answer, to remind Iona that she knew the building better than most. She let the impulse pass. Iona had chosen to speak; Mara had no claim on the choice.

Iona described the Exchange’s public entrance, the auction hall, and the narrow service stair behind the east gallery. Beyond it lay the lower packing rooms and a delivery passage leading to the yard. Carts were often left across that passage. Waste paper and packing straw were stored beside the lower stair. The building had been arranged to move goods, she said, not to empty quickly in an emergency.

“The western side door was nearest the records rooms,” she continued. “Its latch could be secured from outside.”

The presiding officer asked whether the Exchange had posted fire instructions.

“There was a notice by the public entrance. It named the front doors and the yard passage, but said nothing about deliveries blocking the passage or the side door being locked. A notice is not an exit.”

Iona was exact about the limits of her knowledge. She distinguished what she had seen from what she had learned through the duty rolls and workers’ complaints. When asked about Daniel, she paused, pressing her thumb against her forefinger.

“He warned Mara that people were still inside. Mara has admitted that. I will not soften it.” Iona looked toward the tribunal, not toward Mara. “After the fire began, I saw Daniel at the western door, trying to work the latch from within. Smoke had reached the gallery. I did not see him die. He did not come out.”

Elias, she said, had been assigned to the lower packing rooms. The duty roll placed him there; the search record placed his body beyond the service stair. She had not seen his last moments and would not claim otherwise.

“I want the deaths recorded accurately. Daniel went back toward people inside after the warning. Elias was below, at his post. Neither death is a lesson for Mara or a consolation for me. They were people, not arguments.”

Mara lowered her eyes to the table. She could still hear Daniel’s warning as it had sounded that night: clear enough to understand. Iona’s words gave her no new fact to hide behind.

The presiding officer asked whether Iona wished to speak about the sentence.

“Yes. I oppose execution. I opposed it before this hearing, and I oppose it now. Killing Mara will not restore Daniel or Elias, and I will not ask the tribunal to kill someone for me.”

Then Iona turned to Mara. Her expression held no softness, but neither did it ask to be mistaken for vengeance.

“I am angry with her. She chose to set the fire after Daniel warned her. I do not forgive her, and forgiveness is not the price of keeping her alive. I ask for lifelong confinement because I oppose capital punishment. I also ask you to record the Exchange’s unsafe conditions and unlawful practices, and require that they be remedied. The households harmed by them need evidence they can use to seek compensation.”

The tribunal adjourned before noon. In a side room facing a stone wall, clerks laid out the reconciliation book, account rolls, and letters recovered from Saye’s office. Mara was brought in under guard and seated at the end of the table.

A clerk read the dates aloud while Mara matched each entry in the book to the relevant household in the rolls and the corresponding letter or receipt. The original charge stood beside the added sum. The entries showed repeated surcharges; some households had paid twice, while others had been threatened with losing their place at the Exchange if they complained. Mara marked the documents that matched and identified those that needed further checking.

Saye had publicly denied collecting the extra charges. The records gave the tribunal grounds to scrutinize his business and gave affected households evidence for compensation claims. They did not make Mara’s crime smaller. No account could unburn the Exchange.

When the hearing resumed, the presiding officer announced that Saye’s denial was contradicted by the book and correspondence. The tribunal would refer the evidence for further inquiry. Then it addressed Mara’s sentence.

Her conviction stood. She had deliberately set the fire, the officer said, after Daniel warned her that people remained inside. Her cooperation in identifying and verifying the records was sustained and useful, but it did not erase the deaths or excuse her decision.

The death sentence was commuted to lifelong confinement. The tribunal cited proportional punishment and the public interest in confinement over execution. It did not describe the ruling as redemption.

Mara listened until the officer finished. Across the chamber, Iona gathered her coat and turned toward the door. Mara did not call after her. Iona’s anger remained hers, and the sentence changed nothing about what Mara had done.

The tribunal doors opened onto a wet morning. Rain stippled the courtyard stones as guards brought Mara down the steps, her wrists bound. Her sentence had changed; the iron had not.

Iona waited beneath the arch with several Lowbank families. She had come to see Mara transferred, not to bid her farewell. When Mara looked toward her, Iona held her gaze.

“Keep the Exchange accounts with the tribunal’s record,” Iona told the clerk. “And the names of everyone who died.”

“They’ll be included,” he said, sheltering his papers beneath his coat. “The ruling refers the books and letters for inquiry.”

“Then make sure the inquiry reaches the households.”

The guards led Mara through the gate. Iona felt no relief. She had opposed execution because she would not ask for another person to be killed in her name. Lifelong confinement was not forgiveness, and it restored nothing to Lowbank. Her anger remained, clear and unsoftened.

By midday, she was in the registry room helping prepare the first compensation claims. The room smelled of wet wool and stove coal. On the table lay copies of the Exchange accounts, listing extra charges, duplicate payments, and threats in Saye’s letters. Iona checked the names with two Lowbank women: a widow charged twice, a family threatened with losing its place, a cooper whose goods had been held until he paid again.

The list was useful, but one page ended with blank lines. Iona tapped the margin. “Who checked the households that aren’t named in these rolls?”

The clerk glanced at the account book. “The claims come from the recovered records. Without an entry, I don’t know what to put down.”

A man by the door stepped forward, his hands clenched at his sides. His wife stood beside him, holding a folded statement. “We were at the Exchange that night,” he said. “Our son was inside.”

Iona remembered them from the hearing, seated in the back row. The tribunal’s account of the fire named their son among the dead. It also recorded the obstructed passage, unsafe lamps, and delayed doors. Those failures demanded scrutiny; they did not erase Mara’s deliberate act or the warnings she had ignored.

The mother unfolded the statement. A neighbor had seen their son enter the Exchange and had not seen him leave. A note from their landlord confirmed the boy had worked to help pay the family’s rent.

“The accounts show what Saye took,” Iona said. “They don’t name every household harmed by the Exchange.”

The clerk studied the form. “It asks for the charge or payment in dispute.”

“Then it’s too narrow. This is a claim for their loss. The tribunal’s own account records his death, and these witnesses support it. Accept it for review without putting it in the same column as unlawful charges.”

A senior registrar came over and checked the boy’s name against the tribunal record. He asked the parents to confirm the spelling. The mother did, watching his pen as he wrote it down.

“We can’t award compensation today,” the registrar said. “This isn’t a ruling on liability. But we can file the claim for review with its supporting statements.”

“File it,” Iona said.

He stamped the papers. The mother flinched at the sharp sound, then accepted the stamped copy. The clerk entered the family on a separate page for deaths and related losses. It was no award and no promise, but their claim now had a place in the record.

More households arrived with receipts, letters, and witness statements. Some had been overcharged; others had lost work when their goods were withheld. The bereaved parents sat among them, their copy laid carefully on the table. Iona helped sort the papers and corrected names where the account rolls had misspelled them.

They drafted a joint petition asking the tribunal for a hearing on remedies for the Exchange’s safety failures. It named the blocked passage and dangerous lamps and requested an inquiry into who had been responsible for maintenance and enforcement. The petition did not challenge Mara’s conviction. The fire had taken lives; the conditions that preceded it also required an accounting.

Iona read the petition aloud, corrected one date, and made sure the bereaved family’s witness statements were listed among the attachments. Then she signed beneath the households’ names.

The registrar accepted the bundle and marked it for consideration. Outside, the rain had thinned to mist. The families waited on the steps with their copies protected beneath their coats. Their claims had only begun, and the hearing had not been granted. Still, the names were recorded and the petition was in the tribunal’s hands. No one had to mistake a sentence for an accounting.

Several weeks after the petition was filed, a warder brought Mara to a room overlooking the prison laundry yard. Sheets hung between the walls, their lower edges grey with water. Somewhere below, a woman was beating cloth against a board. The blows came in pairs, with a pause between.

A stranger sat at the table with three bundles of paper. She had placed a clean handkerchief beneath the smallest bundle to keep soot from the others.

“My name is Sella Venn. I’m acting for several households named in the compensation petition.”

Mara sat. The warder remained beside the door.

“Has a hearing been granted?”

“No.”

Sella untied the largest bundle. Her fingers were reddened around the nails, and one cuff had been repaired with thread darker than the cloth.

“The registrar has asked us to clarify discrepancies. I need to ask you about the side exit.”

Mara looked at the papers. She had spent the walk from her cell preparing for a different conversation. There were things she could say about the Exchange, about the warnings ignored and the goods withheld, about how long a grievance could remain lawful and still go unheard. She knew how those sentences joined together. Even now, they arranged themselves readily.

Sella drew a narrow sheet from the bundle.

“This concerns a family whose daughter was found near the side passage. Their claim includes evidence that the exit could not be used. There are conflicting accounts of when you reached it.”

“What do you need to know?”

“Whether you tried it more than once. What you saw. Whether anyone else was there.”

In the yard, the wet cloth struck the board again.

“Once,” Mara said. “I tried it once.”

Sella made a mark.

“A summary attached to the surviving watch report says you attempted to open the exit while the fire was still confined to the place where it began. Before it spread through the hall.”

She read the next line silently, holding the page close to the window.

“That would put your attempt before people were trapped in the passage. Is that correct?”

Mara could see a bead of water working down the glass. It stopped against a flaw, swelled, and continued.

There it was: an account in which she had tried sooner. She would still have started the fire, but she would have reached for the door before knowing what she had done to the people inside. Someone reading quickly might even believe she had taken precautions.

She had not invented the error. A clerk had copied something incorrectly, or a witness had confused one movement with another. She could say she did not remember the precise sequence. There had been smoke. There had been shouting. No one could reasonably expect every moment to have remained distinct.

Sella waited with her pen above the paper.

“No,” Mara said.

“The fire had spread?”

“Yes.”

“How far?”

“I couldn’t see the whole hall by then.”

Sella lowered the page.

“Then tell me what you can place in order.”

Mara pressed her hands together beneath the table. She had once imagined that speaking plainly would become easier after the first time, as though a confession cleared a passage through which later words could travel. Instead, each question seemed to find some new obstruction.

“I set the fire deliberately,” she said. “Afterward, I heard people at the side passage. I understood they couldn’t get out. Then I went to the exit and tried to open it.”

“You knew they were trapped before you tried?”

“Yes.”

“And you couldn’t open it.”

“No.”

Sella crossed out a line. Her nib caught in the paper, leaving a small dark tear.

“Was that knowledge based on something you saw, or something someone said?”

Mara swallowed. This was the question she disliked most: the one that required her to go back inside.

“I heard someone calling for room. Then someone shouted that the door wouldn’t open. When I reached them, people were pressed together.”

“Did you see the daughter named here?”

Sella turned the sheet. A name had been written in a careful hand beneath the copied statement.

“I don’t know.”

“Look at the description.”

Mara read it twice. A blue dress, a missing front tooth, hair tied with brown tape. The details belonged to an ordinary morning, to someone preparing a child to leave home.

“I can’t say that I did.”

Sella wrote that down.

There was no thanks for the correction. She did not tell Mara that honesty would count in her favour. She moved the mistaken summary aside and began another page.

“Did the door move at all?”

“I felt it give a little. I couldn’t tell whether it was the door or the frame.”

“Did you see what prevented it opening?”

“No.”

“Then we must leave that uncertain.”

Mara nodded.

They continued. Which direction had the door been pushed? Where had Mara stood? Had she seen anyone outside? Could she distinguish the sound of something falling from the sound of the crowd striking the wood?

Twice she said she did not remember. Once Sella read back an answer, and Mara corrected a word. Beyond the window, the laundry worker dragged a basket across the stones.

At last Sella put the amended statement before her.

“Read it before signing.”

The sequence occupied only a few lines. Mara had deliberately started the fire. She had become aware that people were trapped. She had then attempted, unsuccessfully, to reopen the side exit.

Nothing in the wording explained Lowbank. Nothing described the Exchange’s refusals or the anger she had carried into the hall. Those matters had their places in other papers. Here, the order of three actions needed to be accurate.

She signed.

“Will the family receive anything?” she asked.

“I don’t know. The claim is still under review.”

Sella blotted the signature and returned the papers to their bundles.

The warder opened the door. As Mara rose, Sella noticed an omission.

“One more thing. When you reached the passage, was the lamp above the exit still alight?”

Mara had already turned towards the corridor. For a moment she wanted to keep walking, to make the signed page the end of what could be required of her that morning.

She came back to the table.

“I remember light,” she said. “I can’t tell you whether it came from the lamp.”

Sella took up her pen again.

Several weeks later, Sella laid the surviving records across the table between them. The room smelled of damp wool and the coal smoke that seeped through the courthouse walls. Rain traced thin, wavering lines down the window. Mara sat with her hands folded, careful not to touch the papers until Sella asked her to.

The claim concerned the households whose charges had been collected to cover losses that belonged to other people. Some had kept receipts; others had only entries in ledgers, names copied into the margins, or neighbors willing to attest to what they had paid. Sella had brought the records that remained after the fire, along with Mara’s signed statement and a summary prepared from an earlier account.

“I want to confirm the sequence before I send this for review,” she said. Her tone was neither kind nor hostile. It was the tone of someone making sure a column added correctly. “This summary says you tried to reopen the side exit before the fire had spread. Then, when you saw people were trapped, you left the door.”

Mara looked down at the page. The sentence was short enough to seem settled. It made her action sound like an attempt to help that had come too early to matter.

“That is wrong,” she said.

Sella stopped with her pencil above the paper. “Which part?”

“I started the fire first. I knew people were still inside. I tried the exit later, after I understood they were trapped.”

Sella glanced at the signed statement, then at the summary. “The wording may have been carried forward from the first account.”

“Then it shouldn’t be carried forward again.”

There was no satisfaction in correcting it. The true order did not make the fire worse than it had been, or bring anyone back. It only removed the thin, accidental kindness the mistake had given her.

Sella drew a line through the sentence. “Tell me what you remember. Not what you learned afterward.”

Mara watched the pencil move. “I remember the door giving a little when I pushed. I couldn’t tell whether the door moved or the frame did. I didn’t see what held it.”

“Did you hear anyone on the other side?”

“I heard noise. I can’t say whether it was people striking the wood or something falling.”

Sella wrote that down. She asked where Mara had stood, which way she had pushed, whether the lamp above the exit had been alight. Mara said again that she remembered light but could not say where it came from. Sella did not offer her a guess to choose from.

“What did you understand before you reached the exit?” she asked.

“I understood there were people who couldn’t get out.”

“Before or after you started the fire?”

“After I started it.”

The answer seemed to tighten the room. Rain ticked against the glass; somewhere in the corridor a key turned, then scraped free. Mara had rehearsed an explanation on the walk from her cell. She had intended to speak of the Exchange’s accounts, of households made to carry debts that were not theirs, of refusals repeated until refusal seemed to be the only language anyone there understood. The facts were real. They were also not the answer.

Sella waited.

“Why did you wait before trying the door?” she asked.

Mara pressed her thumb against the side of her forefinger. She had pictured saying that she believed she could control the fire, that she had not known how quickly it would take. Both things were true in pieces, and neither answered why she had not moved sooner.

“I wanted the fire to do what I had started it to do,” she said. “I was watching the people and the papers. I thought about what would happen to the Exchange. I did not make the people inside my first thought.” She looked at the corrected line. “I waited because I had not yet decided to stop what I had done.”

Sella’s pencil paused, but she did not look up.

Mara could have added that she had finally gone to the exit. She could have made the failed attempt stand beside the choice to set the fire, as if one might balance the other. She said nothing until Sella asked whether she had seen anyone trying to get out.

“I remember people moving,” Mara said. “I can’t tell you who. I can’t tell you what each of them understood. I know what I understood, and when.”

Sella recorded the distinction. There was no thanks in her face, and Mara had not expected any. The answers might help the households prove what had happened, or they might leave gaps too large for anyone to cross. The claims remained under review.

“Does correcting this change what they can receive?” Mara asked.

“It may change how the account is understood. I can’t tell you what decision will follow.”

“And my sentence?”

“Remains in force.”

Sella turned the revised page toward her. The amended statement set out the order plainly: Mara had deliberately started the fire; afterward, knowing people were trapped, she had attempted to reopen the side exit and failed. It did not set out the Exchange’s grievances. It did not claim that her attempt repaired anything. The households’ accounts were elsewhere in the file, among figures and names that had not yet been reconciled.

“Read it through,” Sella said. “Mark anything that isn’t accurate.”

Mara read every line. She changed “heard the door break” to “heard a noise at the door,” because she had not heard it break. She left the rest untouched. At the bottom, beside the space for her confirmation, the paper waited without emphasis.

She signed. Sella blotted the ink and placed the page with the others. No one offered a hand. No one asked her to say she was sorry again.

When the warder opened the door, Mara rose. The claim would go forward without a promise; the sentence would continue without alteration. The correction had not made her more deserving of pardon. It had only made the record less false.

She went with the warder, carrying no copy. Behind her, Sella returned to the ledgers, where the names still had to be checked one by one.
