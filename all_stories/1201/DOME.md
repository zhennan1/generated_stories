The first thing Daniel Reed heard was not a sentence but its pieces: a cart wheel ticking over a seam in the corridor, a voice lowered to a murmur, the clink of metal. The monitor beside his bed answered with a measured beep. The room smelled sharply of antiseptic, with a faint papery scent beneath it. When he tried to shift, pain flared across his abdomen, then broke into smaller, bright fragments.

At the Junction, the Brain was already standing at the table. A recovery schedule lay before him, marked in narrow handwriting: settle the breathing; rest; answer necessary questions; let the medication work; attempt a small movement when instructed. Boxes waited beside each task.

“We cannot afford confusion,” he said. “The body has been through emergency surgery. We follow the plan and conserve energy.”

The Heart, beating hard in its chamber, studied the list. “You have made a plan for hours that haven’t happened.”

“That is what a plan is for.”

“And where have you put fear?”

“Fear is not a task.”

“No,” said the Heart. “It is a signal.”

Around them, the other organs stirred in the Junction’s dimness. The lungs wanted slow breaths, but the abdomen’s guarded muscles resisted. The stomach was empty and sour. The hands, heavy beneath the blanket, had no intention of reaching. Each part seemed to be waiting for instructions, yet each had its own sense of what mattered.

“Pain is expected,” the Brain said. “Attending to every sensation will make it harder to rest.”

“Daniel is trying not to attend to it already,” the Heart replied. “That doesn’t mean he is all right.”

A beep sounded through the room. The Brain marked the first task complete.

Daniel opened his eyes to the pale ceiling and looked toward the bedside table. The water cup stood beyond his reach. Asking for it seemed to require more energy than he had.

He had always preferred to manage things himself: the late train, the broken latch, the difficult call made instead of postponed. Now a tube ran beneath his gown, a dressing covered his abdomen, and every movement seemed to belong to someone else’s instructions. The pain had a center beneath the dressing, but it spread when he breathed and sharpened when he considered moving. He pressed his lips together and waited for it to pass.

The door opened softly. Nurse Elena Park checked the monitor and the line in his arm, then looked at him. “How are you doing right now?”

“Fine,” he said.

The word came out dry and small. He watched her straighten the tubing, hoping she would accept it.

“Fine can mean a few different things after a night like this,” Elena said. “Are you comfortable enough to rest?”

Daniel gave a half shrug, then stopped when the movement tugged at his abdomen. “It’s not too bad.”

At the Junction, the Brain lifted his pencil. Manageable, he thought. The schedule could hold.

The Heart struck the table with its palm. “Ask again.”

Elena drew the chair closer. “You don’t need to give me a perfect number. What do you notice? Is it aching, sharp, burning?”

Daniel stared at the blanket. “Mostly aching. It catches when I breathe deep or move.”

“Thank you. That helps me understand.” She explained that pain after surgery was expected, but that they wanted it controlled enough for him to rest and breathe comfortably. He should tell her if it changed or became harder to manage. She described what she would check and what the medication could do, without promising it would erase the pain.

“How bad is it?” she asked.

He drew a careful breath. The ache tightened under the dressing. “Maybe a six when I move. Less if I stay still.”

The Heart leaned toward the Brain. “The signal is useful.”

“It is one answer,” the Brain said. “Not a reason to abandon the plan.”

Elena asked whether he felt nauseated, dizzy, or short of breath. He said no, then hesitated. He was frightened, though the word seemed too large to offer. The operation had happened quickly. He remembered being told there was no time to wait, the bright operating-room lights, then waking in a body he could not trust. Even the monitor’s steady tones made him wonder what might go wrong.

“I don’t know what’s normal,” he said.

“That makes sense,” Elena replied. “You can ask. You don’t have to figure it out alone.”

Daniel nodded. “I’m just tired.”

It was true, but not the whole truth. He left the fear folded beneath the exhaustion, where it still seemed manageable.

Elena adjusted his pillow and placed the call button in his hand. She told him to use it if he needed help and not to try getting up alone. He tested the button’s weight in his palm. When she left, the door clicked almost silently behind her.

The Brain returned to the schedule. “We have a plan. Rest now. No further analysis.”

The Heart listened to Elena’s footsteps recede. “He told her more than he meant to.”

“Not enough to change the course.”

“Enough to begin.”

Neither moved from the table. The lungs continued their cautious work; the abdomen continued to hurt. Daniel held the call button without pressing it. Corridor voices came and went. He drifted toward sleep and surfaced when the ache tightened, when a cart passed, when a voice rose and vanished beyond the door.

Each time he woke, the Brain’s timetable was waiting. But the body arrived before the plan: dry mouth, stiff shoulders, soreness with every breath, a pulse quickening at sounds he could not place. Rest required vigilance, and vigilance made rest difficult. The boxes remained mostly empty.

Near dawn, a pale strip of light appeared at the window. Daniel had slept in short, shallow intervals, never long enough to lose the room entirely. The pain was still there, quieter when he lay still but never gone. He felt wrung out.

At the Junction, the Brain studied the page. One task had been delayed; another needed repeating. The neat columns no longer matched the night.

“Revise it,” said the Heart.

The Brain picked up his pencil but did not write. “We will proceed one step at a time.”

It was neither agreement nor refusal. Daniel closed his eyes. When Elena returned, he would tell her if the pain worsened. He was not sure he would tell her how frightened he was. For now, his body gathered what rest it could, while its two leaders kept watch over a morning neither had planned.

Morning came slowly through the narrow gap in the curtains, turning the hospital room from charcoal to gray. Daniel had slept in pieces. Each time he woke, he had found the same ache beneath the bandage, the same dry taste in his mouth, the same monitor making its measured claim on the quiet. By the time Elena Park came in, his eyes felt gritty and his thoughts had the sluggishness of a roomful of furniture dragged out of place.

“Good morning,” she said, checking the monitor before she looked at him. “How was the night?”

“Fine,” Daniel began, then stopped. The word had come out by habit, polished smooth from use. He tried again. “Not very restful.”

Elena drew the curtain a little farther open. “Thank you. How’s the pain right now?”

He looked toward the window. Beyond it, the morning was colorless, rooftops damp from overnight rain. “It’s there.”

At the Junction, the Brain tapped the schedule with his pencil. The columns had been orderly once. Now the night’s interruptions crowded their margins.

“Vague answers are not data,” he said. “We need a reliable assessment before attempting the next task. Check the plan. Complete the planned sitting. Then reassess.”

The Heart leaned against the table, arms crossed. “He’s exhausted. Don’t make him prove he can follow a timetable.”

“We cannot let a sensation dictate the day.”

“Nor can we pretend the sensation isn’t there.”

Elena asked Daniel to rate his pain from zero to ten, explaining that the number did not need to be perfect; what mattered was what had changed and what made it better or worse. He remembered saying six when she had asked about moving during the night. The number had been honest, but it had not told the whole story.

“About a four when I’m still,” he said. “More when I move. Last night I told you it was six if I moved, but I didn’t say how often it woke me. Or that I was trying not to let it.”

Elena’s expression stayed calm, but her attention sharpened. She asked where he felt the pain, whether it had changed, and whether he felt nauseated or short of breath. Daniel answered slowly, surprised by how much effort it took to describe a body he usually treated as a dependable instrument. The pain was centered near the incision; it pulled when he shifted. No new sharp pain, he said. Breathing was uncomfortable, but he could take a deeper breath if he went carefully.

“Good. We’ll take this one step at a time,” Elena said. “I’ll check you before we try sitting. You can tell me to pause at any point.”

The Brain lifted his pencil. “The sequence must remain controlled. No improvisation.”

The Heart glanced at Daniel’s clenched hand. “And no abandoning him in the middle of it.”

Elena checked his observations and asked him to bend his knees, then talked him through turning onto his side. Daniel pressed a pillow gently against his abdomen as she had shown him. He moved with care, yet the shift tugged deep beneath the dressing. His breath caught before he could hide it.

“Pause,” he said.

Elena stopped at once. She waited while he took a few measured breaths, then asked whether the pain was easing. Daniel nodded. Once it had settled, they continued, Elena supporting his shoulders as he pushed himself up with his arms. The bed rose behind him. He reached the edge and sat with his feet hanging above the floor.

For a moment, the room held steady. Then its edges softened. A lightness opened behind his eyes, and the monitor’s regular tones seemed to drift farther away. His abdomen tightened as he tried to correct his posture.

At the Junction, the Heart stepped forward. “Stay with it. The first moments are the hardest.”

The Lungs drew a shallow breath and shook their head. “Not if he can’t breathe without bracing.”

The Muscles, pale with effort, gripped the table. “We’re trembling. That isn’t strength.”

“His balance is compromised,” said the Brain, suddenly sharper. He pressed the schedule flat with both hands. “Stop the attempt. Return to the bed. We need tighter control, not guesses.”

The Heart’s jaw tightened. “You wanted him to sit.”

“I wanted him to follow the plan.”

Daniel heard Elena ask if he was dizzy. The question reached him through the haze, clear enough to answer.

“Yes. The room’s moving a little. And the pain’s worse now—maybe six.”

“Thank you for telling me.” She kept one hand steady at his back. “We’re going to lie you down again. Don’t try to stand.”

They moved slowly, Elena guiding him onto his side and easing him back against the pillows. The dizziness receded by degrees. The pain remained, but stopped climbing. Daniel closed his eyes until the room settled into its familiar arrangement: window, chair, IV pole, door.

Elena checked his symptoms again and told him she would update the surgical team before they decided what came next. “This doesn’t mean you’ve failed,” she said. “It means we learned how your body responded. We’ll let the team know about the dizziness and the increase in pain.”

Daniel opened his eyes. “Does it mean I shouldn’t try again today?”

“We’ll talk it through with them. They may want us to wait, reassess, or try again later with a different pace. I’ll come back with their guidance.”

He wanted to ask if something had gone wrong inside him. He nearly asked whether the dizziness meant the surgery had caused a new problem. Instead, he chose the question he could get out. “What should I watch for while I’m lying here?”

Elena explained which changes to report promptly, including worsening pain or new symptoms, and reminded him to use the call button rather than getting up alone. Daniel glanced at the button beside his hand. It seemed less like a confession now and more like a tool whose purpose had been clearly stated.

“I’ll tell you if it changes,” he said. “And if I start feeling worse.”

“Good. You don’t have to wait until you’re certain something is wrong.”

When she left to contact the team, the Brain returned to the schedule and began crossing out the morning’s neat assumptions. “We need a tighter assessment before the next attempt.”

The Heart watched Daniel’s hand settle beside the call button. “And we need to listen before we ask him to push.”

Neither conceded the point. But the Brain left a blank space where the next task had been, and the Heart did not fill it with a demand for courage. Daniel lay back, tired and disappointed, yet less alone with the disappointment than he had been the night before. When the ache shifted, he noticed it. When the dizziness lingered, he did not pretend it was nothing. The schedule would have to wait for the body’s answer.

By early afternoon, the square of sunlight beneath Daniel’s window had moved beyond the foot of his bed. His legs still felt heavy from the morning’s walk, though he had traveled no farther than the doorway and back. Whenever he closed his eyes, he remembered the chair arriving behind him, the staff waiting patiently while he tried to find enough breath to thank them.

Inside, the Brain studied the blank space in its schedule.

“We won’t attempt another walk without reassessment,” it said. “That much is settled.”

The Heart gave a subdued beat. “He needs time.”

“Yes. But time needn’t be empty.”

The Brain replaced the missing walk with smaller objectives: take the offered sips, cooperate with examinations, change position with help, answer questions clearly. Each could be completed. Each would establish that the day had not been lost.

The Heart wanted to object to that last phrase. Instead, it listened to Daniel’s breathing and wished the disappointment would loosen its grip.

Elena returned while he was watching dust drift through the sunlight.

“I’ve spoken with the team,” she said. “We’re holding off on another walk for now. We’ll keep checking how you’re doing.”

Daniel nodded too quickly. “Probably sensible.”

“How’s the dizziness?”

“Better lying down.”

“And the pain?”

He considered the question. At rest, the sharpness had receded, leaving a deep soreness that seemed to occupy more of him than the incision alone. Moving pulled at it. Remaining still made him increasingly aware of it.

“Manageable,” he said.

Elena drew her chair closer. “Does that mean the medicine is helping, or that you’re trying to manage without telling us?”

His face warmed.

“A bit of both.”

“Then tell me the bit we’re missing.”

He described the pulling when he shifted, and she asked further questions. Was it different from earlier? Did it stay in one place? She examined him carefully, explaining before she touched him. Daniel watched her face for an answer she did not yet have.

“Some soreness and tiredness are expected after what you’ve been through,” she said. “But we still need to hear about changes. Especially if the pain worsens, you feel sick, or something simply feels different.”

The Brain retained the first sentence with particular clarity.

Expected. After what he had been through.

The uncertainty in the rest became a smaller note beneath it.

After Elena helped him settle against the pillows, Daniel took several slow breaths as instructed. The effort tugged at his abdomen. He stopped, rested, and tried again.

“Good,” said the Brain. “Completed.”

“Not a race,” the Heart murmured.

“No one said it was.”

Yet the word completed remained in the chamber like a small flag.

Through the afternoon, the hospital supplied a succession of occasions for such flags. A blood-pressure cuff tightened and released. A thermometer chimed. Elena checked his dressing and asked about his comfort. Daniel accepted help turning slightly onto his side, then back again when the position became tiring.

The numbers were recorded. The dressing was inspected. Another task was finished.

What could not be ticked off so neatly was the weariness afterward.

It seemed unreasonable to Daniel that lying in bed could exhaust him. He had done almost nothing. His telephone lay beside the water cup, carrying messages he had read without answering. Even composing a reassuring sentence felt like work.

“Tell them you’re making progress,” the Heart urged suddenly. “You stood today.”

Daniel picked up the telephone.

Then he remembered how urgently he had wanted the bed.

The Heart fell silent.

He typed, Taking it slowly, and set the telephone down before anyone could reply.

From its place beyond the council’s brighter arguments, the Liver spoke.

“Recovery spends what we have.”

The Brain turned. “We are conserving effort.”

“You are counting activities. That isn’t quite the same thing.”

“Have you something specific to report?”

The Liver hesitated. It had watched the demands accumulate: the operation’s aftermath, the morning’s exertion, the long hours in which restoration proceeded without applause. Usually, the council noticed its work only when it wanted more.

“Only that reserves are finite,” it said.

The Brain waited.

The Liver withdrew into a bitter quiet. If they wanted an account, they might first acknowledge there was an accountant.

“Then we agree on moderation,” the Brain concluded.

They did not agree, but no one corrected it.

Toward evening, Elena asked whether Daniel felt better than he had after the walk.

“Less dizzy,” he answered.

“That’s useful. What about everything else?”

He looked toward the window, where the glass had begun reflecting the room instead of the sky.

“Tired. Sore.”

“More sore?”

“I don’t know. Maybe just noticing it.”

That was true, but incomplete. He could no longer remember the morning’s pain precisely enough to compare it with this hour’s. It had become the background against which every small effort took place.

Elena did not dismiss his uncertainty.

“You don’t have to work out the reason,” she said. “Describe what you notice. We can ask questions from there.”

“I will.”

He meant it. Yet after she left, he tried to decide what would count as worth describing. A stronger pull when he adjusted the blanket? Another hour of fatigue? If he reported every discomfort, would there be anything else to talk about?

By night, the corridor had quieted to rubber soles, distant voices, and the occasional rattle of a cart. During handover, Elena introduced the night nurse and repeated that he should call rather than struggle alone.

Daniel thanked her. He almost added that he still felt thoroughly worn out. But she already knew he was tired.

Inside, the Brain arranged the day’s observations into columns. Less dizziness at rest. Routine care completed. No further walk attempted.

The Heart searched those columns for reassurance and found only the doorway he had barely reached.

The Liver kept its fuller account to itself.

Daniel lay awake beneath the thin blanket, one hand near the call button. He could name each sensation separately. What he could not explain was why, gathered together, they made him feel less certain than he had that afternoon.

Morning entered the room in a pale strip beneath the blind. Daniel had slept in pieces, waking whenever he shifted. Now the breakfast tray stood across his knees, and the smell of warm porridge made him turn his face away.

He had swallowed two spoonfuls. He felt as though he had finished a large meal.

The Stomach had been waiting for him to notice.

“This is different,” it told the council. “The discomfort used to ease when he settled. It is staying now. There is fullness without food, and the nausea is increasing.”

The Brain reviewed the familiar explanations: surgery, medication, inactivity. Each had accounted for something yesterday. Together, they still seemed sufficient.

“Those sensations can occur during recovery,” it said.

“I am describing a change.”

“And I am asking whether the change requires a new explanation.”

The Heart beat impatiently through the exchange. Yesterday’s walk still troubled it: the doorway reached, the knees trembling, the bed received with humiliating relief.

“He needs something to go well,” it said.

The Stomach had no answer to that. It could only send up another dull wave of sickness.

When Elena came in, Daniel was studying the tray as if appetite might return if he waited politely enough.

“Not hungry?”

“A bit queasy.”

“Since when?”

“This morning, mostly.”

She moved the tray aside and asked whether he had vomited, whether he was passing wind, whether the pain had changed. Daniel answered each question separately. No vomiting. He could not remember passing wind overnight. The pain was still in his abdomen.

“Still pulling?”

“Yes,” he said, because pulling remained part of it.

She checked his observations, examined his abdomen, and asked him to call if the nausea persisted or the pain increased.

After she left, he felt relieved that he had mentioned it. He did not realize how little his answers had conveyed.

By late morning, even the water in his cup seemed uninviting. He took small sips because his mouth was dry, then waited for the unsettled feeling to pass.

It did not quite pass.

Inside, the Brain began sorting again.

“We have reported nausea. It is being watched. There is no advantage in attending to it continuously.”

“Attention is not causing it,” said the Stomach.

“No. But attention can make discomfort harder to bear.”

This was reasonable enough to silence the Heart for a moment. Daniel had endured so much already. The Heart wanted courage to be useful again. It urged him to sit higher against the pillows, to wash his face, to greet Elena without sounding defeated.

He managed all three. Afterward, his pulse remained quicker than the effort seemed to deserve.

The Lungs noticed something else. Daniel was ending each breath early. A deeper breath pressed against the soreness, so he stopped short of it, almost without deciding.

“We are having to work around the pain,” they said.

The Brain paused. “Is he breathless?”

“Not exactly. He is guarding.”

The Heart stirred uneasily.

“People do that when they hurt,” it said.

“Yes,” the Lungs replied. They had meant to insist that this mattered alongside the other changes. Instead, hearing the strain in the room, they added, “Perhaps he only needs a more comfortable position.”

Daniel adjusted a pillow. For several minutes, everyone accepted the adjustment as an answer.

At midday, the Liver spoke.

“He has very little to spend.”

The Brain turned toward it. “On what?”

“On anything. Sitting up. Washing. The effort of feeling ill.”

“We know he is tired.”

“You know the word. You have been treating it as though a night in bed must have corrected it.”

The Heart bristled, then subsided. Daniel had barely touched lunch. The unused spoon lay beside a small crescent of spilled soup.

The Liver continued more quietly.

“I noticed how poorly he recovered after the walk. I noticed yesterday’s rest did not restore him as you expected. I kept it to myself because I was tired of being consulted only when something went wrong.”

“That information would have helped,” said the Brain.

“Yes,” the Liver answered. “It would.”

For a moment, no one defended themselves. Yet the silence did not become agreement. The Brain still wanted a decisive sign. The Heart feared what stopping might mean to Daniel. The Lungs watched another shallow breath, and the Stomach waited through another wave of nausea.

During the afternoon, Daniel tried to read a page on his phone. The words kept slipping away. He lowered it onto the blanket and looked toward the window, where clouds dragged their shadows across the opposite building.

When Elena checked on him, he said the soreness was bothering him.

“More than earlier?”

“A little.”

She asked about the nausea.

“Still there.”

He heard his own answers as two small complaints, neither worth the concern gathering in her expression. When she asked him to explain further, he struggled. He could locate the pain, but he could not measure the difference between this hour and breakfast. He said he was tired. He forgot to mention how full he felt.

By evening, the pain demanded his attention even when he lay completely still. A fresh surge of nausea brought saliva into his mouth. He closed his eyes and gripped the sheet until it eased.

The Brain reached for its explanations.

The Stomach interrupted.

“He is worse.”

Daniel pressed the call button.

When Elena arrived, he began with the familiar word—sore—then stopped.

“It’s been there all day,” he said. “It used to ease when I lay still. Now it doesn’t. I feel full, though I’ve hardly eaten, and the sickness is getting stronger. Taking a proper breath hurts.”

Elena drew closer.

“Thank you for telling me together.”

She checked his observations again, asked more questions, and called for a medical review. Daniel watched her move the untouched water within reach and bring an emesis bowl to the bedside.

Inside, the Brain had fallen quiet. Its account of the day still lay before it, orderly and incomplete, while Elena asked Daniel when he had last felt any relief.

Elena’s question stayed beside the bed after she asked it. Daniel looked down at the blanket, trying to find the point in the day when pain had changed from something that came and went into something that simply remained.

“Maybe around noon,” he said. “It still eased a little when I lay still then. By midafternoon, not really.” He swallowed. “Now it’s bad even when I don’t move.”

“How bad?”

The old answer rose automatically: not too bad. The Brain had used that phrase to keep the day in order, to preserve a shape for it that could be managed. But Elena was watching him, waiting rather than filling the silence.

“Seven,” Daniel said. His hand tightened on the sheet. “Maybe eight when it surges.”

Elena nodded without flinching. “That helps. Has the fullness changed too?”

“Yes. I feel stretched inside. I haven’t eaten much, but it feels like I have.” He paused as nausea moved upward, a slow tide. “And I think I might be sick.”

The Brain collected the answers, but they arrived out of order: noon, stillness, seven, stretched, sick. It tried to lay them over the schedule it had been keeping, the sequence of meals and checks and hours since morning. The pattern would not fit. A timetable could describe what was supposed to happen. It could not explain this.

Elena checked Daniel’s observations again and asked him to breathe normally while she watched his chest. He tried. The breath caught before it could deepen.

The Lungs had been noticing that guarded rhythm for hours: a shallow rise, a careful pause, another breath kept small because expansion pulled at the pain. “That is what we have been doing,” they told the Brain. “Not because we are finished. Because a full breath hurts.”

The Heart felt its familiar impulse gather: one more effort, one more proof that the body could bear this. It had urged Daniel through discomfort before, mistaking endurance for reassurance. Now it held its beat steady and did not send the old command. There was no strength to prove. There was information to give.

Elena brought the emesis bowl closer and set it where Daniel could reach without twisting. Then she stepped into the hall to call for a clinician. The door remained partly open. Beyond it, voices passed in clipped fragments, too indistinct for the Brain to assemble: pain worsening; abdomen; review now.

The wait lasted only minutes, but the Brain could not measure it cleanly. It attended to the white ceiling, the faint rattle of a cart, the cool edge of the bowl near Daniel’s hand. It tried again to infer the whole from separate readings: pain, nausea, the number on the monitor, the way Daniel held himself. Each was a piece. None was the body.

A clinician entered with Elena and introduced herself. She asked Daniel to describe where the pain was, when it began, what made it better or worse, whether he had vomited, and when he had last eaten or drunk. The questions came one after another. The Brain reached for each, sorting and filing, but the examination had already begun before it could make a neat account.

Daniel answered as best he could. He pointed to the area that hurt most and told her it had spread wider since the afternoon. He described the earlier relief when he lay still, then the change: now stillness did little. He had not vomited. He had barely touched the food on his tray. Water had been unappealing.

“Does taking a deep breath make it worse?” the clinician asked.

“Yes,” Daniel said. “I’ve been trying not to breathe too deeply.”

The Lungs gave their account again, plain and unmistakable. The clinician watched Daniel’s chest and noted the shallow breaths. She asked permission before examining his abdomen. Daniel nodded.

Her hands were careful and warm, but even a gentle touch made the Brain’s attention contract around the sore, taut space. The Stomach knew the difference between this and the discomfort it had reported earlier. This was not only an empty ache or passing nausea. Fullness pressed from within, persistent and unfamiliar, while each new wave of sickness crowded closer to the throat.

“He’s more distended than before,” Elena said quietly.

The clinician listened, then pressed lightly in several places, asking Daniel to tell her where it hurt. He did. When he flinched, she stopped and adjusted her touch. The Brain caught another piece of conversation near the foot of the bed: observations stable; pain increasing; not tolerating food. It could not decide what those pieces meant together, and for once it did not pretend otherwise.

The Liver, long accustomed to sending its reports only when called, offered what it knew: the day’s effort had drawn on reserves, and its own account of fatigue and strain had not reached the others clearly. But it could not explain the swelling or name the cause. The Brain received the message without turning it into a verdict. No single organ could do that.

The clinician straightened. She told Daniel they needed to investigate the change and would contact the surgical team. He heard the words, then looked to Elena.

“Is it serious?”

“We don’t know yet,” she said. “Your symptoms have changed, and the team needs to assess them.”

The answer frightened him because it left the space unfilled. The Brain searched for a familiar schedule, a likely next step, some promise that the morning’s plan still held. It found none. Daniel was dependent on other people to look, listen, and decide what must happen next.

Inside, the council stayed quiet together. The Heart did not ask him to be brave by pretending. The Stomach kept its warning clear; the Lungs kept naming each guarded breath. Daniel let Elena see the fear on his face and did not soften his account.

In the hall, Elena began the call. The surgical team was being contacted. The questions had not yet produced an answer, and Daniel waited for the people who could investigate further.

Elena returned with another nurse and a wheeled stand. The wheels clicked over a join in the flooring; behind them, someone was clearing supper trays. Daniel heard cutlery gathered onto a plate and felt a fresh wash of nausea. Elena moved the table away from his bed before he had to ask.

“We’re going to check you more frequently,” she said. “The doctor has prescribed some fluids through your cannula.”

The cuff tightened around his arm. A clip settled over one finger. To the Brain, everything arrived separately: pressure, cold plastic, the nurse’s question about his name, Elena checking the skin around the cannula. It wanted to gather these details into an explanation. Instead, it helped Daniel listen to one question at a time.

“Does that hurt?” Elena asked as she flushed the line.

“No. Not there.”

She looked up at the distinction.

“Your abdomen?”

He nodded. “It hasn’t eased.”

She checked his pain again and recorded his answer without asking him to defend it.

“For now, don’t eat or drink,” she said. “We need to wait for the surgical assessment. I can help with your mouth if it feels dry.”

The instruction frightened him more than the equipment. Supper had belonged to an ordinary evening. Having it taken away made the change in his condition harder to dismiss.

“What happens next?”

“The surgical doctor is coming to examine you. We’re taking blood samples, and they’ll decide what other investigations you need.”

The Liver spoke again beneath the external voices, returning to the account it had barely begun.

“There has been less coming in,” it said. “And the day has still asked things of us. The walk. The effort afterward. I have been drawing on what was available.”

The Brain had counted the walk as progress. It had not asked what the effort had cost.

“I kept waiting until someone needed my report,” the Liver added.

No one challenged the delay. The council made room for the information. It did not explain the swelling, and the Liver did not claim that it did.

A doctor arrived, introduced herself as part of the surgical team, and drew the curtain. She asked Daniel to describe what had changed, starting before the pain became worse.

For a moment, the Brain offered him only the present: the tight abdomen, the queasiness, the fear. Then it found smaller landmarks. The walk. The pulling sensation afterward. The meal he had struggled with. Elena’s earlier visit.

“It was more of a pull before,” Daniel said. “Now it feels tight even when I’m still. And I feel sick more often.”

“Has the pain moved?”

He showed her where it was strongest, then spread his hand uncertainly.

“It isn’t only there.”

“That’s helpful,” she said. “Tell me as accurately as you can.”

She asked about vomiting, passing wind, and his last bowel movement. Where his memory was uncertain, he said so.

The Stomach kept its account plain: the pressure remained between the sharper waves; the nausea rose without bringing relief. The Lungs reported the rhythm they had been guarding.

“We stop short because the deeper breath hurts.”

Daniel found words for that, too.

“I can breathe. But taking a big breath pulls.”

The doctor paused to watch his chest before continuing. She examined his abdomen carefully, telling him what she was doing. When her hand reached a tender place, his muscles tightened despite his attempt to keep still.

The Heart surged with the old impulse: hold steady, endure this without making trouble.

Then it let the impulse pass.

“That hurts,” Daniel said, his voice breaking.

The doctor eased her pressure. “All right. Thank you.”

Afterward she helped him settle against the pillows.

“We need blood tests and a scan to understand what’s causing this,” she said. “We’ll continue the fluids and keep checking you. You’ll have treatment for the pain and nausea as well.”

“Will I need an operation?”

The question left him before he had decided to ask it.

“I can’t tell you that yet,” she said. “The examination and the investigation results will help us decide what treatment you need.”

He looked at her, wanting another sentence. She waited, allowing him time, but did not offer a promise she could not make.

“All right,” he whispered.

Elena connected the prescribed fluid. He watched the clear drops gather and fall. Blood samples were labelled beside the bed; a request for imaging was being arranged. The Brain followed each action, resisting the temptation to mistake activity for an answer. These were steps toward finding out.

After the prescribed medicine, the nausea loosened slightly. The pain remained. Elena asked what had changed, and Daniel gave her both parts.

As the evening lengthened, the ward sounds thinned. A telephone rang beyond the curtain. Somewhere nearby, a patient laughed at something on a screen. Daniel stared at the fold in the blanket above his knees and felt suddenly ashamed of how closely he was listening for footsteps.

“Can you leave the bell where I can reach it?” he asked.

Elena placed it in his hand. “Of course. Call if anything changes, or if you need me.”

He wanted to ask her not to go. Instead, he asked whether she would return while they waited.

“Yes,” she said. “I’ll be checking on you.”

The Brain could help him remember questions and report another change. It could not interpret blood still being tested or a scan not yet performed. For now, it stopped searching the council for a verdict.

The Liver remained with them. The Stomach did not have to soften its warning. The Lungs kept their careful rhythm, and the Heart no longer demanded that fear be hidden.

Daniel held the bell and listened for the team’s return, still hurting, still dependent on findings he could neither hurry nor predict. No answer had arrived. Inside, no one withdrew.

Elena returned while the light outside Daniel’s window was fading. On this fourth day after his emergency abdominal surgery, he had begun to measure time by interruptions: the pressure of a blood-pressure cuff, the arrival of medicine, the squeak of shoes beyond the curtain. Between them, he waited.

“You said you’d come back,” he murmured.

“I did.” She pulled a chair beside the bed. “Tell me how things are now.”

“The sickness is a little better.” He considered the rest before speaking. “My stomach still hurts. Worse when I move.”

“About the same pain when you’re lying still?”

“Yes. I think so.”

“You don’t have to be certain. Tell me what you notice.”

Inside, the Brain reached for its familiar task of sorting everything into danger or safety. The eased nausea belonged on one side; the persistent pain pulled toward the other. Neither side could hold the whole truth.

“Let him finish,” the Lungs said.

Daniel drew a careful breath. “When I tried to shift up the bed, it caught harder.”

Elena asked where, how long it lasted, and whether anything else had changed. She listened without supplying words when he paused. Then she helped him settle against the pillows, supporting the movement so he did not have to struggle through it alone.

The Heart quickened at the brief surge of pain.

“We might still get through this,” it said.

The Stomach stirred uneasily. “I can offer less nausea. That is all.”

“That counts,” the Heart replied, more quietly. “It doesn’t have to become more tonight.”

A doctor came with Elena after the team had reviewed the blood results and imaging. Daniel watched their faces while they explained what they had learned. He understood some of the words and lost others in the effort of listening through pain.

They were keeping him under observation. His symptoms needed further checks, and the findings had not settled every question. The doctor examined his abdomen again, warning him before touching the tender areas. Daniel gripped the sheet, then let it go.

The Brain wanted to ask, Am I going to be all right?

The question seemed too large for the room, too large for the information anyone possessed.

Instead, it gathered what Daniel could use.

“What are you watching for?” he asked.

The doctor drew the chair closer.

“Whether the pain changes, whether your abdomen becomes more swollen, whether you’re sick again, and how your temperature, pulse, and other observations behave. We’ll also review the blood tests again if needed.”

“And tonight?”

“You’ll stay here. We’ll continue fluids, adjust the prescribed medicines for pain and nausea, and examine you again. If things change, we reassess sooner.”

Daniel looked toward Elena. “So I don’t just wait until morning?”

“No. Tell us when something changes. You don’t need to decide what it means first.”

After the doctor left, Elena went over the plan once more, using shorter sentences. She checked that the bell was beside his hand and asked him to explain what he would do if the pain became different.

“Call you,” he said. “Even if I don’t know why.”

“Exactly.”

The answer relieved him, though it left the uncertainty intact.

Later, the council gathered without its old urgency to produce a verdict. There was no grand declaration from the Brain. It listened to the ordinary work continuing around it.

“My rhythm gets shallow when he braces,” the Lungs reported. “With support, he can take an easier breath. I’m still concerned about how much the pain restricts him.”

The Brain held both observations together.

“The medicine and fluids are still passing through,” the Liver said. “My work continues, but I cannot tell you from that alone what is causing this pain.”

Once, it might have withheld an incomplete report until someone demanded an explanation. Now it spoke while the information could still be useful.

“Tell us what changes as you notice it,” the Brain said. “We’ll do the same.”

The Stomach waited through a faint ripple of nausea before answering.

“A sip stayed down earlier. I’m quieter than I was. I cannot promise the next sip will be easy.”

“No promises required,” said the Heart.

Its beat continued, steady for several moments, then faster when a trolley rattled past and startled Daniel awake.

The Brain noticed how quickly fear could borrow a sound. It could not prevent that, but it could help him find the room again: curtain, window, blanket, bell. Elena’s instructions.

They agreed to share what they noticed before attempting an explanation. Effort could change with what Daniel could tolerate. Rest could be part of the work.

He slept in fragments.

Near midnight, he woke while trying to turn. At first he thought he had simply pulled against the sore place again. He lay still, expecting the sharper pain to settle into its familiar ache.

It lingered.

He listened to the ward: someone coughing, water running briefly, a quiet exchange near the nurses’ station. Elena had said she would check on him again. The clock suggested there was still time before that.

For a moment, he worried about calling her too soon.

Then the Brain recalled her actual words.

Daniel pressed the bell.

When Elena appeared, he felt an unexpected rush of relief.

“It’s sharper,” he said. “It started when I turned, but it hasn’t eased like before.”

She came to the bedside and began asking questions.

He answered as clearly as he could. When she offered help repositioning, he accepted it, letting her take some of his weight. He was still hurting, and he still did not know what the night would bring. But he had noticed a change and brought someone to his side.

For now, that was something he could do.

By the fourth morning, Daniel had learned the sounds of the ward without learning what they meant for him. Wheels whispered past the curtain; somewhere a spoon tapped a cup. The window held a thin gray light. He lay watching it, aware of the ache that remained even when he kept still.

The sharper pain near midnight had brought questions, an examination, and treatment. It had eased afterward, but not disappeared. He remained under observation. His mouth was dry, his stomach unsettled, and he felt tired in a way that sleep had not repaired.

When Elena Park came through the curtain, she drew the chair close instead of speaking from the foot of the bed.

“How has it been since I last checked?”

“Quieter,” Daniel said. He considered the word. “Not gone.”

“Quieter, but still there.”

“And worse if I turn.”

She nodded, giving him time to add anything else. He found that he had nothing certain to offer, only an uneasy awareness of his body.

“What happens now?” he asked.

“We keep checking how you’re responding. Your pain, your breathing, your pulse and blood pressure, and what you can manage to drink. The clinician will review how you’re doing. For this next check, I’ll take your observations again, then ask you about the pain and nausea.”

Daniel glanced toward the blood-pressure cuff.

“Does being here still mean I’m not doing well?”

“It means your symptoms still need watching,” Elena said. “I can’t promise you how quickly they’ll settle. You don’t have to work out what they mean by yourself.”

She explained before wrapping the cuff around his arm. The fabric was cool; then came the firm, rising squeeze. Daniel watched her face while she worked, searching it for an answer she had not given.

“When you move, how strong is the pain?”

“About six. I think.”

“About six is useful. And when you’re still?”

“Less. An ache.”

She recorded his answer without asking him to make it more exact.

“If the pain changes again, or becomes harder to manage, use the bell,” she said. “Tell us if breathing feels more difficult, too, or if the nausea worsens. You don’t need to wait for the next check.”

He looked at the bell beside his hand and nodded.

Within him, the council gathered more quietly than before. There was no silence, exactly: the Heart continued its work, and the Lungs kept their soft, uneven measure. But the Brain did not begin with instructions.

“What have you noticed?” it asked.

The Liver spoke early, before uncertainty could persuade it to wait.

“There has been more to process since the treatment overnight. I’m still occupied with it. I can report that now, though I can’t tell you how much it contributes to his tiredness.”

The Brain held the observation without fastening an explanation to it.

“So we shouldn’t assume we have strength to spare.”

“No,” said the Liver. “And I shouldn’t wait until I understand everything before speaking.”

The Lungs followed.

“When he turns, the rhythm becomes quicker and shallower. When he settles, I can settle too. That is a change I can describe.”

“What worries you?” asked the Brain.

“That he holds back because it hurts. And that we might overlook a further change by expecting every difficult breath to pass.”

“Then we will notice without deciding beforehand,” the Brain said.

The Stomach waited through a small wave of nausea.

“Two sips since dawn. Both have stayed down. I’m not comfortable, but I’ve tolerated that much.”

The Heart answered gently.

“That much matters.”

“It does not promise breakfast,” the Stomach said.

“No,” said the Heart. “It doesn’t have to.”

The Brain felt the familiar urge to gather these fragments into one reassuring conclusion. It let the urge pass.

“I don’t know yet how all of this fits together,” it said. “We can keep sharing changes as they happen. We can say when we’re unsure. If moving is too much, we ask for help and pause. We adjust together.”

“And a quieter interval needn’t become a demand for the next one to be better,” said the Heart.

The Lungs agreed. The Liver would speak sooner. The Stomach would report the next sip as the next sip, not as proof of anything beyond it.

Elena set the bell within easy reach.

“Is there anything I can help with before I go?”

Daniel had been trying not to think about the pillow. Its lower edge pressed awkwardly beneath his shoulder, but fixing it meant moving. His first impulse was to say he was all right and attempt it after she left.

He looked at her hand resting on the bed rail.

“Could you help me get comfortable?”

“Of course. Tell me where you want the support.”

He explained, awkwardly at first, then more clearly. Elena adjusted the bed and helped him shift. Pain caught as he moved.

“Pause?” she asked.

“Yes.”

They waited. He stopped trying to conceal how much of his weight she was supporting. When the sharper edge eased, they finished the small adjustment together.

The new position did not take the ache away. It gave him somewhere less uncomfortable to rest.

Elena drew the blanket over him and checked that he could reach the bell. Daniel thanked her. He did not add the apology that had risen automatically behind it.

Outside the curtain, breakfast trays were arriving. He could smell toast, faintly, beneath the clean smell of the sheets. He did not know what he would manage later.

For now, he let the pillow take the weight of his head and closed his eyes.

By late afternoon on the fourth day, a narrow strip of sunlight had reached the foot of Daniel’s bed. Beyond the curtain, wheels rattled over a joint in the corridor floor. He had rested, taken a few sips with less discomfort, and managed a conversation without closing his eyes halfway through. These were improvements. They did not remove the ache or give him much confidence in his legs.

Elena checked his observations and asked about pain, nausea, and dizziness before suggesting another attempt at walking. A physiotherapist named Nisha came with her.

“We can try a short walk here in the room,” Elena said. “We’ll help you, and we’ll stop if you need to.”

Daniel looked toward the doorway. On his earlier walk, he had watched the corridor as though reaching the next landmark might settle something. Later, when his symptoms worsened, he had struggled to describe what was happening without first trying to dismiss it.

“How far?” he asked.

“We don’t need a distance,” Nisha said. “First we see how you manage sitting, then standing.”

He rubbed his thumb along the blanket’s stitched edge.

“I’ll tell you if anything changes.”

“Even if you’re not sure what it means,” Elena said.

They helped him bring his legs over the side. The floor felt unexpectedly cold through his socks. Sitting upright drew a familiar pull through his middle, and he tightened his grip on the mattress.

“Give me a moment.”

Both women waited. Elena asked whether the discomfort was settling or increasing. Daniel paid attention before answering.

“Settling a little. Still there.”

Inside him, the Heart stirred at the prospect of movement.

We could get to the door this time.

The thought carried its old conviction: once they began, determination ought to answer the doubts. The Heart remembered the satisfaction of each completed step more readily than the strain surrounding it.

The Brain remembered both.

“A better interval isn’t dependable recovery,” it said. “We agreed to notice what happens.”

The Heart wanted to object. Instead, it waited while Daniel told Elena he was ready to try standing.

With their assistance, he rose. For a moment the room seemed arranged at an unfamiliar height: the bed low behind him, the wall clock farther away, Elena’s face level with his own. His knees trembled. Nisha stayed close while Elena supported him, and they gave him time to find his balance.

“No dizziness,” he said. “My legs feel weak.”

“All right. Small steps,” Nisha replied.

The first step required more thought than he expected. Lift, place, shift his weight. The second came more easily. His gown brushed his calves, and air from the open doorway cooled his face. Somewhere nearby, someone laughed softly at a visitor’s remark.

He wanted, suddenly and sharply, to be an ordinary man walking through an ordinary room.

The Heart felt that wanting.

“A little farther,” it began.

Then the Stomach spoke.

“The discomfort is growing.”

The Heart listened.

“It was a pull when we stood,” the Stomach continued. “Now it’s tighter. Each step is adding to it.”

The Brain resisted the temptation to explain the change before they had reported it.

“That needs saying.”

The Heart still felt the appeal of the doorway. Only a few steps remained. But it could feel Daniel beginning to brace himself, spending effort on appearing steady that he needed for standing.

“Tell them,” it said.

Daniel stopped looking at the door.

“I need to stop.”

Elena steadied him at once. Nisha brought the chair they had kept nearby behind his legs.

“Let’s sit,” she said.

They guided him down. He let them take the weight he could not comfortably manage. The chair received him with a small creak, and he rested his hands on his thighs.

“The pain’s increasing when I walk,” he said. “Same place, but tighter.”

“Any dizziness?” Elena asked.

“No.”

She asked him a few more questions and checked him again while Nisha remained beside the chair. Daniel answered without trying to improve the account. He did not say he could probably have continued. He did not apologize for stopping.

The Brain recognized something it had previously overlooked. Daniel had contributed to the assessment. His report mattered alongside the observations Elena recorded.

After a brief rest and reassessment, they helped him make the short transfer back to bed. Turning brought another catch of pain.

“Pause,” he said.

They paused.

When they continued, Elena guided his shoulders while Nisha helped with his legs. He allowed the support, breathing out as they settled him against the pillows.

Elena checked his observations again, recorded the change in discomfort, and told him she would update the clinical team. They would continue monitoring him; the next attempt at moving would depend on reassessment.

The Heart reported that its effort was easing. The Stomach said the tightness remained, though it was no longer increasing with every step. The Lungs wanted a little time. The Brain listened without gathering their separate reports into a promise.

Elena placed the bell within reach.

“Tell me if this gets worse, or if anything else changes.”

Daniel nodded. The ache had not gone, and he could not tell what the evening would bring.

“The pillow needs moving,” he said. “Could you help me before you go?”

Elena stayed beside the bed while he explained where he needed it.

Elena slid one hand behind his shoulder and eased the pillow upward with the other.

“Here?”

“A little lower on the left.”

She adjusted it again. Daniel waited for the pull in his abdomen to settle before leaning back. Such a small request, he thought, to require so much explanation. Yet she had listened as carefully as she had when he described the pain.

“That’s better,” he said.

Beyond the curtain, wheels rattled across a join in the flooring. Someone laughed quietly at the nurses’ station, then lowered their voice. The ward continued around him, occupied with needs he could hear but could not see.

Elena checked that the call bell lay beneath his hand.

“Rest for now. I’ll be back.”

He watched the curtain fall into place behind her.

The Heart still carried a trace of its earlier urgency. Before the chair, before the difficult turn into bed, it had wanted another few steps. It had remembered movement as freedom and mistaken wanting it badly enough for having strength to spare.

“We might have gone farther,” it said, though without its former certainty.

“The discomfort was increasing,” said the Stomach.

“I know.”

“And it still hasn’t settled.”

The Brain listened. Earlier, it might have treated those reports as opposing arguments and tried to choose a winner. Now it held them separately: the wish to move, the pain with movement, the exhaustion afterward. None cancelled the others.

Daniel closed his eyes. He did not sleep, but for a while he stopped watching the doorway.

By late afternoon, the discomfort had eased somewhat while he rested. Elena returned, asked him to describe it, and checked his observations. She had spoken with the clinical team. They would continue monitoring him and, if he felt able after reassessment, try a little movement with assistance.

“A little,” Daniel repeated.

“A little,” she agreed. “We can stop whenever we need to.”

The room had changed with the light. A pale strip of sunshine lay across the wall above the visitor’s chair, catching the dents in its vinyl armrest. His water cup cast a crooked shadow on the bedside table.

Daniel wanted to be the sort of patient who could get up, walk, and return without making everything complicated. He recognized the thought now: not quite hope, but an imagined obligation.

“I’d like to try,” he said. “With help.”

When Elena returned with Nisha, they cleared the space beside the bed and helped him sit. He waited there while they checked how he felt. The floor was cool beneath his feet.

Standing required concentration. He felt the shift of weight through his legs, the tightening across his middle, Elena’s steady presence at his side.

“Any dizziness?”

“No.”

They began slowly.

The Heart quickened with the effort. There was the doorway, nearer than it had looked from bed; beyond it, a narrow view of the corridor and a trolley parked against the wall.

“Perhaps to the doorway,” the Heart suggested.

“Perhaps,” said the Brain. “But listen.”

The Stomach’s discomfort had sharpened, gathering into the familiar tightness.

Daniel took another small step. His shoulders rose. He noticed that he was holding his breath.

“It’s getting tighter,” said the Stomach.

The old bargain presented itself: reach the doorway first, then tell them. It would only be a few more steps. He could already imagine explaining that he had managed farther this time.

Instead, he stopped moving.

“I need to stop,” he said. “The pain’s increasing in the same place.”

There was no apology after it.

“All right,” Elena said immediately. “Stay here with us.”

The Heart felt the wish to continue and let it pass.

“Yes,” it said. “Stop here.”

Nisha brought the chair close while Elena kept him steady. They helped him sit, then paused to assess him. Elena asked about the pain and whether anything else had changed. Daniel answered as precisely as he could.

The Brain attended to the exchange. His report had changed what happened next. He had not failed to complete an instruction; he had supplied information the others could not obtain simply by watching him.

After reassessment, they helped him return to bed. The turn was uncomfortable, and he asked them to wait before lifting his legs.

They waited.

Once he was settled, Elena checked him again and recorded what had happened.

“Shorter than I hoped,” Daniel said.

“I understand.” She looked up from the chart. “We’ll keep checking how you are and update the team about the pain with movement.”

She did not call it a setback. She did not turn it into evidence that he was getting well.

As evening gathered, the ache became more insistent again. Daniel lay watching the reflection of the corridor lights in the darkening window.

“My effort has eased,” said the Heart.

“The tightness hasn’t,” said the Stomach.

“That is what we know,” the Brain replied. “We don’t know yet what comes next.”

Daniel reached for the bell before trying to reposition himself.

When Elena came, he told her the discomfort was worse. Then he asked for help shifting onto his side.

He still wanted to recover. He wanted the corridor, the lift, the doors opening onto ordinary weather. For now, he let Elena help him move, and told her where it hurt.
