By Monday, the rain had softened the cardboard boxes in my car, though I had only carried them from the workshop to the boot. I brought them inside one at a time and stood them beside the stove. On the back of an invoice I wrote HOUSE—FRIDAY and underlined it twice.

Friday was when the sale would complete. By then, my mother’s house had to contain nothing that belonged to us. The solicitor had said “vacant possession,” a phrase I liked for its precision until I imagined the rooms.

Across the workbench, Inez was lifting old upholstery tacks from a chair. Each one made a small, reluctant squeal.

“You’re dripping on the invoice,” she said.

“It’s a copy.”

“Your sleeve, Mara.”

I pushed it back. Water had run beneath my watch.

We had three chairs to finish, a walnut table awaiting collection, and six weeks left on our lease. The landlord was converting the building into holiday flats. Inez had found another unit beyond the railway bridge: better light, a proper loading door, enough room to stop storing timber behind the kettle. It also needed a deposit, new wiring, and an extraction system.

Her savings would cover part of it. My part was still tied up in Ada Ellis’s estate.

“The agent called,” Inez said. “Someone else has asked about the unit.”

“We’ll have the money soon.”

“How soon?”

“After completion. Once the solicitor’s settled the remaining bills.”

She set a bent tack in the tin between us.

“Tell him we’re serious,” I said. “I’ll ring the solicitor again.”

Inez looked at the list beneath my hand, then went back to the chair.

I spent the morning easing apart a loose joint without splitting the surrounding wood. The chair was repairable, and I knew what it needed. I warmed the glue, checked the fit, tightened the clamp. At half past one I washed my hands and left, although a dark line remained beneath each nail.

Theo was already at the house.

He had put a towel beneath the porch leak and carried the charity boxes away from the window. His coat hung on the same peg he had used as a boy. Seeing it there gave me a brief, unreasonable relief.

“Did you eat?” I asked.

“Yes.”

“What?”

He glanced up from the newspapers spread across the carpet.

“A sandwich. You don’t need to enter it anywhere.”

“I was only asking.”

“I know.”

The hall smelled of wet wool and the lavender polish Mum had bought in bulk. Beyond it, the sitting room was half dismantled. Pale rectangles marked the walls where pictures had hung; the furniture looked too large now there were fewer things around it.

I took off my coat and checked the list on the mantelpiece.

“Charity collection Wednesday. Clearance people Thursday afternoon. We should do the upstairs rooms tomorrow.”

“I can do tomorrow morning.”

“The whole day would be better.”

“I’ve got something on.”

“What?”

He folded a newspaper around a glass ornament before answering.

“A call about a course.”

I waited.

“Boatbuilding. Up north. Nine months, starting in September.”

There was a small chip in the ornament’s base. I watched him turn it inward before folding the paper over.

“Is it funded?”

“Some of it. My share of the house should cover the rest. Fees, somewhere to live.”

“You’ll want something left afterwards.”

“I’ve worked it out.”

“Of course. I only mean—there are courses nearer. You could stay in your flat and save yourself rent.”

“I’d still be paying rent here.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Yes,” he said. “I do.”

Rain rattled against the bay window. Outside, water spilled from the blocked gutter onto the path, exactly where Mum used to tell us not to stand.

I picked up a stack of magazines.

“Inez and I are taking the unit by the railway bridge,” I said. “If the money comes through in time.”

“That’s good.”

“We need somewhere before the lease ends.”

“I know, Mara. I’m glad you’ve found it.”

He sounded sincere. That made it harder to explain why his plans had unsettled me. We were both depending on the same sale. Until that afternoon, I had assumed it would let us establish a more comfortable version of the life we already had.

“You could use the workshop,” I said. “In the evenings. Get some practice in before you commit.”

“I want the training.”

“Obviously. I just thought there might be a way to make it easier.”

“For who?”

I looked at him.

He smoothed the newspaper against the ornament with his palm.

“I’m not asking you to sort it out.”

For years, asking Theo had been part of how I sorted things out. Could he collect Mum’s prescription? Could he sit with her while I met a customer? Could he come because she had fallen, or because she had frightened me, or because I could not bear another evening of listening for movement upstairs?

He had come so reliably that I had stopped hearing the question when I asked it.

“You’ve done a lot,” I said.

He kept his eyes on the parcel.

“So have you.”

I wanted that to settle something. Instead, he put the ornament into a box and reached for another sheet of paper.

We worked until the light failed. When Theo left, he took two bags of rubbish and promised to return at nine. I nearly reminded him about the charity boxes, then remembered he had moved them before I arrived.

Alone, I went through the rooms turning off lights. In Mum’s bedroom, the curtains were still open. I drew them, although there was no one left to mind the dark windows.

Downstairs, I made tomorrow’s list. Theo could take the loft while I finished the kitchen; then we could meet in the spare room. I added the solicitor, the workshop agent, replacement packing tape. Beside each task I drew a box.

There was enough time if we started promptly. I read the list again, reassured by how little remained that could not be assigned to someone, and set my alarm for seven.

At nine, rain stippled the kitchen windows and ran in silver threads down the glass. Theo arrived with two coffees, his collar turned up against the drizzle.

“I brought the kind you like,” he said.

“I like coffee.”

“You like coffee someone else has made.”

I took the cup. On the counter, my inventory divided the cupboards into KEEP, DONATE, and DISCARD. Theo glanced at the headings, then opened the nearest cupboard.

“Could we see what’s there before we decide?”

“We’re seeing it now.”

He gave me a look, but reached for the pots. The largest had a dent in its side and black tape wrapped around one handle. I put it in the discard pile. Theo lifted it out again.

“Mum fixed this.”

“She wrapped the handle.”

“It still works.”

“It’s a pot, Theo. They make more.”

He turned it over, checking the bottom. “She used it every week.”

“She used it because she wouldn’t replace it.”

“Maybe she liked it.”

“Maybe she didn’t want to spend money.”

Neither of us could prove what Ada had meant. She had been proud of managing on her own, and he remembered that as a choice she made gladly. I remembered how often her refusal to ask for help left me anticipating what she would need next. Sometimes she wanted independence. Sometimes she wanted me to notice the cost of it. Often I couldn’t tell which, even when she was alive.

Theo put the pot on the counter. “Can we keep one battered saucepan without making it stand for everything?”

“I’m not making it stand for anything.”

“Good.” He wrapped it in newspaper.

I wrote SAUCEPAN on the box, then drew a small question mark beside it. Theo noticed but said nothing.

The next cupboard held a jar of bent nails, rubber bands, batteries, and screws too small to identify. Theo smiled as he lifted it out. “She kept everything.”

“She kept things she could use.”

“She could use most things.”

“That was the problem.”

His hands stopped around the jar. I wiped dust from the shelf; it streaked gray across my finger.

“She was proud of doing things herself,” he said.

“She was proud of not needing help.”

“Is there a difference?”

“There is when someone else has to be ready every time she insists she doesn’t.”

He lowered the jar onto the counter. Outside, water tapped from the gutter onto the paving stones. I could hear the sharpness in my own voice, but couldn’t find a softer version that felt true.

“Mum would have hated being talked about like she was a job,” he said.

“I know.”

“I’m not saying you didn’t do a lot.”

“You don’t have to say it.”

“I think you want me to.”

I turned back to the shelf. “We have to finish by Friday.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No.”

He picked up the nails. “She used these on the garden gate. It banged all night until she fixed it.”

“I remember.”

“You were angry she wouldn’t let you call someone.”

“I was angry because it was falling off.”

“She said it needed one more screw.”

“She said that about her shoulder, too.”

His mouth tightened. We had both been there, though not in the same way. Theo came when I asked him to sit with her or collect a prescription. I had stayed through the quiet hours, learned the pattern of her steps, and felt relief when she slept. We had different portions of the same years. Neither one could tell us what Ada would have wanted us to keep.

We agreed to donate the unmarked glasses, keep the saucepan, and take the jar of nails to the workshop. Theo began sorting the cutlery while I separated batteries from twist ties. Under a stack of tea towels, my fingers found a black mobile phone with a cracked corner.

For a moment I heard its old vibration against the kitchen counter. I saw the screen lighting Ada’s face, then my own hands at the workshop, busy with a chair frame. Her call had come while I was working. I had seen her name and thought I would ring back when I was finished. By the time I did, there was no answer. I had never told Theo I’d let it ring.

The phone was dark. My thumb pressed against the crack.

“What’s that?” Theo asked.

“An old phone.” I set it beneath the towels. “Probably dead.”

“There might be photos on it. We should check.”

“I’ll do it later.”

He looked at me, then at the box. I slid the towels on top and wrote PHONE on the inventory sheet.

“You have a charger?”

“I don’t know. It won’t be worth much.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

I picked up the sheet. “I can handle it.”

He paused, then lifted the towels just enough to see the phone. “Put it aside, then.”

“I will.”

He moved the box away from the donation pile. I was grateful he didn’t press me, and resented him for noticing there was something to press about.

By noon, the cupboards were half empty. Dust showed in pale bands where jars had stood, and the kitchen smelled of damp coats and cardboard. We packed the pots, the screws, and a set of chipped mugs Theo said were fine for the workshop. He offered to take the phone home and find a charger. I told him I’d keep it with the papers until we were done.

“That works,” he said.

It was a temporary arrangement, no more substantial than the boxes stacked by the door. Still, I marked the phone for KEEP, then circled the word so I wouldn’t mistake it for a decision about what to do with it.

When Theo went upstairs, I took the phone from beneath the towels and slipped it into my coat pocket. The weight against my hip was slight, but I could feel it whenever I moved. I told myself I was keeping it safe, though the box had been dry and the coat was hanging over a chair.

I crossed DONATE off the towels and rewrote it. The list offered neat spaces for decisions; the house kept producing things that didn’t fit inside them. Above me, Theo crossed a floorboard. I kept my eyes on the cupboard and waited for the sound of his steps to fade.

By five, we had cleared the cupboard under the stairs and made the hall almost impossible to cross. A bag of Ada’s winter shoes leaned against a box of baking tins. The vacuum cleaner stood in the narrow passage between them, its cord wound around the handle with the care she had once reserved for my hair.

Theo put on his coat.

“I can come back Thursday.”

“You’ve got your course.”

“In the evening.”

“You’ll need time to eat.”

He looked at the boxes rather than at me. “I know how evenings work, Mara.”

“I only meant—”

“I know.”

After he left, I stood with one hand on the vacuum cleaner. I had meant to save him something: a rushed meal, another journey, the particular exhaustion of sorting possessions that still seemed to belong to someone. But I had spoken as though he needed permission to arrange his day.

The house settled around me. In the kitchen, the refrigerator switched off, leaving a silence so complete I could hear rain ticking against the extractor vent.

I took Ada’s old phone out of my coat and laid it beside the kettle. Without the towels around it, the cracked screen looked exposed. Theo was right; there might be photographs on it. There might also be messages I had never seen, appointments she had made without me, complaints about me sent to someone else. The possibility was almost welcome. I wanted evidence that her life had extended beyond the part I had been responsible for.

I found a cable in the kitchen drawer. It did not fit.

That small failure was enough. I shut the drawer too hard, then leaned against it until the urge to open every other drawer had passed.

Upstairs, the book waited on the little desk in my old bedroom.

I had left it closed beneath a stack of estate-agent leaflets. Taking those away felt less like uncovering an object than removing someone’s hand from its mouth. I sat down without switching on the overhead light. The desk lamp made a yellow enclosure of the blotter, the book, and my hands. Beyond it, the room was full of half-packed shapes.

The entry about the call was still there.

I read the time first. I always did. As if the figures might have shifted while I was downstairs, giving me an extra minute in which to become a different daughter.

Then I read the sentence beneath them.

You saw her name and thought: I cannot bear one more thing she needs.

I pulled my hands off the desk.

“No,” I said.

The word sounded foolish in the empty room. I had not said the thought aloud that night. I had barely allowed it to finish. But the book had not accused me of wishing her dead, or even of knowing she was in danger. It had recorded something smaller and, because it was exact, harder to argue with.

I had been sitting on the edge of my own bed, still wearing my shoes. There was a supermarket bag on the floor with milk warming inside it. I had spent the afternoon collecting a prescription that was not ready, returning to the pharmacy, then persuading Ada to eat something before taking the tablets. She had wanted toast, then found the toast too hard. I had made soup. By the time I got home, I could smell it on my sleeve.

When the phone lit up, I knew only that she wanted me again.

Perhaps the television had lost its sound. Perhaps she could not find the second blanket. Perhaps she was frightened.

That last possibility had occurred to me. I could not give myself the mercy of pretending otherwise.

I had watched until the screen went dark.

A dependable daughter would have answered.

The sentence arrived with the familiar authority of a rule I had never agreed to but had spent years obeying. I reached for it almost gratefully. It made the night simple: there had been a duty, and I had failed it.

Then I looked down at my hands. A cardboard cut crossed one knuckle. Dust had gathered beneath the edge of my thumbnail. All day I had mistaken weariness for something I could postpone if I kept moving.

I had been tired that night too. Not nobly tired. Not tired in a way that made me patient or wise. I had been cross, hungry, and unable to bear the sound of a teaspoon tapping against a mug.

Why did that cease to count as soon as I called myself dependable?

I turned my own phone face up on the desk, then face down again.

I still did not know what Ada would have said. Whatever had prompted the call, whatever help might have followed, there was no way now to ask her. That uncertainty was part of the grief. So was the plain fact that I would never hear her begin with my name again, briskly or irritably or with relief.

But another pain sat beside it, one I had kept presenting as the same thing.

I had always been able to say I went.

Late, annoyed, sometimes silent all the way there, but I went. When relatives asked how she was managing, I could describe the shopping, the appointments, the small adjustments that kept her days possible. I disliked their praise and remembered it afterward.

The missed call spoiled that account. It left me unable to say always.

I bent over the book, ashamed now of a different thing: not that I had wanted rest, but that even here I was checking what her death said about me.

“Both,” I whispered.

I missed her. I also missed being certain who I had been to her. Admitting the second did not make the first untrue.

Downstairs, a box shifted with a soft cardboard sigh. I listened until the house was still again.

I could not yet forgive myself for the call. I was not sure forgiveness was what I needed from this page. But for once I let the memory include the whole room: the shoes, the milk, the soup on my sleeve, and the woman on the bed who had reached the end of what she could give that day.

I closed the book and went downstairs to eat.

By the following afternoon, the landing smelled of dust and the lavender paper Ada had put in her drawers. I found a pale-green nightdress folded beneath the winter vests. Its neck had stretched; one sleeve bore a faint rust-coloured mark the washing had never quite removed. I laid it on the bed and went back to the book.

The next entry described the afternoon I had made her wear it at half past one. It included the words I had said, and the small detail that I had kept looking at the clock. My face grew hot before I reached the bottom of the page.

That morning, Ada had needed help changing after an accident in the bathroom. The clean trousers were upstairs, the bed needed stripping, and a prescription was waiting at the chemist. I had been awake since six. In my memory the house always smelled of disinfectant that day, although there had also been toast burning downstairs. Every unfinished thing seemed to be calling me by name.

She wanted the blue trousers with the elastic waist. I brought the nightdress instead. It was easier to manage, and I could not face another struggle with her feet and the narrow openings of the trouser legs. I told her she would be more comfortable.

“It’s lunchtime,” she said. “I’m not going to bed.”

“You can sit in your chair in this.”

“I want my trousers.”

“For God’s sake, stop being difficult. I need to get you done.”

I remembered the towel clenched in my hand. I remembered her crying. For months those two details had been the whole scene: my hand, her wet face, the terrible sentence between us. I could summon it whenever I needed proof that I had failed her.

Downstairs, Theo was peeling old masking tape off a biscuit tin. It came away in yellow scraps, leaving a sticky border.

“Do you remember the day I made Ada wear her nightdress? When she wanted the blue trousers?”

His fingers stopped moving. “Yes.”

“I was horrible to her.”

“You were both bloody furious.”

“She was crying.”

“I know. She was angry too.”

I leaned against the sink. On the windowsill, rainwater had found its way beneath the wooden frame and darkened a patch we kept forgetting to repair.

“She told you she was a grown woman,” he said. “That she could decide what she wore. I heard her from here.”

I could almost hear it then: Ada’s voice carrying down the stairs, thin but sharp. In the version I usually remembered, she had scarcely spoken after my cruelty. Yet there she was, saying no.

“She wanted to sit outside,” Theo said. “It had stopped raining. She said she wasn’t going into the garden dressed for bed where anyone could see her.”

“Did she go out?”

“After you went to the chemist. I helped her change. She sat by the back step for ten minutes and complained about the weeds.”

The ordinary complaint unsettled me more than the tears. In my memory, I had kept her in that room, crying, with nothing happening after I shut the door. But she had put on her trousers. She had noticed the weeds.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

Theo set the tin down. “I did. When you came back.”

“What did I say?”

“That I hadn’t been there since six in the morning.”

For a moment I wanted to repeat it. He hadn’t been there. He hadn’t changed the sheets or cleaned the bathroom or watched the morning disappear into small, necessary acts nobody would remember unless they were left undone.

All of that was true. It had also been a way of making his account inadmissible.

“The trousers were harder,” I said, then heard myself. “That wasn’t what you were saying.”

“No.”

He began picking at the tape again. I stayed beside the sink, but neither of us found anything else to say.

Upstairs, the nightdress still lay on the bed.

I had thought my shame made me honest about that afternoon. Yet I had used it to establish how much I alone had endured. If care had driven me to something I hated, then surely I understood its demands better than anyone who had managed to remain kind at a distance.

When Theo offered suggestions, I remembered the towel in my fist and dismissed him. When Ada objected to a new arrangement, I heard another difficulty to be overcome. Even my remorse had left me in charge.

I sat at the desk. The eraser lay beside the open book, pale against the dark wood. I picked it up and pressed its edge with my thumbnail.

I wanted not to have said it. Failing that, I wanted to stop hearing myself say it.

There would be such relief in looking at that afternoon without the familiar contraction beneath my ribs. I could tell myself what I had told myself about the final call: I had been exhausted. That was true too. But I was frightened of how easily the truth of my exhaustion might become permission, once nothing in me recoiled from what I had done.

Was the recoil what kept me responsible? It had not stopped me speaking over Ada afterward. It had not made me listen to Theo.

I turned the eraser over. One corner was still sharp enough to take off a single word.

I did not trust myself to know what I wanted removed: the cruelty, its consequence, or the knowledge that I was capable of it.

At last I set it down and left the entry uncovered. I tried to remember Ada’s answer in the voice Theo had given back to her—not forgiving me, not reduced to being hurt, but insisting, angrily, on her trousers.

Story:

The next morning Theo came in looking for the sale documents. Rain had darkened the windows and left the kitchen smelling of wet wool and cold tea. I had spread the papers across the dining table the evening before, and now he stood among them with one hand on the back of a chair, scanning the headings.

“Have you seen the folder from the solicitor?” he asked.

“Not since yesterday.”

He glanced at the inventory, then toward the desk in the adjoining room. The book was still open there. I had left it that way because closing it would have felt like another kind of concealment, though I knew how thin that distinction was.

Theo crossed to the desk. “Is this part of the inventory?”

“No.”

He leaned over the page. His face changed before he touched it: first concentration, then a stillness I recognized from the moments when he was trying not to let someone else’s reaction dictate his own. Along the margin, his name stood beside a strip of pale, abraded paper. The erased place was rough under the light, the fibers lifted where I had rubbed them away.

“What was here?”

I came to stand behind him. I could have reached past him and shut the book. I could have said it was old, meaningless, private. Each answer arrived ready-made, and each one made the distance between us feel more deliberate.

“I erased an entry.”

“An entry about what?”

“Your apprenticeship.”

He looked at me then. “You said they turned me down.”

“I know.”

“No, you said they’d chosen someone else. You said the place was gone before I could answer.”

“I lied.”

For a moment he seemed to be waiting for the rest of the sentence to correct the first part. The rain ticked against the glass. Downstairs, a pipe knocked once in the wall.

“I called them pretending to be you,” I said. “I told them you were declining the apprenticeship. Then I told you they had refused you.”

The words sounded plain. That was worse than if they had come out tangled. I had made this choice in a room with a telephone, and there was nothing in the telling that could make it grander or less exact.

Theo’s mouth tightened. “Why?”

I could have begun with our mother, with the days when Ada’s needs consumed the house and every decision seemed to come with a new demand attached. I could have told him how frightened I was of being left alone to manage it. Those things were true, and I had used them so often they had acquired the shine of a defense.

“I thought if you left, I couldn’t keep everything together,” I said. “I wanted you to stay. I decided that wanting it gave me the right to choose for you.”

“And the book?”

“After I lied, I used the eraser on the entry.” My eyes settled on the pale scrape. “I thought removing it might undo what I’d done. I can’t prove it changed anything.”

“Did it?”

“I don’t know.” My throat had gone tight, but I made myself continue. “Afterward, your shame about wanting to leave Ada got worse. You started speaking as if wanting the apprenticeship meant you’d abandoned her. And the notation in the book changed. I saw it. I can’t tell you what caused what. I won’t use that uncertainty to excuse my part.”

He gave a short, disbelieving laugh. “You watched me think I’d failed.”

“Yes.”

“You let me believe I wasn’t good enough.”

“Yes.”

The second answer seemed to land harder. He moved away from the desk and folded his arms, not to protect himself from the cold but to keep his hands still. I knew that gesture too, but knowledge was not the same thing as understanding. For years I had treated knowing his habits as if it meant I knew what he needed.

“You said I was making it up,” he said. “When I told you I wanted to go.”

“I remember.”

“You made me ashamed of it.”

“I did.”

His eyes searched my face, as if he expected to find some better reason hiding behind the ones I had given him. “Would you have told me if I hadn’t started suffering?”

I opened my mouth. The question left no clean path through it. The truth was not that I had planned to confess; it was that I had begun to fear what his pain said about me. I had watched him carry a shame I had helped put there. The thought of keeping silent had become harder to bear only after it stopped allowing me to think of myself as the injured one.

“I don’t know,” I said. “I want to say yes. I can’t honestly say I would have.”

He looked away. I heard the front door open and shut, then Inez’s steps in the hall. She had come to collect the measurements and figures for the workshop move. She stopped at the threshold when she heard the word apprenticeship.

“Inez,” I said.

She looked from Theo to the open book. “What happened?”

Theo answered before I could. “Mara called them as me. She declined the apprenticeship for me. Then she told me they’d turned me down.”

Inez’s gaze dropped to the scraped paper. She didn’t ask me to confirm it. She looked instead at the sale documents, still spread across the table, and at the figures we had used to plan the move: the expected proceeds, the deposit, the rooms we meant to rent. Her expression tightened as she understood what those plans had rested on—not only money, but a future in which my account of Theo’s choices had gone unchallenged.

“So all this,” she said quietly, “was built on what you kept from him.”

“Partly,” I said.

Theo looked at her. “I didn’t ask her to sort out my plans.”

“I know,” Inez said.

She did not come closer to me. She did not offer comfort to him that might have turned the moment into something easier for me to witness. She gathered the papers nearest her, then stopped, leaving the rest where they were.

“I need to think about whether I still want this move,” she said. “I’m not agreeing to it today.”

Theo returned his attention to the blank in the book. I could feel how much I wanted him to look at me, to let me see that the confession had reached him as a beginning. But I had taken enough decisions out of his hands. I kept mine at my sides.

He closed the book, carefully, without accepting my explanation or offering one of his own. The sale documents remained scattered across the table. Nothing in the room had moved, and yet the plans we had made no longer seemed to belong to any of us.

Theo kept one hand on the closed book.

“I need time,” he said. “And I need you to leave me alone while I take it.”

His voice was quieter now, but not calmer. He held his jaw as though there were something between his teeth he could neither swallow nor spit out.

“No finding another place for me. No ringing anyone. No asking Mum to talk sense into me.”

“All right.”

“And don’t make a plan and then tell me it’s what I wanted.”

A lorry passed outside, rattling the window in its frame. I waited until the sound had gone.

“I won’t.”

Already, possibilities were arranging themselves in my head: another yard, another intake, the cost of a room somewhere beyond the reach of our daily lives. I had to stop myself from speaking them. Having useful information was not the same as having permission.

“There is something I can offer,” I said. “You don’t have to answer now.”

Theo’s fingers pressed against the cover.

“Money,” I said. “From my inheritance. Not from your share of the sale, or anything belonging to Mum. There’s enough to cover comparable training, tools, travel, and living costs while you learn.”

“You’ve worked it all out?”

“Not an arrangement. An amount I could give.”

He looked at me with such suspicion that I heard how little difference those words might make to him.

“It wouldn’t have to be boatbuilding,” I added. “If you took it, you wouldn’t have to account to me for what you did with it. You could train somewhere else, do something else, or keep it until you knew.”

“And you’d get what?”

“Nothing you owe me. Not forgiveness. Not visits. You wouldn’t have to stay nearby.”

I wanted him to believe me immediately. That desire was familiar enough to make me keep still.

“It won’t bring the apprenticeship back,” I said. “I know that.”

He looked down at the book.

“Are you sorry now?”

I could have said that I was sorry he was suffering. It was true, and it would have allowed me to step neatly around what he meant.

“No,” I said. “I still don’t feel remorse for what I did.”

Inez drew a slow breath beside the doorway.

Theo’s face changed, not with surprise exactly, but with the fresh impact of something he had already understood.

“I know it was a betrayal,” I said. “I know you lost something because I chose to take it from you. Not feeling what I ought to feel doesn’t change that.”

“Convenient.”

“Yes.”

My throat had tightened. I was frightened of losing him, frightened of what Inez would decide. None of that was remorse, and I could not offer it in remorse’s place.

Inez stepped towards the table.

“That money was the reserve for the move,” she said.

“Most of it.”

“Without it, these figures don’t work.”

“No.”

She rested two fingers on the sheet nearest her. I remembered us leaning over it together, making the new rooms possible by moving sums between columns.

“I can’t put that money into the workshop,” I said. “Not while keeping it available for Theo.”

“I didn’t ask you to give up your move,” he said sharply.

“No. And you don’t have to take less, or refuse it, to keep our plans going. That isn’t yours to resolve.”

Inez looked at me.

“Don’t promise you’ll find another way.”

I had been about to do exactly that.

“I won’t.”

Beyond the window, water ticked steadily from the gutter onto something metal. Nobody reached for the papers.

Theo noticed the folded wrapper beside the book.

“Is that all that’s left?”

I opened it. The eraser had worn down to a dirty smear and a few flattened crumbs caught in the creases. There was nothing left that either of us could hold against a page.

“All of it,” I said.

He studied it without touching it. Whatever the erasure had done, there was no second use waiting for him, no way for either of us to erase a little further and arrive back before the phone call.

He pushed his chair away from the table.

“I’m not accepting anything.”

“I understand.”

“I haven’t refused it, either.”

“All right.”

“And don’t come after me when I go.”

I moved aside before he had to ask. At the doorway he stopped, looking past Inez towards the hall.

“I’ll get in touch when I’m ready.”

I nodded. He waited a moment, perhaps for the question I wanted to ask—when, where, how would I know he was safe? I kept it to myself. Then he went to fetch his coat.

We heard the outer door open and close.

Inez remained standing. I wanted to ask whether she was leaving too, but she spoke before I could.

“I’m going to look at what I can afford on my own.”

“The workshop?”

“Among other things.”

There was no cruelty in her voice. She looked tired, and I realised how many of the figures on the table she would now have to work through again.

“I need to decide where I want to work,” she said. “And where I want to live. I can’t start with your assurance that we’ll manage and fit everything else around it.”

“I know.”

“Do you? Because I’m not asking you to make a better offer.”

I lowered my eyes to the papers. Some contained calculations in my handwriting; others held her careful measurements, with small corrections crowded into the margins.

“I’ll leave you to decide,” I said.

She began separating her sheets from mine. When she reached across the table for the last page, I moved my hand out of her way.

For three days I did not contact Theo. I answered the estate agent’s emails and booked the collection for the furniture we had agreed to donate. Whenever my phone lit up, I turned it over too quickly, then made myself read whatever had actually arrived. There were questions I could have disguised as practical necessities. I left them unasked.

His message came on the fourth morning.

I can do the house tomorrow. Two until four.

I wrote back that I would be there, then put the phone beside the kettle before I could add anything.

The house had grown cold between visits. When I opened the curtains, dust lifted from the hem and hung in the pale light. I turned on the heating and carried empty boxes into the kitchen. At two, Theo’s key scraped in the lock.

He kept his coat on while he spoke.

“Only house things today. What’s being kept, what needs moving, the sale.”

“All right.”

“Not the apprenticeship. Not the money. Unless I bring it up.”

I set down the roll of tape I was holding.

“Unless you bring it up.”

“And I need to leave at four.”

“I heard you.”

He watched me briefly, then took off his coat.

We began with the kitchen cupboards. There were more serving dishes than I remembered, stacked behind the everyday plates, and a drawer that would only open halfway because a potato masher had caught against its top. Theo freed it with a flat knife. I wrapped the dishes while he made space on the table. For a while, our conversation consisted of quantities, chipped edges, and whether the charity would accept electrical things.

“The buyers still want the same completion date,” I said.

“Any problems?”

“Nothing new. The solicitor’s waiting for one answer from their side. The agent says we should keep clearing.”

He nodded and handed me another plate.

At a quarter to four, we still had the cupboard beneath the stairs untouched and three boxes without lids. My back ached. I looked at the clock, then at Theo, who was kneeling to retrieve a saucepan from the lowest shelf.

I could have asked whether he was any nearer knowing what he wanted. An answer would let me settle figures that I kept calculating and crossing out. I could have called that a reason he ought to answer.

“Leave that cupboard,” I said. “We need time to put these somewhere dry.”

We carried the packed boxes into the front room. At five to four he washed his hands, rubbing dust from the creases beside his nails.

“Some of the lids are in the shed,” he said.

“I’ll find them.”

He put on his coat. I did not follow him into the hall until he called back to ask where I wanted the key left.

“Keep it. You’ll need it when you come again.”

“All right.”

The door closed at four.

I stood among the boxes for a moment, listening to his car start. Then I went out to the shed. Rain had darkened the wood around the latch, and the first lid I found was too small.

That evening, Inez asked me to look at her revised figures.

She had drawn a firm line beneath the monthly costs: rent, power, insurance, materials. Beside the electricity estimate she had written a higher number and circled it.

“That’s based on the worst winter bill,” she said. “I’d rather have something left than need another rescue halfway through.”

The income column contained confirmed work, not the orders we had hoped would follow a move. My proposed contribution was absent.

“I can keep the workshop where it is,” she said. “There’s not much room, but I can do it.”

“And relocating?”

“Not yet.”

She turned the page towards herself again.

“I’m postponing because I don’t trust the plan we made. Too much of it depended on your saying we’d manage, and me accepting that instead of asking what would happen if we didn’t.”

“I know.”

“This isn’t a punishment, Mara. I’m not making you wait for some length of time you can put right by being sorry enough.”

I pressed my thumb against the edge of my notebook.

“And I’m not asking for another promise,” she added. “I need to use figures I can sustain.”

“Then those are the figures we use.”

She looked up. I could hear how easily that might become another claim on her decision.

“For the arrangements that concern me,” I said. “The rest is yours.”

I opened my notebook and crossed out the proposed moving date. Beneath it were estimates for a van and storage. Neither had been booked. I removed them from the month’s total and began recalculating the expenses that would remain.

“I’ll tell the removals firm we won’t be taking that date,” I said.

“Yes. Thank you.”

There was a pause in which I wanted to ask what staying meant for us. I wanted a word from her that would make the revised figures feel temporary and safe.

Instead, I checked a subtraction. I had missed an insurance payment.

Inez pointed to it, and I wrote it in.

The following morning Theo sent another message.

Sunday, ten till twelve? Dining-room cupboards and whatever’s left downstairs.

Yes, I replied. I’ll bring more boxes. The papers can stay together until we have time to go through them.

Fine.

I put the collection confirmation with the other sale documents and sent the agent our updated clearance schedule. There was still enough time, provided I arranged help for the heavy furniture rather than assuming Theo would do it.

On Sunday’s page in my notebook, I wrote his hours. Then I put spare tape, a marker, and two flat boxes by the door.

Story:

The sale had closed, and the money was in the account. The moving date, once pencilled into my notebook, had been crossed out. There was no replacement date. On the next sorting morning, the house still smelled of dust, old wood, and the lavender sachets Ada tucked into drawers. Theo arrived at ten, as agreed, and set his coat over a chair.

“The box I kept out,” he said.

I moved the donation pile away from the table. He had asked that we sort the papers together, not that I decide what they meant. I’d brought folders and labels, but left the marker capped.

The box held envelopes, receipts, folded lists, and a bundle of notes bound with an elastic band gone brittle with age. We spread them across the table. Outside, wind worried at the bare branches against the window. Inside, the quiet made the turning of each page sound deliberate.

The first note was for Theo. *Soup in the blue pot. Don’t let me forget you’re the one who likes pepper.* Underneath, Ada had drawn a pepper pot with its lid hovering above it.

Theo gave a small laugh. “She remembered.”

“I’d forgotten she drew things.”

“She did it on shopping lists, too. Usually when something cost more than she thought it should.”

The next sheet was a list of household instructions: the boiler’s temperamental switch, the plumber’s number, how to lift the back door before turning its latch. A letter folded beneath it was addressed to a cousin. *Call before you come. I am still capable of answering my own door.* The words were underlined twice.

“She didn’t want people deciding what she needed,” Theo said.

“She could be very sure about what we needed.”

He looked at me, then nodded. “Yes.”

In another letter, Ada told me I had always been good at “making a plan out of a muddle.” I read it aloud. The praise warmed me, though it carried the weight of an expectation I had long mistaken for a compliment. I could picture her proud of me; I could also picture her relying on me because I seemed able to manage.

The papers did not reveal a truer Ada than the one we remembered. They held the same person in different moods: funny, affectionate, exacting, unwilling to have her independence treated as a problem to be solved.

Theo lifted an envelope from the apprenticeship office. Its edges were soft from handling. “You think she’d have been pleased I got in?”

“I think she’d have asked what it paid and whether you’d worked out the travel costs.”

“That sounds right.” He turned the envelope over. “Maybe she’d have been pleased after.”

“I thought she might have been proud you were going somewhere on your own.”

“She might have been. Or she might have said I was leaving everyone else to manage.”

Neither of us reached for the letter. I remembered Ada thanking him for staying; he remembered the repeated requests, the tasks that grew into a daily arrangement. I could not say what his years of care had meant to her, and he wouldn’t claim that his memory gave him the whole answer.

“She could have been grateful and still expected it,” I said.

“Maybe.” His finger rested on the envelope. “I don’t know what she understood about those years. I know what they felt like to me. The papers can’t settle that.”

I wanted to tell him he had done enough. I wanted, too, to make the apprenticeship into proof that he had once been allowed to want something beyond the house. Both ideas seemed too easy, each a way of telling him what his own past should mean.

He set the envelope down. “I did want to leave,” he said. “I wanted the training, the room I’d have rented, the feeling that my days might belong to me. It wasn’t just something I thought I ought to want.”

“I hear you.”

“The shame attached to it now feels bigger than the wanting itself.” He looked at the table rather than at me. “Like wanting to go made me selfish, whatever else happened.”

“I can understand that those are different things,” I said.

I didn’t tell him what he should feel, or suggest that he revive the training plans he had abandoned. For a moment, the only sound was a branch brushing the glass. Then Theo drew the apprenticeship letter toward himself.

“About the money you offered,” he said. “I want the offer in writing. The amount, and what you mean by restitution. I need to look at it privately.”

“All right.”

“If I accept it, I decide what it’s for. It won’t be a promise to forgive you, or a commitment to the future you imagine for me.”

“I understand.”

He watched me, perhaps waiting for the question I might once have asked: when would he decide? I gathered the receipts into a pile instead.

“I’ll write it down,” I said. “There’s no deadline.”

“Good.”

We finished sorting. The household instructions went into a practical folder; the notes and letters stayed together. Theo placed the apprenticeship letter between them, paused, then took it back out.

“I’ll keep this one for now.”

I gave him an empty folder, and he slipped the letter inside. It settled nothing about Ada, or about what he would choose. It was simply his to keep until he decided what it meant.

The sale papers covered the dining table, their corners held flat by the brass candlestick and a chipped blue mug. Beyond the windows, rain darkened the garden wall and threaded down the glass. The house had already begun to look emptied, though the boxes stacked by the hall still held more than we had carried away.

Theo read the transfer document once, then again. I had written the amount, the account it would come from, and the terms in plain language: the money was his without conditions. No requirement to forgive me, stay in the house, or make any particular use of it.

“You could have put that in the offer,” he said.

“I should have.”

He turned to the signature page. I watched his hand pause above it, then move on. The papers were mine to sign. I did so, one after another, keeping my name within the printed lines. My signature looked as it always had: narrow, controlled, a little too upright. There was nothing in it that could show what I understood now, or make that understanding useful to him.

When I finished, I slid the documents across the table.

“The inheritance will be available as soon as the transfer clears,” I said. “There isn’t anything you need to do for me.”

Theo kept his palm on the papers. “I’m going to use some of it for training. Somewhere else.”

He said it without looking at me, as if he were testing whether the words could stand upright on their own. On the counter lay a folded prospectus from a technical institute in another city. He had not shown it to me. I had noticed it when I came in, and made myself leave it where it was.

“All right,” I said.

“That doesn’t mean I forgive you.”

“I know.”

He studied my face, searching for the softening, perhaps, or for the defense that would follow. I didn’t offer either. Accepting the money did not undo what I had done with the entry, the eraser, or the burden I made him carry. It did not return the apprenticeship he had lost. I could name those things, but naming them was not the same as giving them back.

“I don’t want you explaining my choice to anyone,” he said.

“I won’t.”

“And don’t tell me what the money ought to mean.”

“I won’t do that either.”

He gathered the papers and placed them in the folder I had given him the day before. The movement was careful, almost formal. I felt the old impulse to ask where he would go, what kind of training he had found, how soon he meant to leave. I let the questions remain unasked. They belonged to him until he chose to share them.

In the kitchen, Inez stood beside the boxes, holding a roll of packing tape. The relocation schedule was still pinned to the wall, its dates crossed out and rewritten so often the paper had begun to tear at the folds. I had treated the move as a decision made between us, and then, when I began to understand what I had assumed, tried to make postponement sound like another arrangement I could manage.

“We should put it on hold,” I said. “The move. And the workshop.”

Inez’s fingers tightened around the tape. “For how long?”

“I don’t know. Until you know what you want. Not what I want you to want.”

She glanced at the boxes. Some were hers; I had labeled them in my handwriting, as though a label could settle what belonged where. “I need time to think about the workshop without it being attached to leaving with you.”

“Yes.”

“And I don’t want you asking me every few days whether I’ve decided.”

“I won’t.”

She set the tape down. Her expression did not ease, but the guardedness in it shifted, as if she had been braced for me to negotiate. We agreed to leave the boxes where they were and discuss the workshop only after she had time to consider it on her own. The schedule came off the wall. I folded it once and set it in a drawer, not the wastebasket. The dates had mattered to us; they simply could not command us now.

Later, Theo found me in the back room, where the workshop door stood open to the wet yard. Dust and the sharp smell of oil lingered in the air. The tools hung in their places, though several gaps showed where things had already been packed. A strip of bare wood marked the space where I had taken down the old ledger.

He was looking at the prospectus. “I keep thinking that wanting the apprenticeship was the part I should be ashamed of,” he said. “Not what happened after.”

The shame had fastened itself to the wanting. I had helped put it there, first by making his ambition seem like an accusation against the family, then by erasing the entry and leaving him to bear what followed. Nothing about his having wanted a life elsewhere made my choices less deliberate.

“You don’t have to make it mean that,” I said.

“I know that’s what you think.”

“I do. But it’s yours to decide what it means.”

He folded the prospectus along its old crease. “I’ll decide where I apply. I’ll decide what I use the money for.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t want help making a plan.”

“Then I won’t make one.”

He nodded once. It was not agreement between us so much as a boundary set down where I could see it. I left it there.

When I returned to the kitchen, Inez was at the window, watching rainwater spill from the gutter. The boxes remained unopened. Theo’s folder rested on the table beside the transfer receipt, ready for him to take when he chose. The house sale would move forward; the relocation would not, at least not yet. The workshop’s future stayed undecided.

For the first time in weeks, I did not try to turn those unfinished things into a plan. I could not make them right by arranging them neatly. The papers were signed, the money was his, and what he did next would be his to choose.

On the final day in the house, its emptiness made every sound conspicuous. Without the furniture, footsteps travelled from room to room, and the walls showed pale rectangles where pictures had hung. Rain pressed against the windows. Water spilled from the broken gutter into the yard with a regular, hollow sound. The house smelled of wet plaster, extinguished fires, and the soap Inez had used on the empty shelves.

Inez stood at the kitchen table among the boxes we had meant to carry to the new place. None had been labelled for transport.

“We are not moving next week,” she said.

“No.”

“We are not choosing another workshop until I have decided what I want.”

“No.”

She watched me, waiting for the attempt to turn her hesitation into a schedule. I had believed the sale would give us a more comfortable life: better rooms, better equipment, a future I could arrange around the money. Now the sale had only removed one certainty. Inez needed time, and I had no right to spend it for her.

Theo sat near the window with his folder open. The inheritance transfer receipt lay beneath his application papers. He had used part of the money for the first fee, travel, and a room near the training centre. The rest was his without conditions.

“The office confirmed my place,” he said. “I leave Monday morning.”

The date made his departure real. He had applied, paid, and chosen the arrangements himself. I did not ask whether he wanted advice. He had already told me that his plans were not mine to make.

“How do you feel about it?” I asked.

“Relieved. Frightened.” He closed the folder. “And ashamed, sometimes.”

The old shame had attached itself to his wanting. He had wanted the apprenticeship, and after I erased the entry, he had been made to carry the burden of what I had done. He still seemed to believe that desiring another life had been a kind of betrayal.

“Wanting it was not the harm,” I said.

“I know you say that.”

“I am not asking you to believe me. You decide what it means.”

His gaze moved to the rain-streaked glass. “Leaving does not make Ada easier.”

“No.”

“I do not know what I feel about her.”

“You do not have to know before Monday.”

There were questions he had not asked Ada and questions I had kept him from asking. I could not settle them by sending him away, and he could not settle them by staying.

Before noon, I followed him into the workshop. Most of the tools had been packed. Empty hooks interrupted the familiar rows, and the bench smelled of oil, damp wood, and metal dust. A clean mark showed where Ada’s things had been removed.

“Do you want me to keep one of Ada’s tools for you?” I asked.

I did not name one or suggest that he would regret leaving it behind. Theo stood before the remaining shelves, considering them. He picked up a small marking knife with a dark handle. Its blade had been sharpened down almost narrow enough to disappear.

“That one.”

“Do you want it with you?”

“Keep it here. For now.”

I wrapped it in clean cloth and placed it in the drawer beneath the old ledger’s empty space. The choice was his. I did not ask him to explain it.

While I closed the drawer, I waited for remorse and found the same absence that had followed me since the erasure. There was no sudden inward punishment, no feeling proportionate to the damage. The calm did not make me innocent. I had erased the apprenticeship, turned Theo’s ambition into an accusation, and left him with the consequences. Restitution could return money and opportunity; it could not return the moment before I chose.

At the front door, Inez held the keys for the buyer. Before they changed hands, I retrieved Ada’s tool from the drawer and put it in my bag. Theo lifted his own.

“Do you want me to come to the station?” I asked.

“No.”

“All right.”

He stepped into the rain. We said goodbye without forgiveness, or even an agreement about what repair would require. He walked toward the road and did not look back.

The house was no longer ours. The workshop had no future yet. Nothing between Theo and me had been settled, but he had left with a plan that belonged to him, and I had not tried to direct it. Whatever repair remained possible was still unfinished, waiting beyond his departure.
