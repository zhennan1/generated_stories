The board lifts more easily than it should. Underneath is a hollow space lined with black dust, and inside that space rests a leather-bound book. **You regret leaving the hospital room before your brother woke.**

The room seems to contract around you.

Your breath catches. You remember the hospital’s blue hallway, the vending machine humming beside the elevators, the way your brother’s fingers had curled weakly around your sleeve. You have called it mercy for years. You have called it exhaustion. You have called it nothing at all.

The book turns another page by itself.

**You may erase one.**

A broken clothes hanger, or something made from one. A pale strand runs from the fabric into the darkness beneath the radiator. I lower the beam until my knuckles nearly brush the floor.

The strand pulls taut.

I jerk backward so hard my shoulder strikes Mara’s knee. She catches my coat, and the metal scrapes toward the radiator, making the same long, deliberate sound we heard from the stairs.

“Jesus,” I say.

“Did you see something?”

“Something’s attached to it.”

My pulse hammers in my wrists. I sweep the light beneath the radiator. There is a gap behind its rusted feet, a narrow passage between the pipes and the wall. Dust lies thick everywhere except along a curved track through it.

The metal moves again.

This time I keep the beam steady.

A pointed nose appears beneath the radiator, followed by a small dark eye. The rat freezes in the light with the pale strand caught between its teeth.

For a moment, none of us moves.

Then Mara lets out a breath so abruptly it sounds like a cough.

“A rat with poor judgment,” she says.

The animal retreats. The strand tightens, drawing the hooked metal another inch across the floor. Black cloth snags on a radiator foot. The rat pulls twice, releases it, and disappears into the gap.

I remain crouched, staring at the place where it was.

There is the hand I have come looking for. Teeth, actually. An animal apparently collecting something soft, dragging the attached weight back and forth whenever it catches.

“That explains the sound,” Mara says.

“And the movement.”

My voice comes out sharper than I intend. She releases my coat.

I follow the track with the flashlight. It curves behind a stack of flattened cardboard farther along the corridor, then returns toward the radiator. From the office doorway, the metal could have disappeared behind the stack. It could have come back while I listened to my dead brother explain that I was allowed to live.

I want the explanation to feel reassuring. Instead I feel foolish, then angry at feeling foolish. My brother is still dead. Mara still kept the tape. A rat does not make any of that smaller.

“Hold this,” I say, offering her the flashlight.

She takes it without comment.

I walk farther down the corridor to a stack of discarded shelving and find a loose wooden slat. When I return, Mara directs the beam beneath the radiator while I pin the hooked metal to the floor. The strand stretches between it and the darkness.

“Don’t put your fingers there,” she says.

“I’m not.”

“I know. Just—don’t.”

Using the splintered edge of the slat, I worry at the fabric until the rotten knot parts. The released strand flicks beneath the radiator. There is a brief rustling, then silence.

I lift the metal by its bare end and set it on a high shelf, out of the rat’s reach. My hand leaves a clean patch in the dust.

“There,” I say.

Mara lowers the flashlight. “You didn’t have to do that.”

“It kept getting stuck.”

She looks toward the radiator, then back at me. I cannot tell what she makes of my answer, and I am grateful when she leaves it alone.

We stand in the corridor with the wooden slat between us. Without the scraping, I can hear water moving in the pipes overhead. Somewhere upstairs, a door closes.

“I’m still angry,” I say.

“I know.”

“He asked you not to tell me yet. I heard that.”

“Yes.”

“But you got to decide how long yet lasted.”

Her thumb moves along the flashlight’s ridged casing. “I did.”

There is no defense attached to it this time. I put the slat against the wall because I am gripping it hard enough to feel a splinter entering my palm.

“I don’t know what to do with that.”

“You don’t have to decide down here.”

I look toward the open office. The edge of the desk is visible through the doorway. On it sit the cassette case and the small white eraser, exactly where I left them.

Before the tape, the hospital regret had seemed the obvious choice. Now I cannot even name it precisely. Leaving his room? Being afraid? Believing afterward that fear had killed him?

I take the flashlight back, turn, and walk along the corridor. At the threshold I step over the chair leg and enter the office. Mara follows, but stays beside the door.

I pull out the desk chair and sit. For now, I move the cassette closer to me and leave the eraser where it is.


The cassette case has a cracked hinge. When I lift it, the lid hangs crookedly, and I have to guide the two halves together before they will close. Such a small repair to make after hearing his voice. I press until the plastic catches, then keep my hand on it.

Mara stays beside the doorway. Without the scraping in the corridor, I can hear the faint drag of her sleeve against the frame.

“I need to see what it actually says,” I tell her.

“The book?”

I nod. “I keep calling it the hospital regret. As if that’s one thing.”

She looks at the eraser. I resist the impulse to cover it.

“There’s leaving,” I say. “There’s being relieved when I left. There’s everything I’ve told myself about it since. I don’t know which of those I was about to rub out.”

My throat tightens around the last words. Until now, choosing has meant finding the worst pain and pointing at it. The book made that seem sensible. Everything written down, everything given its own place, as though a life could be sorted well enough to remove one part without disturbing the rest.

“Do you want me to come?” Mara asks.

I rub the edge of the cassette case against my palm. I want her gone. I want her where I can ask another question without waiting for an answer to a message. Both wants feel equally strong.

“You can walk back with me,” I say. “That isn’t me saying we’re all right.”

“I understand.”

I put the cassette in my inside pocket and the eraser in the other, keeping them separate. Then I push back the chair. Its feet complain against the floor, and for an instant I brace for an answering scrape from the corridor. Nothing comes. I switch off the desk lamp and step over the chair leg holding the door. Mara waits while I remove it, set it inside, and pull the office door shut.

We pass the radiator. The hooked metal remains on the high shelf, an ordinary piece of rubbish now that I know how it moved. I shine the flashlight beneath it once, then turn toward the stairs.

At the first landing, I stop.

Mara is two steps below me. “What?”

“I heard him say it.”

She waits.

“That he wanted me to have my own life.” I grip the rail; its paint feels gritty. “I heard him. I’m not pretending I didn’t.”

“You don’t have to prove that to me.”

“I think I’m proving it to myself.”

His reassurance is still there, unmistakable in his voice. It has not made me feel better in the uncomplicated way I expected truth to work. Instead it has made some of my oldest explanations difficult to repeat. I start climbing again before I can turn that difficulty into another reason to stay underground.

Outside, the air cools the sweat beneath my collar. Mara puts away the flashlight. We walk toward the house with enough space between us for another person, although neither of us steps aside when the pavement narrows.

“I wish you’d let me be angry then,” I say.

She turns her head.

“When he asked you to wait. Or afterward. I could have been angry with him. We could have had that.”

“I kept putting it off,” she says. “After a while, giving you the tape also meant telling you how long I’d had it.”

I look at her. She keeps walking, her hands down by her sides.

“That’s not an excuse,” she adds.

“No,” I say. “It isn’t.”

For several paces, our shoes are the only sound. I feel no satisfaction in having made her admit it. But I am glad the words are outside me now, where she has to hear them too.

At the house, I fumble the key twice. Mara does not offer to take it. Inside, I leave my coat on and climb the stairs, then pull down the attic steps. The hinges give their familiar complaint. I go up first, carrying the cassette in my hand so it cannot bend against my ribs.

The leather-bound book is where I hid it. I kneel and draw it out. Dust catches in the creases of the cover. From below, Mara asks whether she should stay downstairs.

“On the steps,” I say. “Please.”

Her weight settles on a lower tread. I can see one hand on the rail, but not her face.

I open the book and search for the hospital entry, turning carefully, unwilling now to skim the surrounding regrets as though they are unimportant. When I find it, I hold the page flat with both hands.

I read it once. Then again, more slowly.

On a loose scrap beside the book, I write: *What happened?* Beneath that: *What did I decide it meant?*

The eraser rests in my pocket. I leave it there.

I place the cassette across the scrap to keep it from curling and bend closer to the page. Before I choose anything, I am going to answer both questions.


The entry is shorter than I remember.

*I left his room before he woke. He asked me to go. I said I would come back. I did not. I have carried the leaving as if it were the whole event.*

Below it, in darker ink, I have added a sentence at some later point, the letters pressed so hard they have cut into the paper.

*He died while I was gone.*

I touch the groove beneath the words. The statement is true, but it has always stood in my mind as if it were a verdict. I read the entry again, looking for the part I have omitted by refusing to look directly at it. There is no mention of the nurse who came into the hall, no mention of the call I ignored because I was standing outside the hospital with my hands shaking. There is no mention of his request.

Mara shifts on the stair below. “What does it say?”

I keep my finger on the page. “That I left. That he died while I was gone.”

“That’s not all of it.”

“No.” I look toward the opening in the floor, where her face is partly hidden by the attic’s shadow. “The first sentence says he asked me to go.”

She does not answer. The silence is not evasive this time. It gives the sentence room.

I turn the page back and find the hospital entry’s first version beneath the one I have been reading. The ink is faded, but I can make out the line I crossed through: *He looked relieved when I said I needed air.* Underneath, I had written, *I abandoned him because I was relieved too.*

My stomach tightens. I remember the room in pieces: the plastic cup beside his bed, the blue curtain that would not close all the way, his mouth dry from the medication. I remember asking whether he wanted me to stay. I remember him saying, “Go home, Next. Sleep.” I remember believing that permission was another form of accusation.

“I made the meaning first,” I say.

Mara’s hand tightens on the rail. “What meaning?”

“That if I wanted to leave, even for an hour, it proved I wanted him gone.” I rub my thumb over the crossed-out words. “Then when he died, I changed it to mean I had chosen wrong. Not just that I was gone. That I had chosen being gone.”

The cassette in my hand has left a rectangular warmth against my palm. I had thought the tape would tell me whether I was guilty. Instead, my brother’s voice has made the question less useful. He had known I wanted to leave. He had heard the shame in me and tried to remove it before it hardened.

“Can we listen to the rest?” Mara asks.

I glance at her. “You can stay there.”

“I will.”

I close the book, but do not put it away. I pick up the cassette, carry the book beneath one arm, and climb down from the attic onto the landing. Mara moves aside to let me pass. I go downstairs first, then cross the hall to the office where the small tape player sits beside the desk. The transition feels deliberate: not an escape from the page, but a way of placing its words beside the recording.

The cassette clicks into the machine. I press play.

Static fills the room. Then my brother’s breath, thin and uneven, comes through the speaker.

“If you’re listening to this because you found the book,” he says, “you’re probably trying to identify the exact moment you failed me. Don’t. There isn’t one.”

I grip the edge of the desk.

“I asked you to leave because I wanted you to leave. I wanted you to go home, eat something, sleep in your own bed. I was sick, not trapped, and you were not my guard.”

A small laugh catches in his throat. It is the laugh I remember from the kitchen, before the hospital took away his strength.

“You looked relieved when I told you to go. Good. You should have been relieved. Loving me didn’t require you to be afraid every minute.”

The tape warbles. I reach toward the player, then stop myself.

“I need you to understand this part. If I die while you’re gone, that won’t turn your leaving into a decision to kill me. It will mean I died while you were gone. Those are different facts. Don’t use my death to erase the difference.”

My knees weaken. I sit in the desk chair.

Mara remains by the door, close enough to hear, far enough not to touch me without permission. The voice continues, softer now.

“And if there’s something you regret more than this, choose carefully. Don’t erase the pain just because it tells you I mattered. You can keep loving me without keeping the punishment.”

The tape clicks off.

For a while I watch the reels stop turning. Then I take the eraser from my pocket and set it on the desk beside the book. I do not lift it. I write beneath the hospital entry: *I left because he asked me to. I decided it meant I had abandoned him. Those are different facts.*

The sentence does not absolve me. That is why I trust it.


The ink shines beneath the lamp. I wait for it to sink into the paper before moving my hand.

Nothing else changes. The hospital entry remains above my addition, with its crossings-out and the deep groove of the sentence about his death. My brother is still dead. His voice is on the cassette, not waiting in another room. I know this, but after hearing him speak so plainly, the silence makes me look toward the door.

Mara stands there, rubbing one thumb along the seam of her sleeve.

“Are you going to use it?” she asks.

The eraser lies beside my wrist. One corner is rounded; another looks sharp enough to lift a single letter. I pick it up. It weighs almost nothing.

“I thought I could choose the worst one,” I say. “Then I thought I could choose the least dangerous one. Something small enough that losing it wouldn’t matter.”

“And now?”

“Now I don’t believe I know what losing it means.”

I hold it above the sentence I have just written. There is a precise temptation here: not to change the night, not to bring him back, but to remove the conviction that I abandoned him. I can almost imagine waking without that familiar argument already underway.

Then I look at my handwriting. I have spent years making his request mean its opposite. The recording has given me something with which to challenge that. I have barely begun.

“If I rub this out,” I say, “I won’t know whether I’ve stopped believing it or just lost the ability to question it.”

Mara pulls out the other chair, then pauses.

“Yes,” I tell her.

She sits. The desk remains between us.

“I can’t tell you it would be safe,” she says.

“I’m not asking you to.”

“I know.”

I put the eraser down. My fingers leave a damp mark on its pale surface.

The choice feels disappointingly physical. There ought to be some unmistakable sensation when I turn away from the thing I have wanted this badly. Instead, my shoulders ache. I am hungry. Part of me is still making a case for trying one word.

“I’m keeping the entry,” I say. “All of it. Including what I wrote tonight.”

Mara looks down at the page. “You can change your mind.”

“Yes.” I draw the book toward me. “But I don’t want to spend every bad night deciding again.”

I find an empty envelope in the desk drawer and slide the eraser inside. Across the front I write what it promises, followed by what I do not know: whether it changes memory, feeling, events, or anything that belongs to someone else. I fold the flap without sealing it.

“Will you keep that away from the book?” I ask. “Somewhere I can’t reach for it without asking you.”

She studies the envelope before accepting it.

“I can do that.”

“This doesn’t make us all right.”

Her hand stops halfway across the desk. “I understand.”

“I need you to tell me things even when telling me makes you look bad. Especially then.”

She sets the envelope in her lap. “I should have given you the tape.”

“Yes.”

“I’m sorry.”

I nod. I hear the apology without trying to turn it into either forgiveness or another charge against her. There will be more to say. Tonight, I have managed to say what I need.

I close the leather cover, leaving my scrap of questions between the pages. Then I lift the cassette from the player and turn it over. On the other side, beneath a rubbed patch of ink, I can make out two words: *first version*.

“The song?” I ask.

Mara leans forward to see. “Yes.”

My thumb catches against the cracked hinge of the case. I have been pursuing explanations so hard that I nearly put the music away unheard.

“Not in here,” I say.

I carry the player out of the office, with the book under my arm. Mara follows me through the hall into the kitchen. I set the book on the counter, clear two unopened envelopes from the table, and plug the player into the socket beside the kettle. The ordinary mess takes a little work to make room for us.

“Tea?” Mara asks.

“Yes, please.”

While she fills the kettle, I slip the cassette back into the player, the song side ready, and press play.

There is a scrape, a cough, then my brother counting himself in too quickly. He misses the first chord. Starts again. His voice is stronger than it is on the spoken recording, though he pushes for a note he cannot quite reach.

I laugh before I can stop myself.

Mara turns from the sink. I point at the player.

“He always blamed the guitar for that.”

“He did.”

The kettle begins its low rumble. I sit and listen through the rough opening, the unfinished line, the place where he changes his mind about the tune without stopping.

When Mara puts my cup down, I move the empty chair out with my foot.

We listen to the rest without touching the book.
