## Chapter 1: The Ledger of Weights

By four o’clock, everyone had taken back their casserole dishes except the woman who had brought the scalloped potatoes. Her blue glass pan stood on the kitchen counter beneath a sheet of foil, holding three pale slices and the impression of a serving spoon.

Mara washed it, dried it, and set it beside the sympathy cards.

There was nothing else she could do for her grandmother.

Outside, the tide was drawing away from the shore, exposing a black shelf of rock. The house had always smelled faintly of the sea, even with the windows shut: salt, damp wood, something mineral in the plaster. Today it smelled of lilies as well.

June had offered to stay.

“I can sleep on the sofa,” she’d said, standing in the kitchen with her coat half on. “You won’t even know I’m here.”

“I’ll know. You snore.”

“Only in houses with ghosts.”

Mara had managed a smile and sent her home.

Now she stood at the foot of the attic stairs, still wearing the black dress she had bought for her mother’s funeral eleven years earlier. It pinched beneath the arms. At forty, she had learned to measure time by the clothes she kept for losing people.

Her grandmother had left her the house, its contents, and a modest account intended, according to the lawyer, “for whatever the roof does next.”

The roof could wait. Mara needed a task with edges.

At the historical society, she spent her days sorting the remnants of other people’s lives: letters, receipts, photographs labeled in hands that no longer existed. She knew how to begin.

She fetched three empty cartons from the pantry and carried them upstairs.

The attic light came on after two pulls. Beneath it lay the familiar geography of neglect: trunks, a broken cane chair, Christmas ornaments packed in newspaper. Mothballs lent their sharpness to the dry air. Near the eaves stood her grandmother’s narrow writing desk, which had disappeared from the downstairs bedroom sometime in Mara’s teens.

She dragged a carton toward it.

“Paper first,” she said aloud.

The sound made the room feel less empty, though not more welcoming.

The top drawer held envelopes, brittle rubber bands, and an address book whose dead occupants had been crossed out with single, decisive lines. The second drawer stuck. Mara pulled until it gave with a wooden shriek.

Inside lay a folded wool scarf and, beneath it, a leather-bound book.

She lifted it with both hands. The leather was dark green, almost black, worn smooth at the corners. No publisher’s mark appeared on the spine. Across the front, in fading gold, was her name.

MARA ELLISON.

Not Margaret, her grandmother’s name. Not some ancestral Ellison whose papers she had forgotten.

Her own.

She rubbed a thumb over the letters. Dust came away, but the gold remained.

Beneath the book lay a small brass object, rectangular and rounded at one end. At first she took it for a paperweight. Then she saw the pale, dense strip set into its underside: an eraser in a metal sleeve, like an elaborate piece of schoolroom equipment.

Words had been engraved along the brass.

One removal. One transfer. No return.

Mara turned it over, looking for a maker’s stamp. There was none.

“Very helpful,” she murmured.

She placed it on the desk and opened the book.

The first page contained only a date and a sentence.

June did not break the blue bowl. I did.

Mara went still.

She had been eight. Her grandmother’s mixing bowl had slipped while she was trying to lift it down for a game neither girl was supposed to be playing. When asked, Mara had pointed at June.

June had stared at her, astonished, then cried through the scolding.

Years later Mara had confessed, and June had said she barely remembered. Mara had never quite believed her.

She turned the page.

I let Mrs. Avery think I had read the book.

Then another.

I did not sit beside Daniel Mercer on the bus after the others began calling him names.

Some entries occupied a line. Others filled half a page. Dates stood above them, precise and unadorned. There were no explanations, no allowance for youth or fear or exhaustion.

The handwriting was hers.

She recognized the open lower loop of the g, the cramped space she left between words when she was ashamed of what she was saying. On the page about Daniel, the pen had pressed hard enough to score the paper.

Her pulse began to beat in her throat.

She turned farther.

The last words I said to Dad were, “Then do it yourself.”

For a moment the attic vanished behind the memory of a telephone cord wound around her finger. Her father had wanted help moving a cabinet. She had been twenty-four, busy with something she could no longer remember.

He had died that evening in the garage.

Mara shut the book.

A gull cried beyond the small attic window. Downstairs, the refrigerator motor started.

Someone had made this.

Her grandmother had known about the bowl. About her father, too. Perhaps June had helped; perhaps this was some grotesquely misjudged exercise in remembrance. But June would never have—

She opened the book near the middle.

The entry there was written in heavy black ink.

I declined the Vienna fellowship to stay with my mother.

Beneath it, the date.

Mara rested her hand over the words. She remembered the acceptance letter’s cream paper, the small apartment she had found online, a window overlooking a courtyard. Then Alma’s diagnosis. The phone call Mara had made standing in this house’s pantry because she did not want her mother to hear.

She pulled her hand away.

“What did you think this would do?” she asked the empty room.

Anger steadied her. She turned back through the pages, looking for some error that would expose the trick.

Instead she found August 1998.

The entry was short.

I found June’s letter to her father in the pocket of my denim jacket. I read it. I put it in the stove because she had written that I made everything about myself.

Mara stopped breathing.

She had told June the jacket had gone through the wash. She had helped her search for the letter, kneeling beside her under the bed.

No one had seen her at the stove. No one knew how carefully she had fed the folded sheets into the little fire her grandmother had lit against the wet August cold.

Even now she remembered one corner refusing to burn.

The book lay open beneath her fingers. Beside it, the brass caught the weak light from the window.

One removal.

She reached across the desk, and her hand closed around the eraser.

## Chapter 2: The Weight of a Thousand Days

Mara carried the ledger downstairs and set it beside the cold kettle. She kept the eraser in her pocket, its brass sleeve pressing against her thigh.

Outside, the sea moved in the dark. Her grandmother had slept through that sound for sixty years. Tonight, with every room empty above her, Mara heard each withdrawing wave.

At two in the morning she opened the book again.

The Vienna entry was several pages in, darker than the others. Beneath a date eleven years old, she read:

I declined the Vienna fellowship to care for my mother. She died anyway. I stayed.

She remembered the envelope.

Cream-colored, heavy, delivered to the historical society on a wet Tuesday. Mara had opened it during lunch beneath a portrait of a shipbuilder. Nine months in Vienna, access to collections she knew only through microfilm, a stipend sufficient for a room and meals. The committee praised her research and requested an answer within ten days.

That evening she read the letter aloud to Alma, who sat by the window with one hand pressed beneath her ribs.

“You must go,” her mother said.

“I haven’t decided.”

“You’ve wanted this since you were twelve.”

“I’ve wanted plenty of things.”

Alma held out her hand for the letter. “Don’t make it smaller on my account.”

Three days later, a doctor said pancreatic cancer. Advanced. He spoke of treatment and managing symptoms. Alma asked precise questions while Mara stared at a diagram on the wall, unable to reconcile its tidy organs with the woman beside her.

She called the fellowship committee from her car. Rain ticked against the windshield; Alma’s bedroom light shone upstairs.

“I’m afraid I have to withdraw.”

The coordinator expressed sympathy. Then she asked, “Would a deferral be possible?”

Mara watched her mother’s shadow cross the curtains.

There might be six months. There might be a year. She could not imagine packing a suitcase while Alma waited to learn which.

“No,” she said. “Thank you, but no.”

For ten months, her days acquired a new order: pills, appointments, sheets, meals. She learned which pharmacy stayed open late and how to lift Alma without hurting her. She learned to sleep lightly on the couch.

There were good afternoons. Alma made a pie sitting at the kitchen table, issuing instructions whenever Mara reached for the wrong bowl. They watched old mysteries and argued over the suspects. In February they drove to the shore and ate French fries in the car, the heater blowing against their ankles.

“You look tired,” Alma said.

“I am tired.”

“I didn’t ask you to do all this.”

“You didn’t have to.”

Alma folded the greasy paper carton. “You’re allowed to be angry.”

Mara had denied it too sharply. Then she had driven them home in silence, ashamed that her mother had needed to comfort her.

By summer, Alma could not climb the stairs. By autumn she slept most of the day. She died on a bright morning while Mara held a cup of water to her lips. The water spilled down her nightdress. For years afterward, Mara remembered reaching for a towel before she understood.

People said she had done everything she could.

She thanked them. She never told them that, some mornings, she opened the fellowship letter before she opened the curtains.

Now, eleven years later, she was still an assistant archivist. She cataloged donated papers, corrected dates on photographs, and helped visitors locate their dead. Her apartment held books, hardy plants, and furniture bought when she expected to move soon.

There had been men, but no marriage. No children. She could not blame all of that on one telephone call. Yet whenever an opportunity arose—a job elsewhere, a trip, an invitation she might have accepted—she found some reason to remain.

Alma had needed her for ten months. Mara had been staying ever since.

The telephone rang at eight.

“You sound terrible,” June said.

“I didn’t sleep.”

“I should have stayed.”

“You offered. I sent you home.”

“I could come back after school.”

Mara looked at the ledger. She imagined explaining it: her own handwriting, the secrets, the small object in her pocket. June would come immediately. She would bring food and ask sensible questions, and Mara would have to show her the entry about the burned letter.

“No,” Mara said. “You’ve done enough.”

A pause. “Are you all right?”

She rubbed a thumb along the book’s leather edge.

“I’m functioning.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

For a moment Mara almost told her. Instead she asked about the drive home, and June described the fog and a deer standing squarely in her lane. Mara laughed where she was meant to.

Before they hung up, June said, “Don’t spend the whole day sorting things. Go outside.”

“I will.”

She stayed at the table until the kitchen brightened. Then she carried the ledger upstairs.

The attic smelled of cedar and damp wool. She laid the book on her grandmother’s desk and opened it to Vienna.

One removal. One transfer. No return.

She turned the brass sleeve, searching for another instruction. Transfer to what? Another page? Another part of herself? There was nothing else.

She could put it away. Take it downstairs, call June back, confess everything. The thought brought such immediate relief that she reached toward the book to close it.

Then she saw the sentence again.

She died anyway. I stayed.

Mara took out the eraser.

If it worked, perhaps she would simply stop aching over the letter. She would still remember Alma, still have those months. Nothing on the brass promised another life. Nothing promised mercy, either.

She rested the pale tip against the first word.

Her hand shook. She thought of Alma passing her a hot French fry, pinched delicately between two fingers. She wanted to keep that. She wanted to keep all of it except the bitterness she had brought into every year afterward.

“I don’t want to regret you,” she whispered.

She rubbed.

The ink lifted in a thin black curl.

Mara stopped, breathing through her mouth. The first words were gone. Beneath them the paper looked scraped and tender.

She rubbed again, across the fellowship, across her mother’s illness, across the final, stubborn sentence. Black fragments gathered at the edge of her hand.

Then there was only a blank scar.

The desk lurched beneath her. Mara seized its edge, but the floor had tilted too, and the attic window swelled with light.

She tried to release the eraser.

The house slipped away from her, and the world went white.

## Chapter 3: The Rewritten World

Mara woke to sunlight across a white ceiling.

For several seconds she lay still, listening to an unfamiliar room breathe. A machine hummed somewhere beyond the walls. Traffic murmured far below. Beside her, a man slept on his back, one arm stretched across the empty half of the bed.

She turned.

He had dark hair silvering at the temples and a narrow, handsome face softened by sleep. The bed was enormous, the sheets gray linen. Beyond a wall of windows, towers and rooftops rose beneath a clear blue sky.

Mara sat up. The room tilted.

The last thing she remembered was the attic: the ledger, the blank scar where the Vienna entry had been, the brass eraser slipping from her fingers. Then the window had swollen with white light.

The man stirred. “Hey. You all right?”

Mara pressed her hands into the mattress. “Who are you?”

His eyes opened fully. Concern crossed his face.

“Adrian.”

The name meant nothing.

He pushed himself up. “Mara?”

She looked down. She wore a pale-blue silk camisole she had never owned. A gold chain lay against her collarbone. On her left hand was a wedding ring.

Adrian followed her gaze. “You hate waking up after red-eyes, but you didn’t fly anywhere.”

“What day is it?”

“Tuesday.”

“The date?”

He told her.

It was the same date—the day after Margaret’s funeral, the day Mara had entered the attic. Yet this room held the bright, expensive calm of a life that had been proceeding without her.

She reached for the phone on the bedside table. It was a black glass rectangle, not her cracked old device. The screen opened to her face.

Notifications filled it.

**Interview: Ashworth Museum’s New Exhibition.**

**Trustees’ breakfast, 8:30.**

**Press call—Vienna Collection feature.**

Her calendar showed meetings from morning until evening. Beneath her name was a title:

**Mara Ellison, Chief Curator.**

She let the phone fall into her lap.

Adrian sat up. “You’re frightening me.”

“I don’t know where I am.”

“Our apartment.”

“I don’t know you.”

His expression emptied. “Did you hit your head?”

“No.”

“Do you know who you are?”

“Mara Ellison.”

“Our address?”

She looked toward the windows. “No.”

He stood, keeping his distance. “I’m calling a doctor.”

“Don’t.” She rose too quickly and caught the edge of the dresser. “I’m fine. I just need to understand.”

She opened the photographs on her phone.

There were hundreds. Mara at a podium beneath the Ashworth Museum’s gold crest. Mara in Vienna, laughing beside a fountain. Mara in formal clothes before paintings she recognized from catalogues. Mara and Adrian on a beach, his arm around her waist.

She looked confident in every photograph. Not merely composed, as she had learned to be at the historical society, but certain. In none of them did she see the woman who had spent eleven years refusing to leave Maine.

A framed photograph stood on the dresser. She and Adrian posed before a courthouse, married. Her hand rested in his. The ring was visible.

“How long?” she asked.

“Almost six years.”

Six years of a marriage she had not lived. Six years that had somehow filled the space left by one erased line.

“Were we happy?”

Adrian’s face tightened. “That’s a strange question.”

“Answer me.”

“We’ve been happy.”

The careful wording chilled her.

She crossed to the closet. Inside hung rows of expensive clothes arranged by color. A garment bag bore the date of tonight’s museum opening. On a shelf lay a familiar camel-colored coat.

Her coat from the old life. The one she had worn to Alma’s hospital appointments, with its shiny elbows and torn pocket. Here it had been cleaned and mended, preserved like evidence.

Mara gripped the closet door.

“What happened to my mother?”

Adrian did not answer.

She turned. “What happened to Alma?”

“She’s alive.”

The words seemed to strike the air from the room.

“What?”

“Your mother. Alma. She’s alive.” His brow furrowed. “You spoke to her last week.”

“No.”

“Mara—”

“She died.”

He went still.

“She died eleven years ago,” Mara said. “I was there. She couldn’t swallow. I held water to her lips. I reached for a towel, and when I looked back—”

“Stop.”

“She died.”

Adrian stared at her as though she had become someone else. “You need a doctor.”

Mara opened her contacts. Names she did not recognize slid past her thumb: trustees, reporters, museum directors. Then she saw it.

**Mom.**

She had dialed that number so many times after Alma’s death that it had become a ritual, a door she knew would never open.

Her finger pressed the call button.

One ring.

Two.

A woman answered, slightly breathless. “Mara? I’m in the garden. What is it?”

Mara could not speak.

“Hello?”

“Mom?”

A pause. Wind moved faintly across the line.

“Yes, darling. Who else would it be?”

Mara closed her eyes. The voice was older than memory but unmistakable—the rasp at the end of certain words, the impatience threaded with affection.

“Mom,” she whispered.

“Are you all right?”

“I wanted to hear your voice.”

“You heard it three days ago.”

“I know.”

“Are you ill?”

Mara pressed her free hand over her mouth. The hospital room rose before her: the oxygen machine, the plastic cup, Alma’s fingers cooling in hers. Then it shattered, replaced by sunlight on a garden she had never seen.

“Could I see you?” Mara asked.

“Of course. Today?”

“Yes.”

Alma hesitated. “Lunch, then. At one?”

Mara looked at Adrian. He stood near the bed, watching her with frightened concern. She could not tell whether he feared for her or feared her.

“At one,” she said.

When the call ended, Mara held the phone against her chest.

Adrian said, “What did she say?”

“She wants to meet me for lunch.”

“That’s good.”

Mara looked at the wedding ring, the photographs, the calendar crowded with achievements she remembered earning and did not remember living.

“Is it?” she asked.

Outside, the city flashed in the morning sun. Somewhere in it, her mother was alive and waiting to see the daughter who had abandoned her—or perhaps the daughter who had never left.

Mara dressed for lunch.

## Chapter 4: Strangers

Alma was already seated when Mara reached the restaurant, her cane hooked over the spare chair.

For eleven years Mara had carried her mother at fifty-eight: the thinning hair, the yellowed skin, the small astonishment of her last breath. The woman beside the window had white hair cut sharply beneath her ears and a mouth drawn slightly downward on one side. She wore lipstick. She was studying the menu through red glasses.

Mara stopped so abruptly that a waiter nearly struck her with a tray.

“You’re letting the cold in,” Alma said.

Mara bent to kiss her. Her mother smelled of face powder and wool. Alive was not a sufficient word for it. There was warmth beneath the powder, an irritated breath against Mara’s cheek.

She held on too long.

“What’s all this?”

“I’m glad to see you.”

“So you said on the phone.”

Mara sat. On the table, Alma’s left hand rested beside her water glass, the fingers gently curled. When she reached for the menu, she used her right.

“What happened to your hand?”

Her mother looked up.

“The stroke, Mara.”

“Of course.”

“Eight months ago. We needn’t pretend it was memorable for everyone.”

Mara opened her napkin beneath the table.

A waiter came. Alma ordered chowder and asked him to have the kitchen cut the bread. Mara ordered the same because she could not have named a food.

“You look tired,” Alma said.

“Margaret’s funeral.”

“Yes. I do know where you were yesterday.”

For a moment they listened to cutlery against china.

Mara tried to find a question that would not expose the impossible thing between them.

“Do you ever think about when you were first ill?”

“The cancer?”

Mara nodded.

“When somebody orders me to eat more vegetables, generally.”

“How did you—” She caught herself. “I keep thinking about how different things could have been.”

Alma took off her glasses.

“The treatment worked. I was fortunate. You’ve heard the doctors say it often enough.”

Fortunate. A word large enough to conceal everything Mara did not understand. In one life a treatment had failed; in another, it had not. She wanted dates, hospital records, an account of every cell. Instead she watched her mother struggle to tear the paper sleeve from a straw.

Mara reached across.

“I can manage.”

“I know.”

“Then let me.”

She withdrew her hand.

The chowder arrived. Alma waited until the waiter had gone.

“Is this about Vienna?”

The name made Mara’s stomach contract.

“What do you mean?”

“Your sudden concern. The questions. Are you writing something?”

“No.”

“Good. I’d rather not become an anecdote in another interview.”

Mara stared at the untouched bread.

“There were nurses,” Alma said. “Very competent women. You made sure of that. One of them used to call me Alice.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You sent flowers from Austria. Beautiful flowers. I had to ask the nurse to change the water.”

Mara remembered changing sheets at three in the morning. Alma’s weight against her shoulder. The cheap hand cream they had both used until its smell became unbearable.

“I wanted you to be looked after.”

“You left me when I needed you. You chose your career over your mother.”

Alma said it quietly, almost politely. Then she lifted her spoon, as though they had settled a minor error on the bill.

Mara could not tell her about the towel she had reached for after her mother stopped breathing. She could not claim credit for ten months this woman had never lived.

“I’m here now,” she said.

“Yes,” Alma answered. “For lunch.”

***

Adrian was home when she returned, standing at the kitchen counter with his phone in his hand.

“How was she?”

“Angry.”

“With me or you?”

There was a familiar shape to the question, a joke polished by repetition. Mara did not know how to answer it.

He set down his phone and touched her shoulder.

“I moved your interview. And I’ve found someone who can see you tomorrow morning.”

“A doctor?”

“A neurologist.” His hand tightened briefly. “Please don’t make me persuade you.”

“All right.”

He kissed her forehead, carefully, as if testing a surface for heat. She wanted to put her arms around him, but could not tell whether that was something they did. His hand had already fallen away.

At four, she went to the museum.

She could not bear another room full of possessions that implied she knew how to live there.

At the Ashworth, the security guard greeted her by name. A young woman met her outside the lifts with proofs to approve, and nobody asked whether she knew what she was doing.

Before Mara had found a way to confess that she did not, she saw the error.

“This attribution needs qualifying,” she said, pointing to a label. “The workshop mark isn’t proof of his hand.”

The young woman sighed with relief. “That’s what I told Publications.”

In the gallery, Mara stopped beneath a suspended light.

“It’s catching the varnish. Lower the angle.”

People listened. A technician climbed a ladder. The glare slipped off the painted face, and a woman emerged from beneath it, grave and luminous.

Mara knew this work. Eleven years of sorting local deeds had not erased what she had studied, what she loved. Yet the authority with which they received her belonged to someone else. She stood in that woman’s shoes while assistants thanked her.

In her office, she shut the door and opened the computer.

There were biographies, interviews, photographs. Vienna appeared everywhere: the beginning of a career presented as inevitable. She searched for June.

The school website loaded slowly.

June Mercer, Visual Arts, Grades 6–8.

In the photograph June stood beside a display of paper masks. She wore a gray cardigan, her hair pulled back severely. Mara enlarged the image, looking for the friend who had called yesterday—another yesterday—to ask whether she had eaten.

Farther down the search results was a divorce notice, three years old.

Mara found June in her contacts. Their last message was sixteen months old.

*If you’re coming up, let me know.*

No answer beneath it.

She took her coat from the chair. Outside, evening traffic had begun to gather along the museum steps.

By the time her assistant called about the opening, Mara was on the northbound road.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “I have to go home.”

She kept driving toward the town she remembered, and the only person there she still knew how to ask for.

## Chapter 5: The Mirror in June

The hardware store had closed by the time Mara arrived. Behind its dark windows, coils of rope hung above the snow shovels, and a cardboard fisherman advertised a sale on work gloves. She had called June from the road. After a long silence, June had given her the address.

“Side door,” she had said. “The front stairs don’t go anywhere anymore.”

Mara climbed a narrow flight smelling of sawdust and old cooking oil. At the top, a television murmured behind one door. Behind the other she heard a chair scrape.

June opened it with the chain still on.

For a moment neither spoke. In Mara’s memory, they had talked only yesterday: June offering soup, offering company, knowing better than to press. This woman looked at her as though she were a debt collector.

Then June closed the door, slipped the chain free, and let her in.

The apartment was smaller than Mara’s museum office. A table stood beneath a window overlooking the alley, its surface crowded with student drawings, unopened mail, and a bowl holding two softening apples. A drying rack occupied most of the space between the sofa and the radiator.

“You could have given me more notice,” June said, moving a stack of sketchbooks from a chair.

“I know.”

“I’d have bought something.”

“Please don’t.”

June looked at her coat, then her shoes. “Well. I didn’t.”

Her hair, once the red-brown of wet chestnuts, was threaded heavily with gray. She wore slippers with flattened heels. There was a split beside her thumbnail, red and raw.

Mara sat. On the table, a child had drawn a house with enormous windows. June had written in green pencil: *I like how you’ve made room for everyone.*

“How are you?” Mara asked.

June gave a short laugh. “You drove three hours to ask that?”

“I wanted to see you.”

“Why?”

There was no answer Mara could give that would not sound either false or deranged.

“Margaret’s funeral,” she said at last. “It made me think.”

“I’m sorry about her.”

“She loved you.”

“I loved her too.”

The past tense settled between them.

June opened a kitchen cupboard and took down a bottle of red wine. “I’ve got this. It’s terrible.”

“That’s all right.”

“It might have been open since Sunday.”

She poured it into two mismatched tumblers. Mara tasted something sour and faintly metallic.

For several minutes they discussed school. June had thirty-two children in one class and enough good brushes for eleven. The kiln had been disconnected after an inspection. She bought charcoal herself.

“You always wanted to teach,” Mara said.

June turned her glass on the table.

“I wanted to do a number of things.”

“I didn’t mean—”

“No. You wouldn’t.”

Outside, a truck reversed down the alley, its warning beeps reaching them through the glass. Mara looked at the student drawings because she could not bear June’s face.

“Do you still paint?”

“Sometimes.”

“Could I see?”

“Not tonight.”

Mara nodded. Her phone vibrated in her pocket. She silenced it without looking.

“The museum?” June asked.

“Probably.”

“I saw the photograph. The new exhibition.”

“I wasn’t sure you followed any of that.”

“It does turn up.”

June drank, then glanced at Mara with a look that was almost curious.

“Do you remember when we used to say we’d have adjoining studios? Somewhere with terrible plumbing and good light?”

“Yes.”

“We were very particular about the light.”

For an instant Mara heard the old June. She smiled, and June’s expression closed.

“You got yours, anyway.”

“June—”

“What? A room with good light. I’m not accusing you of anything.”

But she set her glass down too hard.

Mara waited. She knew that gesture, the small violence against an object when June was trying to keep her voice level.

“I had the letter out last week,” June said.

“What letter?”

“The fellowship letter. God knows why I keep it.”

Mara’s hand tightened around her glass.

“Vienna?”

June stared at her. “Yes, Vienna.”

“You were offered it?”

A flush rose in June’s cheeks.

“Don’t do that.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You know I was. You were the reserve candidate. They called you after I said no.”

Mara remembered opening her own letter at Margaret’s kitchen table. She remembered the paper trembling. There had been nothing about June, no sense of arriving after a door had closed on someone else.

“Your father,” she said carefully.

“What about him?”

“Was that why?”

June leaned back. The chair gave a tired wooden click.

“He couldn’t manage the stairs by then. Wouldn’t have a stranger in the house. My brother had his children, his business, all the things that apparently counted more than whatever I was doing.”

She picked at the split beside her nail.

“I thought nine months was too long. I thought there’d be other chances.”

Mara could hear the refrigerator running.

“Were there?”

“Not like that.”

June looked toward the window.

“I should have gone. He died anyway. And you went, and you never looked back.”

The words were so nearly her own that Mara felt the glass slipping and set it down.

*She died anyway. I stayed.*

For eleven years she had carried that sentence through grocery aisles, through quiet evenings, through the little rooms of the historical society. She had rubbed it from a page, and here it was in June’s mouth.

One removal. One transfer.

Not forgotten. Given to someone else.

“Are you all right?” June asked.

“No.”

June looked away. “Well. I didn’t invite you here to make you feel guilty.”

“I know.”

She could not ask June to comfort her. She made herself remain seated until she could speak without doing that.

“I’m sorry I left your message unanswered.”

“It was a long time ago.”

“Yes,” Mara said. “It was.”

At the door, she asked if she could call again.

June folded her arms. “You can call.”

Downstairs, Mara stood beside the dark hardware-store window. She had come hoping to find someone who still knew her. Instead she had found the place where her missing pain had gone.

She took out her phone and saved June’s address. Then she sat in the car without starting it, trying to think what repair could mean when an apology was nowhere near enough.

## Chapter 6: The Hollow Crown

For a month, Mara learned to answer to her own name.

At the museum it meant someone decisive, someone who could turn a disputed provenance into three measured questions. At home it meant a woman who knew which side of the bed was hers, which friends disliked each other, why Adrian sometimes stopped speaking when she entered a room.

The neurologist found no obvious explanation. There were scans, blood tests, instructions to rest. Adrian kept the appointment cards in a kitchen drawer. Neither of them mentioned that the person he wanted restored might never have existed for her.

She apologized for missing the opening. She mastered the museum calendar. She called Alma on Sundays and learned not to ask too many questions about her treatment.

Twice she telephoned June. The first conversation concerned a broken school boiler. The second lasted four minutes.

Then the prize nomination came.

“The Wexler,” her assistant said, standing in the office doorway with both hands pressed over her mouth. “Mara. You’re on the shortlist.”

Mara knew the prize. In her old life she had once spent a lunch hour reading an interview with its winner, then returned to cataloging water-damaged tax records.

Now everyone was embracing her.

The museum planned a dinner that became a gala. A sponsor paid for flowers. Her portrait appeared on the website above the words *A Life Devoted to Seeing Differently.*

She sent June an invitation with a note: *I would like you there.*

For three days she regretted sending it. On the fourth, June replied: *What would you like me to do there?*

Mara wrote, *Nothing. Just come.*

She could not think of a less honest answer.

***

Adrian stood behind her as she fastened an earring on the evening of the gala.

“Congratulations,” he said.

“You’ve already said that.”

“I wasn’t sure you’d heard.”

Their reflections met. He wore a dark suit and the expression he used when answering questions from doctors: attentive, composed, carefully unalarmed.

“Are you proud of me?” she asked.

“Of course.”

“What are you proud of?”

He glanced toward the bedroom door. “Your work. Your determination. You always knew where you were going.”

“And if I didn’t?”

“Mara, we’re due downstairs.”

She turned from the mirror.

“If I left the museum. If I wanted something else.”

For a moment he looked almost angry.

“We built a life around this. Around what mattered to you.”

She believed him. That was the worst of it. He had loved a woman whose ambitions supplied their direction, whose certainty made his own choices easier. Faced with Mara uncertain and frightened, he could be kind, could arrange appointments and remember her medication. He could not seem to find her.

“I’m still here,” she said.

“I know.”

But he said it as reassurance, not recognition.

***

The museum’s central hall smelled of lilies and hot electrical cables. Above the tables, suspended glass caught the light and broke it into pale shards. People she barely knew kissed her cheeks. Everyone seemed to possess a small, polished story about her.

At dinner, Adrian rested his hand briefly over hers when the director praised her “uncompromising vision.”

Then she was at the lectern.

Her prepared speech lay beneath the microphone. She had revised it that afternoon, removing a graceful sentence about the necessity of leaving home.

“Thank you,” she began. “This work has given me more than I ever expected.”

Beyond the first tables, the audience dissolved into shadow.

“We speak about ambition as though it belongs to one person. As though a career is made by the person whose name appears on the door.”

A waiter stopped moving beside a pillar.

“But every decision asks something of other people. Our freedom can depend on someone else agreeing to stay.”

She saw June at the back.

No evening dress, no champagne glass. A black cardigan over a blue shirt. Her arms were folded tightly, one hand gripping the opposite elbow.

Mara looked down.

“I’ve been thinking about sacrifice,” she said.

The word sounded intolerable in that room, among the flowers bought to honor her.

She abandoned the next paragraph, thanked the trustees, and stepped away to applause.

June was waiting near the coatroom.

“You came,” Mara said.

“I wanted to hear what you’d say.”

“June—”

“Did you mean that? About other people staying?”

“Yes.”

“Did you mean me?”

Mara hesitated. June gave a small, disbelieving laugh.

“You invited me to watch them congratulate you.”

“I wanted to see you.”

“You know where I live.”

Behind them someone called Mara’s name. June looked toward the glittering hall, then back at her.

“You have my life. You took the thing I wanted most, and you don’t even deserve it.”

Mara opened her mouth.

There were answers she might have given: that the fellowship committee had chosen its reserve, that June had made her own decision, that talent and eleven years of work were not nothing.

None belonged to what she knew.

June waited. At last she pulled her coat from the attendant’s hands and left.

***

Mara told Adrian she needed to go to Maine.

“Tonight?”

She was already taking her car keys from her bag.

“Please don’t follow me.”

He stood beneath the museum lights, looking humiliated and worried. She left him there with both feelings.

Three hours later, Margaret’s house rose out of the dark. Mara had not returned since the funeral. The key stuck as it always had; she lifted the handle and felt the lock yield.

In the attic, her evening shoes caught between the floorboards.

The ledger lay on the desk.

She had half expected it to be gone, another object displaced by the life she had chosen. Instead her name still faded across the green leather.

She opened it with cold hands.

The childhood lies remained. Her father’s last afternoon. June’s letter, burned unread by anyone except the person it had not been meant for.

Then the Vienna page.

Blank.

Mara laid her palm against the scarred paper. Somewhere south of here, Adrian would be returning to their apartment. Alma would be asleep beside the telephone Mara could still call. In the morning, museum staff would unlock her office.

She would have to put the words back.

She sat down, still wearing her borrowed honors, and could not yet make herself reach for a pen.

## Chapter 7: The Ledger's Echo

The attic had grown colder while Mara sat at the desk. Beneath her palm, the ledger’s blank page felt rough as an old scar.

Downstairs, the kitchen clock struck two. She was still wearing her gala dress. One loose thread caught against a splinter in the chair, and she freed it carefully, grateful for a problem she could solve with her fingers.

The words were gone, but she knew exactly where they belonged.

She turned back through the ledger. The blue bowl she had broken and blamed on June. Daniel Mercer, alone in the schoolyard. Her father’s last afternoon, the cabinet he had asked her to help move.

Then do it yourself.

The book had spared her nothing. June’s burned letter remained, too, its entry as legible as it had been the first time Mara opened the ledger.

Only the Vienna regret was missing.

She ran her fingertip over the damaged paper. It was like probing the gap of a missing tooth: she could not leave the absence alone.

She would have to put it back. Yet she had no reason to believe the book would let her.

Mara opened the desk drawers. The first held receipts and dried rubber bands. The second contained Margaret’s reading glasses, folded over an old electricity bill. In the third, beneath a packet of envelopes, she found the brass eraser.

She had forgotten its smallness. Something that fit between two fingers had rearranged eleven years.

Turning it beneath the lamp, she searched for the inscription.

Only two words remained.

NO RETURN.

Where the rest had been, the brass was smooth.

Mara rubbed it lightly over the scarred page. Nothing happened. She tried again, pressing until the paper bowed beneath her hand. There was no warmth, no lifting ink, no whitening room.

“Please.”

She hated herself for asking. She did not even know what she was asking it to do.

The eraser left a dull smear. Mara set it down before she tore the page.

For a moment she considered calling Adrian. Her phone lay beside the ledger, its screen dark. He deserved to know she had arrived safely. He deserved an explanation for the woman who had abandoned him at the gala.

She typed, I’m at the house. I’m safe, and sent it before she could add a promise.

Then she opened the ledger’s front cover, looking for instructions she might have missed.

A narrow line of lettering lay close to the spine, almost concealed where the leather folded inward. She tilted the book toward the light.

What is written may be rewritten only by the hand that first carried it.

She read it again.

Not removed. Rewritten.

Her hand rested beside the words. There was the little burn near her thumb from Alma’s stove, the crooked middle finger she had broken at twelve. These were the hands that had signed away the fellowship. That had measured medicine and washed sheets and held a cup against her mother’s mouth.

She pulled open the bottom drawer again. Beneath the envelopes lay a black fountain pen.

The cap came free with a soft click. Mara tested the nib on the corner of an envelope. Ink welled out, black and dense.

She turned to the empty page.

The sentence came back without effort. She had repeated it for eleven years, although not always in those words. Sometimes she had called it duty. Sometimes bad timing. On the worst nights, alone after work, she had called it the end of her life.

She had expected staying to mean something death could not undo. Instead Alma had died, and Mara had remained in the town, waiting for the sacrifice to become worthwhile.

She lowered the pen, then stopped.

If she wrote only to escape this life, what would she be accepting? Another exchange. June relieved, Adrian gone, the museum surrendered—and Alma dead again. She could dress the bargain in generosity, but she would still be trying to make the ledger choose for her.

Her mother was alive somewhere tonight. Perhaps a lamp was burning beside her chair. Perhaps she was asleep, her good hand resting outside the covers.

Mara pressed her lips together.

She could not make losing that woman feel right. She could not promise that she would never again ache for Vienna.

The pen trembled above the paper.

She remembered an afternoon near the end, when Alma had asked her to open the window. It had been cold, and Mara had argued about drafts until her mother said, with sudden irritation, “I know what the weather is, Mara.”

She had opened it.

For several minutes they had listened to someone raking leaves next door. Alma had closed her eyes. Mara had sat beside her, relieved not to be needed for anything more difficult than company.

There had been hundreds of such minutes. They had not cured her mother. They had not advanced Mara’s career. At the time, she had hardly thought to count them.

She wanted those minutes back—not as evidence that she had chosen correctly, but because she had loved the woman sitting beside her.

The resentment had been real, too. She would have to take that back with everything else.

Mara sat until the ink on the envelope dried and the cold reached her knees. There was no certainty waiting beyond the next breath. Only her own hand, still holding the pen.

At last she bent over the ledger.

She wrote slowly, following the remembered shape of each word.

I declined the Vienna fellowship to care for my mother. She died anyway. I stayed.

The final period shone beneath the lamp.

Mara kept her hand against the page. She had not softened the sentence or added an explanation. The words were hers again, including the ones she had once thought she could not bear.

Around her, the attic remained quiet.

Then, beneath her palm, the ledger grew heavy.

## Chapter 8: Rewriting

The final period shone beneath the attic lamp.

Mara kept her palm against the page. The ledger grew heavier, drawing her wrist toward the desk until the wood creaked beneath it.

Outside, a branch scraped the roof.

Then the sound stopped halfway through.

Black threads rose from the wet ink. The words remained legible, but their shadows lifted, twisting around her fingers. She tried to withdraw her hand. The paper held her—not by the skin, but somewhere deeper, where she had carried that sentence for eleven years.

The lamp narrowed to a white point.

The attic opened.

Beyond the rafters stood Adrian, barefoot in their kitchen, holding a cup of coffee. Morning light fell across the counter. He wore the blue shirt she had watched him iron before the gala.

“You’re awake,” he said.

His voice hurt more than she had expected.

“Adrian.”

He looked up. “What is it?”

She moved toward him, though she could still feel the desk beneath her hand. The apartment assembled around them: pale floors, enormous windows, the bowl where he kept his keys. On the refrigerator was an appointment card from the neurologist. Adrian had written the time twice, once in the margin, as though repetition could make her well.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

He set down the coffee.

She had mistaken his uncertainty for emptiness. He had been living beside a wife who no longer remembered loving him, trying to find the right distance from which to help.

He reached for her.

His fingers passed through hers.

For an instant she saw him clearly: the tired eyes, the small crease between his brows. Then light entered his body, thinning his shirt, his face, the hand still extended toward her.

“Wait,” she said.

But the kitchen was already gone.

She stood beneath the Ashworth’s vaulted ceiling.

Applause rose around her. A glass pressed cold against her fingers. Across the room, her name glowed beneath the words WEXLER SHORTLIST.

Behind her hung a painting she had helped reattribute. She knew the argument, the tiny discrepancy in the inventory, the delight of finding evidence where no one had thought to look. That delight had been real. Her hands knew the work even if her memory could not supply the years.

The applause dissolved into a long, hollow rustle.

The painting faded. Its wall became mist.

At the back of the room stood June.

She wore the gray sweater from the gala, one cuff unraveling. Her arms were folded tightly against her body. Mara started toward her, and the guests slipped away on either side.

“You have my life,” June said.

Mara stopped.

The accusation had no answer that could make it fair. She could not give back eleven years with an apology. She could not ask June to understand what had happened.

“I shouldn’t have asked you to come,” she said.

June watched her.

“I knew where you lived. You were right.”

A movement crossed June’s face, too slight to name. Then she drew a breath that seemed to reach farther into her chest than it had before.

Her shoulders lowered.

The bitterness did not vanish into happiness. She was still tired; the sweater still needed mending. But the fierce tension around her mouth eased. Something that had held her face rigid released its grip.

Mara felt it arrive beneath her ribs.

Cold, familiar, hers.

June lifted a hand toward her own throat, surprised. Behind her, a window brightened. Mara thought she could smell paint.

Then June, too, began to fade.

Mara reached out but did not ask her to stay.

The apartment returned in fragments: Adrian’s books beside the bed, rain against the windows, a photograph of a wedding Mara had never remembered. Museum corridors opened and closed. Faces turned toward her, expecting answers. Somewhere a telephone rang.

Last came Alma.

She sat across a restaurant table, her cane propped against her chair, struggling to open a sugar packet with one hand. Mara leaned forward to help.

Alma looked up.

Mara wanted one more minute. She wanted to hear her mother complain about the coffee, to watch her gather her coat, to know that somewhere beyond the restaurant doors she would go on living.

The sugar packet slipped through Mara’s fingers.

“Ma,” she said.

The table dissolved.

She fell against the attic desk.

Her knees struck the floorboards. For several breaths she stayed there, forehead resting on her arm, listening.

Wind. The branch against the roof. Her own breath.

She wore her old brown coat, not the gala dress. One cuff was frayed where she worried it with her thumb. Dust marked the knee of her trousers. The room smelled of cedar and mothballs and the sea.

Mara raised her head.

Margaret’s blue suitcase stood beneath the eaves. The boxes bore the same cramped labels. Nothing gleamed. Nothing awaited her explanation.

The ledger lay open.

She pulled herself to her feet and read.

I declined the Vienna fellowship to care for my mother. She died anyway. I stayed.

The scar in the paper was gone. Her words rested among the other entries, dark and whole.

Beside the book lay a faint brass-colored stain. She touched it, then searched the open drawer, lifting the envelopes, looking beneath them.

No eraser.

Her phone was in her coat pocket. Its cracked case caught against the lining as she drew it out. On the screen was June’s message from the night after the funeral, asking whether she needed company.

Mara pressed the phone between her palms.

Her mother was dead again.

The thought brought no peace. She bent beneath it, mouth open, and let herself cry.

When she could stand upright, the house was still there. A car passed on the road below. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked.

She laid her hand on the ledger.

There would still be mornings when she wanted Vienna. Accepting the choice did not make every day that followed inevitable; she could see that now. But the ten months beside Alma belonged to her again, including the hours she had counted and the tenderness she had not known how to count.

The book was heavy beneath her palm.

For the first time, she was grateful to feel its weight.

## Chapter 9: The Return

By morning, the fog had lifted from the road.

Mara drove toward town with both hands on the wheel, the sea appearing and vanishing between the pines. Margaret’s house receded in the mirror until it was only a pale shape behind the trees. She had left the ledger in the attic beneath the folded quilt on the desk. Not hidden, exactly. It would remain where it had been waiting, heavy and patient.

June’s message still showed on Mara’s phone.

*Do you need company? I can come over.*

Mara had finally answered:

*I’m coming to you.*

June’s reply had arrived almost at once.

*Good. I’ll put coffee on.*

June opened the door before Mara knocked.

“There you are,” she said. “You look terrible.”

“Thank you.”

“I mean it affectionately.”

“I know.”

June pulled her into a hug. Mara closed her eyes. June’s hair smelled faintly of shampoo and turpentine. There was no hesitation in the embrace, no careful distance, no accusation waiting behind it. It was the friendship Mara remembered: battered, familiar, alive.

For one instant she saw another June, gray with exhaustion beneath the hard light of an apartment above a hardware store, saying, *You have my life.*

Then the image vanished.

June released her and studied her face. “You’ve been crying.”

“I have.”

“Funeral hangover?”

“Something like that.”

“Good. Funerals are supposed to make people cry.”

Mara followed her into the kitchen. It was exactly as she remembered it: yellow curtains, a chipped blue kettle, painted stones lined along the windowsill. A watercolor of the harbor hung above the sink, unfinished but luminous.

“You made that?” Mara asked.

June glanced over. “I made a lot of things when I couldn’t sleep.”

“It’s beautiful.”

“Don’t be dramatic before coffee.”

They sat at the small table while rain tapped against the glass. June talked about her seventh graders, one of whom had glued his self-portrait to the ceiling, and about the school boiler making a noise like a trapped animal. Mara listened. The world had returned to its proper proportions: June’s complaints, the bitter coffee, the ordinary comfort of being known.

At last June reached across the table and touched her wrist.

“You’re really all right?”

Mara looked at their hands. She thought of all the ways an answer could become a lie.

“I’m sad,” she said. “But I’m here.”

June squeezed her wrist. “That counts.”

Afterward, Mara drove to the cemetery.

Alma’s grave lay beneath a maple at the far edge, where the ground sloped toward the marsh. The stone was modest, her name and dates carved beneath a small cross. Lavender grew around it, sparse and woody, with a few purple flowers still holding on.

Mara sat in the wet grass and placed her palm against the stone.

“I thought staying meant I had failed,” she said.

The wind moved through the leaves. Beyond the cemetery wall, a gull cried over the water.

“I thought that because you died anyway, everything I gave up had been wasted. I thought I could be angry only if I was grateful, and that one feeling canceled out the other.”

She drew a breath.

“But they were both true. I was angry. I loved you. I wanted Vienna. I wanted more time with you. None of that made the rest false.”

Her fingers traced the carved edge of Alma’s name.

“I used to wish I had chosen differently. I wished I’d gone, and that you’d lived, and that none of it had cost anything.” She smiled through her tears. “I thought love was supposed to make the choice clean.”

The cemetery offered no answer, and she was grateful. She did not want absolution. She wanted the imperfect love to remain hers.

“Staying wasn’t a failure, Mom,” she said. “It was a gift. You gave it to me, too, even when we were miserable. Especially then, maybe. I don’t know what to do with all the years after you died yet. But I know what those ten months were.”

At the historical society, the building smelled of dust, radiator heat, and old paper. Mara hung up her coat and found Helen Park wrestling with a box of county tax records.

“You’re back,” Helen said.

“I am.”

“How was the funeral?”

“Sad. Good. Both.”

Helen nodded, then glanced at the boxes. “I saved you the 1912 ledgers.”

Mara looked around the narrow office, at the stacks of photographs and uncatalogued tapes. For years she had treated the society as a place to preserve what was over. Now she saw another possibility.

“I’ve been thinking,” she said.

“That usually costs us money.”

“I want to start an oral-history project. The older people in town. Recorded interviews, transcripts, photographs if they have them. Not only dates and buildings. I want the stories people tell about what they chose.”

Helen set down the folder.

“Love, regret, the things they carried,” Mara continued. “The parts that don’t go into official records.”

“That’s ambitious.”

“It can start small.”

“Everything starts small here.” Helen studied her. “You’ll need consent forms, equipment, a filing system, and someone patient enough to listen to Mr. Bell talk about the 1968 lobster strike for six hours.”

“I can do that.”

“You hate Mr. Bell.”

“I can listen anyway.”

Helen smiled. “Then write me a proposal.”

Mara sat at her desk. The computer flickered awake, revealing the familiar cataloging database, its empty fields waiting to be filled. She opened a new document and typed:

**Voices of the Harbor: An Oral History of Choice and Memory**

Her hand hovered above the keyboard.

There were regrets still waiting in her: the blue bowl, the burned letter, her father’s cabinet, Daniel Mercer in the schoolyard. None had vanished. None needed to.

Mara began to write.

She would not erase anything. She would learn to live with the weight and let it show her where she belonged.

## Chapter 10: The Thread

By October, the house smelled less of mothballs and more of things Mara had cooked.

There were onions in a basket beside the stove, muddy boots beneath the back door, a library book lying facedown where Margaret would never have permitted it. The roof had been repaired. During the first hard rain, Mara had stood beneath the attic window listening for the old drip and heard only water moving where it was supposed to go.

That afternoon she went upstairs for a tablecloth.

June was coming at six, bringing bread and a painting she claimed she was tired of looking at. Mara had cleared a place for it in the dining room without knowing its size. She had learned that it was possible to make room before knowing exactly what would arrive.

The tablecloths were in a cedar chest beneath the eaves. On her way there, she passed the desk.

The folded quilt still lay over the ledger.

For months she had left it alone. Not because she feared another world opening, though sometimes, waking, she had reached across an empty bed before remembering whose absence she meant. She had simply been occupied. The oral-history project had received a small grant. There were microphones to borrow, appointments to arrange, hours of recordings to transcribe.

One retired lobsterman had spent forty minutes explaining an engine repair before mentioning the brother he had not spoken to in twenty-six years.

“Leave that part in,” he had told her afterward.

Mara lifted the quilt.

The book looked ordinary in the autumn light: worn green leather, a softened corner, her name almost rubbed away. Beside it, the brass-colored stain remained on the desk. She touched the mark with one finger. Nothing came away.

Then she sat and opened the cover.

The childhood entries seemed impossibly small.

The blue bowl. June’s frightened face when Margaret asked who had broken it. Mara remembered the fierce, brief relief of not being blamed, followed by the misery of watching June search for words.

Last month, washing dishes together, June had dropped one of Mara’s glasses.

“I suppose this is mine too,” she had said.

They had laughed. The bowl had remained broken.

Mara turned a page.

Her father’s cabinet waited where it always had.

*Then do it yourself.*

She could still hear her own irritation, the careless emphasis on *yourself*. For years she had followed those words into the garage, revising the evening until she arrived in time.

Now she remembered something else: her father at the kitchen table, bending a paperclip into a hook to retrieve a ring she had dropped through a heating grate. His patience had annoyed her then. She had wanted the ring immediately.

She rested her palm against the page. She wished she had helped him. She would always wish it. But she had begun to remember whole afternoons with him again, not only his last one.

Farther on lay the summer of 1998.

She had told June about the letter in August, sitting on the back steps with mosquitoes gathering around their ankles.

“You burned it?”

“Yes.”

“And then helped me look for it?”

“Yes.”

June had gone home early. For nine days their messages had been brief and practical. Mara had discovered how much effort it took not to explain herself past the point of apology.

Eventually June called about a leaking pipe.

“I’m still angry,” she said.

“I know.”

“Can you come hold a bucket anyway?”

Mara had gone.

The entry remained. So did June’s anger, sometimes. Mara no longer expected either to disappear because she had finally told the truth.

She turned to Vienna.

*I declined the Vienna fellowship to care for my mother. She died anyway. I stayed.*

Her handwriting was steady. She remembered writing each word, although there was no difference now between this ink and the ink on the other pages.

She read it twice.

The wish was still there: a city she had never visited in this life, work that might have absorbed her, mornings belonging entirely to herself. Alongside it came Alma’s hand closing around hers in the dark. The basin. The damp washcloth. Her mother asking whether the birds had come back to the feeder.

Mara had filled it that morning. She had forgotten that until now.

Outside the attic window, a branch scraped the glass.

She could have stayed in that chair until dusk, following every entry into its adjoining rooms. Instead she closed the book.

It was heavy when she lifted it. She carried it to the shelf beside Margaret’s sewing basket, where a loose red thread trailed over the wicker rim. For a moment it caught beneath the ledger. Mara eased it free and wound it around the spool.

Downstairs, the door opened.

“Something smells dangerously competent,” June called.

“Don’t commit yourself. I haven’t tasted it.”

“I brought emergency bread.”

Mara took the tablecloth from the cedar chest and switched off the attic light.

At the foot of the stairs June stood with a paper bag beneath one arm and a wrapped rectangle under the other. There was blue paint on her wrist. Mara reached for the bread; June surrendered the painting instead.

“Careful,” she said. “The corner’s still wet.”

Above them, the ledger stood among the ordinary possessions of the house, its gold name turned toward the room. Someday another hand might find it and mistake its promise for mercy. For now it waited, holding every word.

Below, Mara helped June carry things to the table.
