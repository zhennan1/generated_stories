## Chapter 1: The Ledger of Weights

The last mourner left a casserole on the porch because Mara would not answer the door. Through the lace curtain she watched the woman hesitate, smooth the foil with her palm, and retreat toward a car beaded with October rain.

For forty years, people had brought food to this house when they did not know what to say. Her grandmother had always returned the dishes washed, with a handwritten note. Now Mara would have to discover whose dishes they were.

She turned away from the window. In the hall, her grandmother's shoes stood beneath the coat hooks, toes pointing toward the door.

“Not those,” Mara said aloud.

The attic would be easier. Nothing up there had been used in years.

She changed out of her funeral dress and put on an old cardigan, leaving the black stockings on. The house was hers now, according to the lawyer: three bedrooms, a leaking porch roof, and a view of a narrow Maine inlet where the tide exposed a wreck of pilings twice a day. Her grandmother had left no instructions about what to keep. Mara, who spent her working days preserving other people's papers, resented this omission more than she could reasonably explain.

At the top of the attic stairs, she pulled the string for the light. A bare bulb illuminated trunks, a dress form, and a wooden desk under the eaves. The air smelled of mothballs and cold wool. Rain ticked against a roof slate.

Her phone vibrated.

June: Tell me you ate something.

Mara typed, There are approximately nine lasagnas downstairs.

That is not an answer.

She put the phone in her pocket before she could be asked another question. June would come if invited. June would also come if she was not invited and became sufficiently worried. Mara wanted company, but not yet the work of being grateful for it.

She began with a carton of Christmas cards. At the historical society, she would have labeled a box before opening it. Here she knelt on the floor and tipped its contents into her lap.

There were the usual winter churches, glittering fir trees, photographs of children who now had children. Beneath them lay an envelope marked Boiler warranty in her grandmother's upright hand. Inside were pressed violets.

“Oh, Gran.”

She sat for a moment holding the envelope open.

Then she put it aside and moved to the desk.

A square of faded curtain covered its surface. When she lifted it, dust caught in the light, and something small rolled against the raised wooden edge with a metallic click.

A book lay beneath the cloth. Brown leather, no ornament, the corners worn almost smooth. Mara brushed its cover with her sleeve and uncovered a name in fading gold.

MARA ELLISON.

She touched the letters. Perhaps a birthday present never given. Her grandmother had bought things years in advance, then forgotten where she put them.

Beside the book lay a brass object the length of Mara's thumb, rounded at one end and flat at the other. She picked it up. It was heavier than she expected, its underside pale and faintly yielding beneath her nail. An eraser in a metal sleeve, she thought, though she had never seen one like it.

Tiny letters ran along its side.

One removal. One transfer. No return.

“Useful,” she murmured. “Very clear.”

She set it down and opened the book.

The first page held a single sentence.

I told June I hadn't seen the blue glass horse.

Mara stopped breathing through her nose.

She had been eight. June's glass horse had broken against the Ellisons' kitchen tiles, its slender legs scattering under the stove. Mara had swept it up before June came back from the bathroom. For weeks afterward, she had helped June look for it.

The handwriting was her own. Not a child's: her present handwriting, with the cramped capital I and the peculiar open loop of the d.

She turned the page.

I let Mrs. Bell believe I had read the book.

Another page.

I didn't go back to apologize to Dad. The last thing I said was, Then fix it yourself.

She closed the ledger.

From downstairs came the steady knock of a loose shutter. She listened until she could separate it from the sound of her pulse.

A trick required someone to make it. June knew about the horse now; Mara had confessed over wine in their twenties. Her grandmother might have overheard the argument with her father. An elaborate present, then, or a punishment. Neither explanation fitted the woman they had buried that morning, but Mara opened the book again, determined to find the mistake that would make it ordinary.

Some pages carried only a few words. Others were crowded with paragraphs. She saw names she had not thought of in years, a dog she had been impatient with, a thank-you letter unwritten. She began turning faster.

Then the ink changed.

Near the middle, black letters had scored the thick paper so deeply that she could feel them on the reverse.

I declined the Vienna fellowship to stay and care for my mother. She died anyway, and I have never stopped wondering who I might have been if I had gone.

Mara gripped the edge of the desk.

Eleven years, and still the word Vienna could do that: open a room inside her where nothing had been touched.

She looked toward the stairs. For a moment she wanted June so badly she reached for her phone. But what would she say? That someone had written down the ugliest part of something June already knew?

She turned back toward the beginning, looking for a date, a publisher's mark, any evidence of another hand.

Summer, 1998.

I heard Mum crying in the pantry after she lost her job. I stood outside the door until she stopped. When she asked whether I'd just come in, I said yes, because I didn't want her to need me.

Mara sat down on the desk chair without pulling it out. Its wooden back struck the wall.

She remembered the cold brass pantry knob beneath her fingers. Her mother's muffled breath. The terrible adolescent calculation: if she opened the door, the afternoon would no longer belong to her.

No one had seen her standing there. Her mother had never mentioned it. Mara had not confessed it to June, to her grandmother, even to herself in words as plain as these.

Downstairs, the shutter knocked again.

She read the entry once more. Then she looked at the brass object beside the book, at the three brief sentences engraved along its side.

Her hand closed around the eraser.

## Chapter 2: The Weight of a Thousand Days

Mara slept badly in the bedroom at the back of the house, beneath a quilt that smelled faintly of cedar and her grandmother’s lavender soap. Rain worried the windows all night. Whenever she surfaced from sleep, she expected to see the ledger on the bedside table. It was not there. She had left it in the attic, closed beneath the square of curtain, with the eraser beside it.

Still, Vienna followed her into every dream.

Eleven years earlier, the acceptance letter had arrived on a Tuesday in March. She remembered the envelope first: cream paper, foreign stamps, her name typed with an accent over the a that no American office would have bothered with. The fellowship would place her in an archive in Vienna for a year, studying the correspondence of a forgotten nineteenth-century collector. There would be a stipend, a room, instruction in German, and a chance—so improbable she had been afraid to say it aloud—to become someone whose life was not a corridor leading back to Maine.

She had carried the letter to her mother’s kitchen.

Alma sat at the table with a cup of tea gone cold between her hands. She looked tired in a way that sleep did not repair.

“I got it,” Mara had said.

Her mother looked up. “Got what?”

“The fellowship.”

For one bright second Alma’s face opened. “Mara.”

Then she coughed. Not the ordinary winter cough she had been dismissing for weeks, but a deep, tearing sound that folded her over the table. The doctors found the tumor in April. By May, the word treatment had acquired a grammar of its own: appointments, scans, ports, infusions, numbers that rose and fell without ever meaning what Mara wanted them to mean.

She called the fellowship committee from the pay phone outside the oncology ward. The receiver was warm against her ear. A woman named Dr. Feldman listened while Mara explained that a family emergency had made travel impossible.

“We can defer you,” Dr. Feldman said. “One year.”

Mara looked through the glass at Alma sleeping in a vinyl chair, her head tilted toward the window.

“No,” Mara said. “I can’t ask you to hold it.”

“We’re not holding it for us.”

But Mara had already begun apologizing. She apologized for the inconvenience, for the paperwork, for having accepted something she could not use. When the call ended, she stood in the hospital corridor with the dead receiver in her hand and felt, briefly, clean. She had chosen correctly. She had chosen love. The certainty lasted until the next morning.

Caregiving was not a single act. It was a thousand small refusals, each one too minor to protest. She refused Vienna, then sleep, then a better job, then invitations that required explaining why she might have to leave early. She learned how to lift Alma without hurting her, how to disguise medication in applesauce, how to answer the same question six times in an hour.

June came most Sundays with groceries and loud stories from the school where she worked. Sometimes Mara resented the liveliness of her friend’s voice. Sometimes she leaned against June in the kitchen and let herself be held.

“Go for an afternoon,” June said once. “I’ll stay with her.”

Mara shook her head. “You have your own life.”

“So do you.”

At the time, Mara believed this was evidence of devotion: to make herself unnecessary to everyone but Alma.

Alma died in January, during a snowstorm that turned the windows white. Mara had been in the next room, heating broth. She heard the silence before she understood it. The hospice nurse came. The nurse spoke gently. Someone made a phone call. By dawn, Mara was sitting beside the bed with her mother’s hand cooling inside both of hers.

Nothing in the room resembled Vienna. That was the insult of it. The world had not opened a door or offered a judgment. The kettle clicked off. Snow slid from the roof. Alma was dead, and Mara had stayed.

In the gray morning she found the fellowship letter in a drawer beneath old tax forms. She did not burn it. She folded it into quarters and put it back.

Years gathered around that fold.

At forty, Mara was an assistant archivist at the Penobscot Historical Society, where she cataloged other people’s decisions: ship manifests, diaries, farm ledgers, letters from men who went west and women who waited for them. She lived in a second-floor apartment with a good view of a brick wall. She had no husband, no child, no particular plan beyond Friday and the next box.

People called her dependable. It was a word that sounded like praise until she heard the vacancy inside it.

Her phone rang at ten past eight the next morning. June.

Mara watched the name pulse on the screen before answering. “Hello.”

“Did you sleep?”

“Some.”

“That means no. Have you eaten?”

“There’s food.”

“Mara.”

She sat on the edge of the bed. In the hall, the house creaked as if someone were moving through it. “I’m fine.”

June exhaled. “You said that at the funeral while standing next to a casket.”

“I’m handling things.”

“You don’t have to handle them alone.”

The kindness in June’s voice made Mara close her eyes. She could tell June about the ledger. She could say, There’s a book upstairs with my name on it, and it knows things I never told you. June would come. She would put on her coat, drive through the rain, and make the impossible less private.

But the ledger had written down her life as if it were a collection of evidence. Mara could not bear to hand it to anyone else.

“I’ll come back tomorrow,” June said. “I’m bringing soup.”

“Don’t.”

“Soup is already happening.”

After they hung up, Mara remained still, listening to the quiet. She understood then that she had not merely declined one fellowship. She had organized herself around that refusal. Every ordinary choice afterward had pointed backward, as though she were building a shrine from absences.

She went upstairs.

The attic was colder than the bedroom. Dust silvered the desk and the ledger waited beneath the cloth exactly as she had left it. Mara opened to the black entry. The sentence seemed larger in daylight.

I declined the Vienna fellowship to stay and care for my mother. She died anyway, and I have never stopped wondering who I might have been if I had gone.

Her hand shook as she lifted the eraser. The brass was cold, almost wet. Along its side, the words caught the light.

One removal. One transfer. No return.

She thought of Alma’s hand in hers. She thought of June saying, So do you, and of all the years Mara had treated a life as something that could be postponed without consequence.

Then she placed the pale underside against the first word.

The eraser moved without pressure. Ink rose in a thin black curl, gathering like ash along the edge. Beneath it, the paper did not return to white. It opened into a scar, smooth and colorless, the shape of the sentence disappearing beneath her hand.

Mara drew one breath.

The attic tilted. The desk, the roof beams, the rain-streaked window—all of it swung away from her. Somewhere, impossibly far off, June was calling her name.

The blank scar widened.

The world went white.

## Chapter 3: The Rewritten World

Light lay across her face. Not attic light, sifted through dirty glass, but a clean, extravagant brightness.

Mara opened her eyes beneath a white ceiling. A curtain breathed beside an open window. Somewhere below, a truck reversed with three discreet electronic cries.

She was in bed.

A man's bare arm rested on the sheet between them.

She scrambled backward. Her heel struck a wooden bedframe, and the man woke with a startled intake of breath. He was perhaps her age, dark-haired, with gray beginning at his temples.

“What's wrong?” he asked.

“Who are you?”

He lifted his head from the pillow. “Mara?”

She clutched the sheet against a nightdress she had never seen. “Where am I?”

For a moment he looked offended. Then he sat up, keeping his hands visible, as though approaching a frightened animal.

“At home. It's Adrian.”

She stared.

“Your husband,” he said.

On his left hand was a plain gold ring. She looked down and found its narrow companion on her own finger.

The room was large, its walls almost bare. Through the window she could see rooftops and, beyond them, the pale vertical shape of a church steeple. Nothing smelled of cedar. Nothing creaked.

“I was at my grandmother's house,” she said.

“You came back yesterday afternoon.”

“No.”

He glanced at the bedside clock. “You barely ate. You said you wanted to sleep.” His voice softened. “Was it a dream?”

She remembered the eraser moving beneath her fingers. Ink lifting. The instruction cut into brass.

One transfer.

“I don't know.”

Adrian reached toward her, then stopped. “Do you have a headache? Can you tell me what day it is?”

She gave him the date. He nodded. It was the same day she had climbed into the attic. There should have been rain at the window, June's voice still close in her ear.

“I need a minute,” she said.

“If you're confused, we should call someone.”

“Please. Just a minute.”

He studied her before getting out of bed. He found a robe on a hook without looking for it.

“I'll make coffee. Don't stand up too quickly.”

At the doorway he turned. “I'm downstairs.”

It was both reassurance and an instruction.

Mara waited for his footsteps to recede, then searched the bedside table. No ledger. No brass eraser. There was a novel with a receipt marking her place, reading glasses in a leather case, and a phone charging on a white cord.

The phone opened to her face.

She nearly dropped it.

Her calendar was crowded with colored blocks. Ashworth Museum: collections meeting. Interview with Arts Quarterly. Opening remarks—Wednesday preview. There was an appointment labeled Adrian / dinner, as if the man in her bed required scheduling.

She opened her email. Hundreds of unread messages waited beneath her name and a signature identifying her as Curator of European Collections, Ashworth Museum.

Her old supervisor at the historical society had once praised her for processing nine boxes in a week.

Here, someone wanted approval to borrow a painting from Prague.

She found a photograph of herself on the museum's website. She stood on a staircase in a black suit, one hand resting on the stone rail. Her hair was shorter. Her mouth held an expression she recognized from no mirror: amused patience, the certainty of being listened to.

The accompanying profile mentioned Vienna in its first paragraph.

Mara pressed the heel of her hand against her breastbone. She remembered the hospital pay phone, the warmth of the receiver. She remembered carrying a basin down a hallway at three in the morning. Those things had happened. No photograph could persuade her otherwise.

She went into the adjoining bathroom. Her own face waited above a basin wide enough to wash a child in. There were unfamiliar jars on the shelf, a second toothbrush, a small framed photograph beside the door.

In it she and Adrian stood outside a registry office. He was laughing. She had tilted her face toward him, eyes closed against the sun.

She touched the glass where her cheek should have been warm.

From downstairs came the measured clatter of cups.

Back on the bed, she opened her contacts. June's name was there. Her thumb hovered over it. How could she ask whether soup was still happening, whether yesterday had belonged to both of them?

Then she saw another name.

Mum.

Mara stopped breathing.

She had kept Alma's number for months after the funeral, dialing it once just to hear the recorded greeting. Eventually a stranger had answered, and she had apologized and deleted it. She still knew the digits. The number on this screen was the same.

It might be an old contact carried from one phone to another. It might mean nothing.

She pressed Call.

The ringing seemed to happen inside her ribs. Once. Twice. She stood, then sat again, unable to decide which position a person should occupy when calling the dead.

“Hello?”

Mara closed her eyes.

The voice was thinner, the syllables separated by a slight effort, but she knew it before the word was finished.

“Mum?”

“Yes?” A pause. “Mara, is that you?”

She bent forward, holding the phone with both hands.

“I'm here.”

“Well, I gathered that.” There it was: the dry little edge, familiar enough to hurt. “Is something wrong?”

Snow at the windows. Broth cooling on a stove. Her mother's hand becoming something she could not warm.

“No,” she said, and a sound escaped her that was almost a laugh. “I just wanted to hear you.”

The silence that followed was not welcoming or hostile. It was cautious.

“That's unusual.”

Mara looked at the ring on her finger. “Can I see you?”

“Today?”

“If you're free.”

“I thought you had the museum.”

“It can wait.”

Another pause. Somewhere at Alma's end a spoon touched china.

“Lunch, then. The usual place. Half past twelve?”

Mara did not know the place. She did not know how old her mother's face would be, or what it would do when it saw her.

“Yes,” she said. “I'll meet you for lunch.”

## Chapter 4: Strangers

She found the restaurant by searching her messages for half past twelve. There were six invitations from Alma, spread across a year. Mara’s replies were brief. Twice she had canceled.

At twelve twenty, she stood outside a narrow restaurant with blue glass lamps, watching a waitress straighten the cutlery. She had bought flowers on the way. Now they seemed absurd: flowers for a woman she had buried.

Alma was already inside.

For a moment Mara could see only the familiar things. The slope of her shoulders. The silver clasp holding back her hair. Then her mother turned, and the left corner of her mouth failed to follow the right.

Mara crossed the room too quickly. She bent to embrace her, pressing her face against the thin, warm neck.

“Mara. The vase.”

Her elbow had struck the table. Water trembled at the rim.

“Sorry.” She stepped back, still holding the flowers.

Alma looked older than she should have, which was ridiculous; she was precisely as old as she should have been. A walking stick hung from the chair. Her left hand lay curled beside her plate.

“Are you all right?” Mara asked.

“About as well as I was on Sunday.”

They sat. Alma ordered soup. Mara ordered the same because she could not read the menu.

“Your hand,” she said.

Her mother looked down. “Still attached.”

“I meant—does it hurt?”

“Only when the therapist insists it can do things it can’t.”

Mara folded the bouquet’s paper into smaller and smaller pleats.

“The stroke,” she said carefully.

“Yes, Mara. The stroke. We haven’t found another name for it in eight months.”

Eight months. In the life Mara remembered, her mother had been dead for more than ten years. No therapy appointments, no walking stick. No extra years in which to buy coats or complain about the price of oranges.

“I should have called more.”

Alma smoothed her napkin with her good hand.

“You sent someone.”

“A nurse?”

“Several. The agency changes them. All very competent.” She paused. “You have always made certain I was competently looked after.”

The waitress brought bread. Mara reached to butter a piece for her mother, then stopped at Alma’s expression.

“I can manage.”

“I know.”

She didn’t know anything.

“What happened after I went to Vienna?”

Alma’s eyes sharpened. “What sort of question is that?”

“I’ve been thinking about that time.”

“Well. I had the surgery. Then the other treatment. The nurses came. Your grandmother came when she could.”

“And you got better.”

“Eventually.”

In Mara’s memory there had been no eventually. There had been a doctor lowering his voice outside a room, and a paper cup collapsing in her hand.

“I’m glad,” she whispered.

“Are you?”

“Mum.”

Alma watched the waitress set down their bowls before she answered.

“I’m not ungrateful. You paid for excellent care. But you do sometimes speak as though sending money was the same as being there.”

Mara’s spoon struck the saucer.

“You left me when I needed you,” Alma said quietly. “You chose your career over your mother.”

There was no anger in her volume. The couple at the next table kept talking.

I stayed, Mara thought. I washed your hair in a basin. I learned which pillow you wanted without asking.

She could not offer those things to this woman. Here they would be lies.

“I’m sorry.”

Alma lowered her eyes to the soup. “Yes. Well. Eat before it gets cold.”

Neither finished. When they parted, Alma allowed a kiss on her cheek but declined a ride. A car had already been arranged. Mara stood with the unwanted flowers while a driver helped her mother into the back seat.

At home, Adrian took the bouquet from her.

“How was lunch?”

“Not good.”

He found a vase without asking where one was kept. “You know how she gets.”

Mara looked at him.

“I don’t think I do.”

He placed a hand at the back of her neck, his thumb making one practiced stroke beneath her hair. It was the gesture of a husband; she waited for the comfort that ought to follow.

“You’re exhausted. After your grandmother, and now this.”

She wanted him to ask what this meant. Instead he reminded her that a car was coming for the museum meeting at three.

“Shall I tell them you’re ill?”

“No,” she said. She needed to see what else she had become.

At the Ashworth, the guard greeted her by name. Her office overlooked a courtyard where rainwater darkened the stone. Someone had left proofs on her desk, marked with questions only she was expected to answer.

During the meeting, a registrar described a disputed loan. Mara listened, frightened of being exposed, until a photograph of the object appeared on the screen.

“The backing isn’t original,” she said.

Everyone turned.

She pointed out the nail pattern, the later mount, the place where a dealer’s label had been trimmed. This was work she understood. Years handling provincial account books and damaged photographs had taught her to notice what people tried to hide.

The registrar began taking notes.

“Exactly,” someone said. “That’s what we needed.”

Mara looked down at the expensive pen in her hand. The knowledge was hers. The office, the listening faces, the salary that had paid her mother’s nurses—those belonged to a woman whose passwords she happened to possess.

Afterward she searched her computer for June.

A photograph appeared first: two young women on a harbor wall, knees touching, June laughing into the wind. Mara remembered taking turns with the camera. She had forgotten how easily they once leaned toward each other.

There were few recent messages. One mentioned a divorce, almost as an afterthought. Another said, The kids are making masks this week. You’d be amazed what twelve-year-olds think they need to hide.

A school website supplied the rest: June taught art at the middle school in their old town. The staff photograph showed a tired woman in a cardigan, her smile stopping short of her eyes.

Mara found an address in an older message and wrote it on the back of a museum envelope.

By five she was driving north. She let Adrian’s call go unanswered until she could pull over and tell him she would be late. Then she drove on, toward the town she remembered never leaving.

## Chapter 5: The Mirror in June

The hardware store occupied the ground floor of a brick building that had once been a bank. Its windows were crowded with rope, padlocks, seed packets, and a handwritten sign: KEYS MADE WHILE YOU WAIT. Above it, a narrow staircase rose between walls painted the color of old oatmeal.

Mara climbed with one hand on the rail. The smell changed from rubber and machine oil to dust, boiled coffee, and something medicinal. At the top, June’s name appeared on a strip of masking tape, the J written in a teacher’s careful hand.

Mara knocked.

Nothing.

She knocked again. A chair scraped inside.

“Who is it?”

“It’s Mara.”

The silence after her name was so complete that Mara wondered if June had gone away. Then the lock turned.

June opened the door wearing gray sweatpants and a cardigan buttoned wrong. Her once-black curls had been cut short; silver showed at the temples. She stared without surprise, as if Mara were a debt collector.

“You found me.”

“I’m sorry. I should have called.”

“You don’t have my number.”

“I found an address.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

June stepped aside. The apartment was one room divided by furniture: sofa facing television, bed behind a bookcase, kitchenette beneath a buzzing fluorescent tube. Student drawings leaned against the wall. A glass horse, painted blue, stood on the windowsill.

Mara stopped breathing.

June followed her gaze. “It’s not the one.”

“No.”

“You remember that?”

“I remember.”

June’s mouth tightened. “Come in. You’re letting the heat out.”

The heat was excessive and dry. June poured wine from a bottle with a screw cap. It was warm and tasted of sour cherries and metal.

They discussed safe things: the drive, school, weather. June taught seventh and eighth grade. Her children were loud, the administration wanted measurable joy, and her ex-husband had moved to Arizona with a dog.

“You have children?” Mara asked.

June gave a short laugh. “No. That was one of our disagreements.”

“Our?”

“Mine and his. He wanted a family. I wanted not to become my mother. We both got what we wanted, eventually.”

Mara looked at the drawings. A red bird had six wings; a house floated above a dark blue sea.

“You look tired,” she said.

“I teach teenagers.”

“I mean—”

“I know.” June set down her cup. “You look strange.”

Mara touched the wedding ring she had stopped noticing. “I’ve had a difficult week.”

“Your grandmother died.”

“Yes.”

“And you came here. Why?”

Because I woke in a life that has your fingerprints on it. Because my mother is alive and hates me. Because my husband touches me like he learned the gesture from someone else. Because a book with my name on it has made a wound in the world.

“I wanted to see you,” Mara said.

June studied her. “You always did hate explaining yourself.”

The words struck with the intimacy of an old bruise. In the life Mara remembered, June had known every version of her silence. They had shared apartments, train compartments, beds after funerals. June had once said Mara could turn a locked door into a personality.

“Did I leave badly?” Mara asked.

June’s fingers tightened around the cup. “You left.”

“For Vienna.”

“Yes.”

The room narrowed around the name.

“Tell me about the fellowship.”

“You don’t remember?”

“I want to hear you say it.”

June rose and opened a cupboard without taking anything out. “The letter came in March. A year in Vienna. Restoration, research, collections nobody here could touch. It was everything I wanted.”

Mara’s pulse beat in her throat.

“You were offered it,” she said.

“I was offered it first. You were next on the list.”

“Why did you refuse?”

“My father was dying.”

She took a clean cup from the cupboard and set it beside the sink.

“He had lung cancer. We knew there wasn’t much time, but knowing doesn’t make time useful. He needed rides, medication, someone to argue with the insurance company. My mother was gone. My brother was in Texas and had reasons.”

“You stayed.”

“I refused the fellowship. Then they called you.”

Mara saw it: not the oncology ward of her remembered life, but a letter on June’s table; June’s hand hovering above the telephone; Mara receiving the call because June had stepped away from it.

“Did I know?”

“You knew enough. You came over with a cake. You said I was brave.”

Mara flinched.

“I should have gone,” June said. “I should have put him in a facility, or let my brother deal with it, or let him be angry. He died anyway. Three months after the program started.”

She looked at Mara. Her tiredness had become something harder, a life polished by repeated handling.

“I should have gone,” she repeated. “And you went, and you never looked back.”

Mara could not speak. The sentence entered her with the precise familiarity of an old wound. In another life she had said it to herself, carrying the accusation through every winter after Alma’s death.

The regret had not vanished. The eraser had found the nearest person carrying the same shape of it and moved the weight.

June mistook her silence for indifference. “You got everything, didn’t you? Vienna, the museum, the husband. I saw your name in an article. You looked very confident.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Don’t.”

“I am.”

“That’s what I can’t stand. You arrive with sorrow after the fact. It’s elegant. It doesn’t require you to give anything up.”

Mara stared at the wine. “I don’t deserve the life I have.”

June’s expression did not soften. “No. You don’t.”

Outside, a delivery truck groaned awake below them, and the apartment trembled.

Mara stood. June did not stop her. At the door, Mara turned back.

“I will come again.”

June looked toward the blue glass horse. “That’s what people say when they want to leave feeling better.”

Mara had no answer. She went downstairs past the smells of rope and rubber, into the cold. The truth followed her, no longer an idea but a weight with June’s face.

The eraser had not removed her regret. It had transferred it. And if it could be transferred, perhaps it could be taken back.

Mara walked to the car, her guilt hardening into resolve.

## Chapter 6: The Hollow Crown

For a month, Mara learned how easily resolve could be postponed by people who needed an answer before noon.

There were loan agreements, proofs, a damaged shipment from Antwerp. At the Ashworth, objects still yielded their histories to her; she could recognize a substituted hinge without remembering the name of the woman who brought her coffee. Work offered the relief of being useful without being understood.

She called June twice. The first time, June let the telephone ring. The second, she said she had marking to do.

Mara meant to drive north that weekend, to see June and search her grandmother’s house. Then an insurer demanded a revised condition report. The following Saturday she spent three hours with Alma, listening while her mother described the therapist’s new exercises. Mara cut an apple into pieces small enough to manage one-handed. When she left, Alma thanked her as she might have thanked a visiting nurse.

The key to the Maine house lay in an envelope from the estate lawyer. Mara moved it from her handbag to her desk and back again. She told herself she needed an uninterrupted day.

She had lived long enough among documents to recognize an excuse in any handwriting.

Then the nomination arrived.

The Vale Prize for Curatorial Achievement: an international shortlist of five, a sum of money greater than her old annual salary. The director came into her office carrying champagne in paper cups.

“You look astonished,” he said. “The rest of us aren’t.”

Her nominated exhibition concerned objects displaced by war and the families who had tried to recover them. She read the citation twice. Beneath the polished institutional language she recognized painstaking work, judgments she might herself have made. That was the trouble. Nothing was wholly counterfeit.

The museum arranged a gala.

At home, Adrian stood behind her while she opened the invitation proof. His reflection occupied the window beside hers, composed and handsome above the city lights.

“Congratulations,” he said, kissing her hair. “I knew you’d get there.”

“To a shortlist?”

“To where they couldn’t overlook you.”

She turned. “If I stopped doing this, what would happen?”

“Doing what?”

“All of it. The museum. Being someone people invite.”

He waited, then straightened the proof on the table.

“You’re tired. Don’t turn a good thing into a crisis.”

She wanted him to ask what she would do instead, or whether she was unhappy. He began discussing the seating plan. Watching him, she understood that he had married someone whose ambitions made their shared life legible. He knew where to stand beside that woman. Without her, he seemed unable to find a place for his hands.

She wrote June a note and enclosed an invitation. Not because she expected forgiveness. Because leaving her out felt like another concealment.

*I would like you there. I know that is a great deal to ask.*

June did not reply.

On the evening of the gala, the museum’s marble hall smelled of lilies and hot electrical fittings. Light shone through ranks of wineglasses. Mara wore a black dress she had found in her wardrobe, its lining cool against her knees. Adrian touched her elbow whenever a photographer approached.

The director introduced her as a woman who understood that the past was never finished with us.

Applause rose. Mara unfolded her speech.

“Ambition is usually described as a solitary quality,” she began. “A matter of individual courage. But nobody goes anywhere alone.”

At the front table, Adrian smiled.

“Every achievement rests on decisions made by other people. Some of those decisions are called sacrifices, especially by the people who benefit from them.”

She had departed from the approved text. The director lowered his glass.

Near the rear doors, between a waiter and a stone column, stood June.

She wore a dark green dress beneath her winter coat. Her hair was flattened on one side, as though she had leaned against the car window before coming in. She was not holding a glass.

Mara looked down. The remaining paragraphs concerned stewardship, access, gratitude.

“I hope,” she said, “that we can learn to look beyond the names on the labels.”

She thanked the museum. People applauded, uncertain for a moment, then warmly.

June waited until Mara reached the edge of the hall.

“Was that for me?”

“June. I wasn’t sure you’d come.”

“Neither was I.” Her gaze moved over Mara’s dress, the flowers, the little knot of people waiting to congratulate her. “I sat in the parking garage for twenty minutes.”

“Can we go somewhere?”

“So nobody hears?”

A server passed between them with a tray of empty glasses. Mara stepped closer.

“I wanted you here.”

“What did you think would happen? I’d see how beautiful it all was and be happy for you?”

“No.”

“Then why invite me?”

Mara had no honest answer that June could understand.

June’s voice grew quieter.

“You have my life. You took the thing I wanted most, and you don’t even deserve it.”

Mara opened her mouth. To confess would sound like madness; to deny it would be worse. She stood silent while June buttoned her coat with shaking fingers.

“Keep the invitation,” June said, pressing the folded card into her hand.

Mara watched her leave. Then she went to the cloakroom, collected her own coat, and sent Adrian a message: *I need to go to the house. I’m sorry.*

She drove north with the folded card on the passenger seat.

By the time she reached the Maine house, the road glittered with frost. The key resisted, then turned. Inside, the air smelled of cold plaster and the lavender her grandmother had kept in drawers.

She climbed without taking off her coat.

The attic bulb flickered alive. Beneath the eaves stood the desk, and on it, exactly where she remembered leaving them, the ledger and the brass eraser.

For a moment she could only grip the chair.

Then she opened the book.

Her childhood lie remained. Her father’s last words remained. Where Vienna should have been, there was a pale, abraded space.

She laid her hand across it. Nothing happened.

She had spent a month hoping to discover something she could do that would not cost her this life. Looking at the blankness, she knew what belonged there. Her own words. Her own burden.

Downstairs, her phone began to ring.

Mara did not move. She thought of the exhibition, of Alma lifting a piece of apple to her mouth, of all the things she might lose by filling a page.

## Chapter 7: The Ledger’s Echo

The attic had no sense of the hour. Its small window held only a square of black glass, and the house below had gone quiet except for the intermittent ringing of Mara’s phone. Each time it began, the sound rose through the ceiling like an accusation. She let it stop.

The ledger lay open on the desk beneath the bare bulb. The page facing her was the page that had once contained the sentence around which she had built eleven years of life. Now it showed only a pale, abraded rectangle, the fibers lifted and rough beneath her fingertip.

A missing tooth, she thought. Not a wound exactly. The tongue kept finding it anyway.

She read the entries before and after it. The blue glass horse. Her father’s last words. The pantry in 1998, the secret that had made her stomach turn when she first saw it in the book. They remained in the same dark handwriting, each regret precise enough to be handled. Her life had not been emptied when the Vienna sentence vanished. It had been rearranged around the absence.

On the desk, the brass eraser waited.

Mara picked it up. It was colder than the room. The inscription, which had once read ONE REMOVAL. ONE TRANSFER. NO RETURN., had changed. The first two phrases were gone, leaving three words cut into the metal.

NO RETURN.

“Fine,” she said. Her voice sounded borrowed.

She turned the eraser over and pressed its gray end to the blank scar. Nothing happened. She rubbed harder. A thread of brass dust fell onto the page, but the scar did not lighten or deepen. She tried the corner, then the flat side, then held the thing between both hands as though warmth might wake it.

Nothing.

A laugh escaped her, small and breathless. Of course. The object had never promised forgiveness. It had offered one removal, and she had spent it. Whatever had been transferred—to June, to Alma, to the whole architecture of the life she had stolen—was not waiting here to be rubbed away.

Downstairs, the phone stopped ringing. A moment later it began again.

Mara set the eraser beside the book and opened the desk drawers. The top one held old receipts, a snapped tape measure, and three envelopes addressed in her grandmother’s narrow hand. The second stuck halfway. She tugged until it gave with a wooden groan.

Inside lay a fountain pen in a nest of yellowed cotton.

She knew it. Her grandmother had used it to sign birthday cards and mortgage papers, dipping the nib into a bottle kept beside the kitchen calendar. The black barrel was warm with age, its gold clip dulled where fingers had touched it. Beneath the pen was a small glass inkwell, stoppered with wax. Mara pulled the stopper free.

The ink inside was blacker than black, with a blue sheen at its surface. It smelled faintly of iron and rain.

She carried the ledger closer to the lamp. The leather cover creaked. Along the inside edge, where the lining had loosened from the boards, a line of writing emerged from the shadow. She had examined this book with a flashlight, with tears blurring her sight, with the panic of a woman who believed a blank page could save her. She had not seen it.

The words were smaller than the entries, but the hand was unmistakably hers.

WHAT IS WRITTEN MAY BE REWRITTEN ONLY BY THE HAND THAT FIRST CARRIED IT.

Mara read it twice. Then a third time.

The sentence did not say restored. It said rewritten. There was a difference large enough to live inside.

She could put the regret back. She could make the page carry its weight again. But the old sentence, if copied as an accusation, would be another kind of evasion. I declined. She died. I wondered. In that order, the words made her choice sound like a crime whose punishment had been eleven years long.

Yet she had not stayed because she was noble. She had stayed because Alma was frightened, because the hospital corridor smelled of bleach and burnt coffee, because her mother’s hand had closed around hers and Mara had understood that leaving would be possible but unbearable. Love had not guaranteed a cure. It had not made the sacrifice wise. It had only made it hers.

The phone rang again below. Mara imagined Adrian calling, or June, or someone from the museum asking where she had gone. In the altered life, people were waiting for her to return to the person they knew. In the old life, perhaps no one was waiting at all. The thought hurt less than it should have.

She uncorked the inkwell and dipped the pen. The nib accepted the ink without dripping. Her hand trembled as she set it over the blank page.

For a long time she did not write.

She remembered the oncology ward: the blue curtains, the paper cup of water, Alma trying to make a joke about hospital pudding. She remembered the fellowship letter folded in her coat pocket. She remembered stepping into the stairwell to call Vienna, and how the committee chair had offered a deferral in a voice full of professional kindness. She remembered saying no. Not because there had been no other choice, but because she had chosen one.

That was the part she had never forgiven herself for: not the staying, but the certainty that a loving choice had to be rewarded. When Alma died, Mara had treated grief as evidence that she had chosen incorrectly.

She lowered the nib.

“I was there,” she whispered. “That was what I chose.”

Then she wrote.

I declined the Vienna fellowship to stay and care for my mother. She died anyway, and I have never stopped wondering who I might have been if I had gone.

The words appeared exactly as they had before, every letter forming beneath her hand with a terrible, familiar ease. When she reached the final period, the ink shone wetly in the lamplight.

The ledger grew heavy.

Not metaphorically. The desk complained beneath it, and the floorboards gave a low, deep shudder. The phone downstairs began ringing again, but its sound stretched thin, as if heard from the far end of a tunnel. The black letters blurred. The attic tilted toward the page.

Mara gripped the pen. She had thought accepting the regret would mean becoming smaller beneath it. Instead, as the room filled with a white and soundless brightness, she felt the sentence take its place inside her—not as a verdict, but as a weight she had lifted with both hands and agreed to carry.

The last thing she saw was the ink sinking into the paper, no longer wet, no longer new.

Then the attic disappeared.

## Chapter 8: Rewriting

The white was not empty.

It carried sound first: a burst of applause, bright and crowded, then the chime of glasses and the murmur of people saying her name. Mara stood beneath the Ashworth Museum’s limestone archway in the dress she had worn to the gala. Her hand was still closed around the fountain pen, though the pen had become a pale line of light between her fingers.

A banner hung above the entrance. VALE PRIZE NOMINEE: MARA ELLISON.

She knew this moment. She had lived it an hour ago, or a month ago, or eleven years in another direction. Adrian stood beside her in his black dinner jacket, one hand resting lightly at the small of her back. He was smiling for the photographers.

“Mara,” he murmured. “They’re waiting for you.”

The applause swelled. Her name moved through the room like a wave.

Then the limestone softened.

The museum’s tall windows ran with color, red and gold dissolving into water. Adrian’s hand slipped from her back. His face remained composed, almost handsome, but the edges of him blurred as if someone had breathed across a mirror.

“Wait,” Mara said.

She reached for him. Her fingers passed through his sleeve and closed on cold air.

The gallery vanished.

She was in the penthouse, standing before its wall of windows. The city glittered below, thousands of lives arranged in avenues and squares. On the dining table lay her museum schedule, invitations, and a note from Adrian reminding her about dinner with trustees. Beyond the glass, snow moved sideways through the dark.

This had been her view. She had thought it proof that she had become someone.

The room began to mist from the corners inward. The lamps dimmed. Her books, shoes, and the photograph of herself receiving a fellowship in Vienna loosened into drifting particles. She tried to seize the photograph. It broke apart against her palm, leaving only dust and rain.

“No,” she said, though the word held no command.

A different room surfaced beneath it: the museum office, shelves crowded with catalogues and conservation reports. Her name was printed on the door. A young assistant stood before her with a clipboard.

“Your interview starts in ten minutes.”

Mara recognized the assistant’s nervous admiration, the pressure of being watched and found remarkable. She recognized, too, the fatigue beneath her triumph. Every achievement had been a room she entered carrying the shape of someone absent.

The assistant’s face dissolved. The office followed.

Applause became wind.

In the wind she saw June.

June stood at the back of the gala in her green dress, one hand clenched around the folded invitation. Her eyes were red, and the bitterness in her face had seemed to Mara like a permanent mark.

“You have my life,” June had said.

Now the sentence lifted from her like smoke.

Not the memory. Not the sorrow. Only the cruel possession inside it.

June’s mouth softened. The line between her brows eased. She looked younger—not young, exactly, because grief had still happened, and her father had still died, and years had still passed—but no longer trapped inside the moment when Mara had become the answer to everything she had lost.

Mara stepped toward her.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

June looked up. For the first time, there was no accusation in her face. There was no forgiveness either. Only recognition, and then a widening distance.

The green dress thinned into light. The winter coat, folded card, and June’s hand became threads and floated away.

Mara tried to follow. She wanted to tell June she had not understood, that she had mistaken regret for a debt another person could pay. The fellowship had never belonged to either of them. It had been a door, and they had each stood before it with someone they loved on the other side.

But there was no longer any place to say it.

The white closed over June’s face.

For one breath Mara felt herself falling through every version of her life: the penthouse, the museum, the hospital corridor, the apartment above the hardware store, the gala with its polished floors and merciless light. She heard Alma laughing at something that had not been funny. She heard the click of June’s blue glass horse being set carefully on a shelf. She heard her father say her name from the other room.

Then the falling stopped.

Mara came back to the attic on her knees.

The bare bulb glowed overhead. The small window showed blackness. Dust rested on the desk and rafters. Somewhere downstairs, a phone rang once and stopped.

She was no longer wearing the gala dress.

An old wool coat lay around her shoulders, brown and frayed at one cuff. Beneath it she wore the gray sweater she had put on after the funeral, its sleeve marked with candle wax. Her shoes were scuffed. Her body felt heavier, older, known.

For several seconds she could not breathe.

The ledger lay open on the desk.

The Vienna page was no longer blank. The writing filled it in the same dark hand, every letter settled into the paper:

I declined the Vienna fellowship to stay and care for my mother. She died anyway, and I have never stopped wondering who I might have been if I had gone.

Mara touched the final period.

It did not lift. It did not shine. It was simply ink.

Beside the ledger, the fountain pen rested uncapped, its nib dry. The eraser was gone. In its place on the wood lay a faint brass-colored stain, no larger than a coin. Mara rubbed it with her thumb. The mark did not come away. It warmed beneath her skin and seemed to vanish, though she could still feel its shape.

Downstairs, the phone began ringing again.

This time she stood.

Her knees trembled, but the floor held. The attic smelled of dust, cedar, and salt entering through old windows. Nothing applauded. No one waited to congratulate her. There was no museum, no husband asking what had happened, no city glittering below a wall of glass.

There was only the house, carrying its silence around her.

Mara looked once more at the restored page. She had returned to the life she had spent eleven years calling a failure. The knowledge pressed against her ribs. It was not relief. It was not happiness. It was weight.

She placed her palm over the sentence.

“All right,” she whispered.

The phone rang below, ordinary and insistent. Mara descended toward it, carrying the ledger’s weight—not because it had become lighter, but because, at last, she had stopped mistaking weight for punishment.

## Chapter 9: The Return

The caller was June.

“Please tell me you’re not still sorting things.”

Mara gripped the receiver. There was a rattle at the other end, then June swearing at something small and domestic.

“What happened?” Mara asked.

“Dropped a lid. Are you crying?”

“I don’t know.”

“Oh, love.” June’s voice changed. “Come over. There’s soup. There may even be bread if I haven’t burned it.”

Mara shut her eyes. “I’m coming.”

Before leaving, she went upstairs and capped the fountain pen. She left it beside the ledger, safely away from the edge of the desk.

Outside, the wind smelled of tidal mud. Her car stood where she had parked it after the funeral, with a folded road map wedged beside the seat. She drove toward town past the dark cranberry bog and the gas station whose illuminated sign had lost its first letter. She knew every bend. At the turn by the church she put on her indicator without thinking.

She had spent a month longing for this road. Here, she had never been away.

June opened her door wearing striped socks and an apron printed with lemons. Behind her, a radio competed with the kitchen extractor fan. There were books piled on the stairs, a wet umbrella spread in the hall.

Mara hugged her before she could speak.

“Well,” June said into her shoulder. Then her arms tightened.

Mara held on until June drew back to look at her.

“Bad evening?”

“Yes.”

“Soup first, then.”

At the table June talked about the funeral flowers, about a neighbor who had borrowed a ladder and returned a different one. Her face moved easily between irritation and amusement. Mara watched her butter bread. This June had sorrows, too; being restored had not exempted her from anything. But she laughed with her whole mouth, and when Mara reached for the salt, June passed it without looking.

“Have I been a good friend?” Mara asked.

June paused. “Generally. You get very quiet when you ought to ask for things.”

“I’d like to stop doing that.”

“Good. You can help me carry those boxes upstairs next week.”

Mara laughed, startling herself. June smiled, satisfied, and ladled more soup into her bowl.

She did not know. There was no reason she should.

Mara almost told her about the room above the hardware store. Instead she said, “Thank you for calling.”

“I was going to call again if you didn’t answer.”

“I know.”

She stayed until the dishes were dry.

***

The next morning she went to the cemetery.

Alma’s stone had a strip of lichen along its lower edge. Mara brushed away a fallen twig and sat on the cold ground, her coat spread awkwardly beneath her. Beyond the wall a truck changed gears on the hill.

“I wished I’d gone,” she said.

She waited, embarrassed by her own voice in the open air.

“Not once. Not only when things were bad. Sometimes when I was buying milk, or filing something at work. I’d think there was another woman somewhere having my life.”

The carved dates remained before her. She had once hated the narrow space between them, all a life reduced to a little measurement she could cover with her hand.

“I was angry with you for needing me. You probably knew.”

There was no use pretending that love had made her patient every day. She remembered snapping over a spilled glass, then kneeling with a towel while Alma apologized from the bed.

“But I’m glad I stayed.” Her throat tightened. “Not glad you were ill. Not glad I lost things. I just don’t want to call being with you a failure anymore.”

She remembered warming Alma’s socks on the radiator. The ridiculous television quiz they had watched. Her mother’s hand opening on the blanket, waiting for hers.

“It was a gift,” she said. “Being there. Even when I didn’t know what to do.”

She sat until the damp came through her coat. When she rose, she left the twig off the grave, but did not pull away the lichen.

***

On Monday, at the historical society, Mara found the same cart of uncatalogued donations beside her desk. Her computer took six minutes to start. Nothing about this had become charming.

While she waited, she wrote a proposal on a sheet of scrap paper: an oral-history archive of the town’s elders. Not just dates and occupations. The choices people had made. Who they had loved; what they still wished they could change.

“We have a recorder somewhere,” the director said when Mara brought it in.

“I’ll find it.”

“And you’d do the interviews?”

“Yes. With permission to keep the awkward parts.”

The director looked over her glasses. “Permission from them, certainly.”

“Certainly,” Mara said, and smiled at the correction.

That afternoon she tested the recorder in the reading room. She spoke her name and the date, then listened to the thin, familiar voice coming back through the speaker.

Upstairs in her grandmother’s house, the ledger still held every sentence. She had changed none of them. Mara saved the test recording, took out a clean notebook, and began making room for other voices.

## Chapter 10: The Thread

By November, the oral-history project had acquired a filing system, three boxes of donated cassettes, and a waiting list.

Mara kept the list in a blue folder beside her desk. Mrs. Tully, who had once run the bakery, wanted to be interviewed after Thanksgiving. The brothers in the white house on Alder Street had agreed to speak together, provided nobody asked about the summer of 1974. Mr. Bell, ninety-two and suspicious of recording devices, had required a demonstration involving his cat.

Mara went to their kitchens and parlors with the recorder in her bag. She learned how people looked away when they reached the edge of a story. Regret was rarely a single stone. It was a room one entered by degrees: the unopened letter, the train not taken, the child one had been too proud to call. Sometimes the speaker laughed after saying the thing that had hurt most. Sometimes they stopped and asked whether the machine was still running.

“Only if you want it to,” Mara always said.

She meant it now.

Her life had not become easier in the months since the attic. Easier was the wrong measure. She still woke before dawn with her heart racing, expecting the penthouse windows, Adrian’s sleeping back, the dense calendar glowing on her phone. She sometimes remembered the altered world so vividly that she reached for a name belonging nowhere in this one. There were mornings when she missed the museum—not its marble floors or applause, but the certainty of knowing what she was for.

Then she would arrive at the historical society and find a note from one of her interviewees, or hear an old man describe the woman he had married at nineteen, and the ache would settle into its proper place.

She did not tell June about the other life. She told her almost everything else.

On a cold Saturday in December, June came to the house carrying oranges and a bottle of wine.

“I brought provisions,” she announced. “Your kitchen looks like a museum exhibit called Woman Who Has Forgotten Lunch.”

“I had lunch.”

“Coffee is not lunch.”

“It had milk in it.”

June put the bag on the counter. “You’re impossible to rescue.”

They cooked together, knocking elbows over a cutting board. June told a story about one of her students painting the principal as a dragon. Mara laughed while slicing onions and had to put down the knife.

Afterward, June carried two plates into the dining room. A crooked wreath hung on the front door. The house no longer seemed to be holding its breath. It creaked, settled, admitted the wind.

“Tell me something from the interviews,” June said.

Mara poured the wine. “A woman named Elsie chose not to leave town when she was twenty-two. She’d been offered a job in Boston. She stayed because her younger sister was pregnant and frightened.”

“What happened?”

“She stayed six years, then went to Boston anyway and became a bookkeeper. She regrets losing the six years, but says her sister would have broken without her.”

“Sounds familiar.”

Mara looked at her.

June shrugged. “Everybody has a version of that story.”

“Yes,” Mara said. “They do.”

They ate. The conversation wandered through weather, the historical society’s leaking gutter, and June’s plan to repaint her spare room. When they had finished, June studied Mara across the table.

“You’re different,” she said.

Mara felt the old instinct to deny it. “How?”

“Less absent.” June considered. “Still strange. But present-strange.”

“I’ll take that.”

“Good.” June reached for bread. “I need help choosing paint next weekend.”

“I’ll come.”

“You promise?”

Mara thought of promises once made to leave a room, and of the one she had kept by returning. “I promise.”

After June left, Mara washed the glasses and turned off the downstairs lights. Then she took the stairs to the attic.

The ledger lay beside the capped fountain pen. Dust had gathered on the desk except in the faint brass-colored stain where the eraser had been. No matter how often Mara looked, she found no mark of the object itself. Only the color remained, a small tarnish like a memory refusing to explain itself.

She carried the ledger to the window and opened it.

The pages held the familiar entries: the blue glass horse; her father’s last words, Then fix it yourself; the pantry in the summer of 1998, Alma crying behind the door while Mara waited outside and pretended not to hear. The Vienna sentence remained dark and exact: I declined the Vienna fellowship to stay and care for my mother. She died anyway, and I have never stopped wondering who I might have been if I had gone.

Once, each regret had seemed to demand an answer. If only she had confessed sooner. If only she had apologized. If only she had boarded the plane.

Now the entries seemed less like weights than threads. They ran backward through the years, tugging her toward June’s blue horse and Alma’s open hand, toward the father she had failed to call, toward the frightened girl who had believed needing someone was defeat. The threads did not absolve her. They connected her.

Mara touched the Vienna page. She remembered Adrian, the museum lights, June’s bitterness lifting from her face, and let the memory remain what it was: not a destination or warning, but a life she had briefly carried and surrendered.

She did not look for an eraser. There was none.

She closed the book and returned it to the shelf beneath the window, between old photographs and a moth-eaten atlas. Before leaving, she rested her palm on the leather cover.

“Stay,” she said, though she did not know whether she meant the ledger, the house, or herself.

Downstairs, she put on her coat and found June’s scarf hanging over the banister, forgotten in her hurry. Mara smiled and went outside to carry it to the car.

In the attic, the ledger remained in the darkened house, its pages quiet, its threads waiting. One day another hand might open it and mistake lightness for freedom. For now, it held what Mara had chosen to keep.

Some weights were not meant to be lifted.
