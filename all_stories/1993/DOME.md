In October 1934, I reached Greywater House with salt dried on my coat and the Northumberland sky darkening behind me. The mansion stood on a tidal island beyond a narrow causeway, its grey walls facing the sea. Edmund Rook had hired me to investigate threatening letters and discrepancies in his collection. His typewritten invitation had called both matters urgent. The weather made the house feel less like a residence than a place already cut off from the world.

Rook received me in the hall. He was lean, silver-haired, and so carefully mannered that his courtesy seemed to leave no room for disagreement. “The collection is exact,” he said, when I asked about the irregularities. “Or it should be.” He spoke quietly, but watched me with the attention of a man accustomed to judging what others had understood.

The ground floor was compact: drawing room, dining room, kitchen, and Rook’s study. Upstairs were the bedrooms; on the landing, the archive held the smell of dry paper and paste. Iris Bell, the archivist and conservator, worked there beneath a shaded lamp. She greeted me briefly, then returned to her folio. Rook asked her for a catalogue reference before we had finished speaking. She supplied it without hesitation. It was clear he relied on her knowledge.

His study had only one door, opening inward. An exposed horizontal sliding bolt was fixed to its inner face. The two windows were narrow and fixed shut, their catches painted over; the fireplace flue was too tight for a person to pass. I could see no other exit. Rook said he bolted the door while working. As we discussed the threatening letters, I noticed fresh varnish around the frame. “You’ve had the woodwork done,” I said. He glanced at it. “The draught was getting in.”

The wind strengthened as we walked back to the hall. Rain swept against the windows, and Rook checked his watch when I asked about the causeway. “It will be under before supper.” His tone suggested a fact no more troubling than the hour. A little later, the telephone rang once, then fell silent. Agnes, who kept the house, told him the line had failed. “It does that in bad weather,” he said. “It does that when the wire is left to rot,” she replied, and went back to the kitchen. The sharpness in her voice had plainly not begun with the storm.

At dinner, the rain drummed hard enough against the panes to interrupt conversation. Helena Rook sat opposite her husband, composed in a dark dress. A travel case stood by the stairs, and a railway timetable lay beside her plate. She asked Agnes when the tide would permit a crossing in the morning. Rook answered for her: “You needn’t concern yourself with that tonight.” Helena lowered her eyes, though her hand stayed motionless beside her spoon.

Daniel, Rook’s son, was less able to disguise his anger. When his father asked whether he had been in the archive that afternoon, Daniel shoved back his chair. “You ask as if you’ve already decided.” Rook said he had decided nothing, only that the missing material had to be accounted for. “You’ve always been better at accounting for people than listening to them,” Daniel said. His father’s expression barely shifted, which seemed to anger Daniel more than a raised voice would have.

Iris remained quiet. Rook turned to her for dates and catalogue numbers; she answered each question precisely, but her attention kept returning to the table. Agnes served Rook last. When he reminded her that the decanters must be polished before breakfast, she said, “If there’s a breakfast.” Helena’s fingers tightened once around her glass. None of them spoke.

Rook set down his knife. “At ten o’clock, the missing material is to be returned. If it is not, I shall name the person responsible and provide the evidence I have gathered. I will not shelter a thief.” The words were calm, but they changed the room. Daniel stared at his father. Helena’s face remained composed. Iris looked up, and I saw alarm before she lowered her eyes again.

Beyond the windows, the causeway disappeared under the tide. The telephone was dead, and no help could reach us before daylight. Rook resumed his meal as if he had merely announced the hour. I listened to the storm press against the house and wondered what evidence he believed he had.

After dinner, Iris unlocked the archive and brought me the accession ledger. Rain rattled against the glass, and the lamp made a steady circle upon the table. Beyond it, the shelves receded into darkness. Three letters by Robert Burns had been replaced with photographic copies. Iris showed me her descriptions of the originals: a watermark, a broken seal, a tear beside a signature.

“I examined them on the third of October,” she said. “They were right then.”

“Who has had access since?”

“Mr Rook has a key. I have the other. But while I’m working, the door stands open.”

Her fingers rested on the ledger’s margin, carefully clear of the paper. Daniel had brought a dealer into the room the following week. Helena sometimes wrote there; Agnes dusted the shelves. Possession of a key told me very little.

I questioned the others separately. Daniel admitted owing money, then demanded whether his father had supplied the figure. Helena said she knew nothing about the letters. When I asked whether she needed money for her departure, her composure faltered.

“I have enough for a railway ticket. Edmund knows that. He knows precisely what I have.”

Agnes stood with her hands beneath her apron while I questioned her.

“I’ve worked here nineteen years,” she said. “If I wanted to rob him, I needn’t have waited until now.”

None of these answers excluded theft. Equally, none explained why someone should risk substituting copies when Iris’s records made discovery almost certain.

At ten we gathered in the drawing room. Rook stood beside the mantelpiece with an envelope in his hand. He waited until Iris had taken the chair nearest the door.

“Daniel took the letters.”

His son rose halfway from the sofa.

“Sit down.” Rook withdrew two sheets. “A Newcastle dealer’s letter, and your signed receipt for fifty pounds advanced against their sale. Mr Harcourt was sufficiently alarmed by my enquiries to send me both.”

I examined them. The dealer acknowledged photographs of three Burns letters and referred to delivery before the end of October. The receipt bore Daniel’s signature.

“I offered them,” Daniel said. His face had lost its colour. “Yes, I took the advance. I intended to persuade you to let me sell them. I never removed anything.”

“You represented them as your property.”

“You said they would be mine.”

“When I died.”

Helena stood. “Edmund, let him explain.”

“He has explained quite enough. The police shall have these tomorrow.”

Agnes made a small movement towards Daniel, then stopped. Iris looked at the receipt in my hand.

I asked Rook to leave the papers with me.

“In a moment,” he said. “There is something else you ought to see.”

He gathered them and returned to his study. I watched him enter from the passage. He closed the door before I could follow.

We dispersed uneasily. I remained in the drawing room, recording the dealer’s name and the wording of the receipt. The accusation established Daniel’s need for money and his willingness to deceive; it did not establish that he had removed the originals. Outside, the wind drove something loose against the guttering with an irregular metallic clatter.

At twenty past ten, Agnes appeared.

“He won’t answer. I’ve brought his tea.”

I followed her to the study. The handle turned, but the door resisted pressure. I knocked and called Rook’s name. There was no reply, nor any movement within.

Daniel came down the passage, with Helena and Iris behind him.

“Stand aside,” he said.

I caught his arm before he threw his shoulder against the door. At my direction he fetched the kitchen poker and broke the narrow panel beneath the handle. Through the opening I could see the exposed bolt, fully seated in its keeper. I reached inside and drew it back.

Rook sat slumped over his desk. Blood had spread across the blotter beneath his head. A bronze bookend lay beside his chair, its base dark and wet. I found no pulse; when I bent close, I could detect no breath.

I told the others to remain by the doorway.

Both windows were still screwed shut. I examined the fireplace; its narrow flue offered no passage for a person. The papers Rook had shown us lay on the desk, partly beneath his sleeve.

Behind me, Helena began to weep quietly. I looked from the wound above Rook’s ear to the door we had opened, remembering the resistance of the bolt beneath my hand.

Dinner proceeded beneath a silence broken chiefly by the clock and the rain against the windows. Rook ate with maddening composure. When the servants withdrew, he laid his napkin beside his plate.

“I have kept certain papers,” he said, “because each of you has given me reason to do so.”

Daniel lowered his knife. Helena’s fingers tightened around her glass.

Rook spoke of unpaid loans, altered signatures, money withdrawn without authority, and letters whose publication would cause considerable distress. He named no names until Daniel challenged him.

“You are making a spectacle of private business.”

“I am offering an opportunity to settle it.” Rook looked directly at him. “Three hundred and eighty pounds, Daniel. By ten o’clock I expect the money, or an arrangement I can accept.”

Daniel’s jaw hardened. He placed his knife precisely beside his plate, as though the small act required his whole attention.

I asked what evidence supported these accusations.

“Original documents,” Rook replied. “Not household gossip.”

“Then I should like to examine them.”

“In a moment. There is something else you ought to see.”

He gathered the papers and went to his study. From the passage I watched him enter and close the door.

The others dispersed. Daniel sought the library; Helena pleaded a headache; Iris remembered correspondence concerning the collection. Agnes remained behind, straightening objects that needed no straightening.

I found Daniel before the library’s empty fireplace.

“Can you meet his demand?”

“Not tonight.”

“Would a creditor wait?”

“An ordinary creditor might.” He looked towards the passage. “Rook knows that if my bank learns about those signatures, every other debt becomes payable. He wants more than repayment.”

His anger had acquired a frightening stillness. Yet anger was not evidence, and I left him without pressing further.

In the drawing room, Helena struggled with her bracelet.

“Have you spoken privately to Rook tonight?” I asked.

“No.”

“Or confronted him recently?”

Her eyes lifted sharply. “We discussed household expenses yesterday. Nothing more.”

She answered too quickly, but that proved only that she disliked the question.

At half past nine Agnes told me she had taken a ledger into the library shortly after nine and found Daniel there. She had returned for it a quarter-hour later; he had still been reading beside the fireplace.

“Was anyone with him?”

“No. I should have noticed.”

Her account supported his whereabouts for two moments, not the interval between them. I recorded that distinction.

Iris was harder to place. She had been comparing a dealer’s receipt with the collection inventory, sometimes in the morning room, sometimes upstairs where the cabinets stood. Her notebook contained figures enough, but nothing that fixed the times of her movements.

Ten struck. Rook did not emerge. Daniel watched the drawing-room door without speaking.

At twenty past ten Agnes appeared with a tray.

“He won’t answer. I’ve brought his tea.”

I followed her to the study, knocked, and called Rook’s name. No sound came from within. The handle turned, but the door held fast.

Daniel approached, Helena and Iris behind him.

“Stand aside.”

I stopped him from charging the door and sent him for the kitchen poker. Under my direction he broke the narrow panel beneath the handle. Through it I saw the interior bolt fully seated in its keeper. I reached inside and drew it back.

Rook lay slumped over his desk. Blood soaked the blotter beneath his head. A bronze bookend lay beside his chair, its base dark and wet. I found neither pulse nor breath.

I kept everyone at the threshold: Daniel on the left, Helena behind him, Iris against the opposite jamb, Agnes still holding the tray.

Both windows were screwed shut. The fireplace and its narrow flue offered no passage; there was no other exit. The bolt was solid, its keeper undamaged. On Rook’s coat near the shoulder, I noticed a small fragment of dark fabric and left it undisturbed.

The doctor arrived after eleven, rain dripping from his bag. Under such conditions, he warned, precision was impossible. Death might have occurred between nine and half past ten.

Helena began to weep. I looked from the wound above Rook’s ear to the door we had opened, remembering the resistance of the bolt beneath my hand.

As ten approached, the house seemed to divide itself into separate routes and silences. The storm worried the windows, driving rain across the glass, while lamplight thinned in the passages. Everyone found a reason to be elsewhere, or to cross the house without meeting anyone. Yet each explanation seemed shaped by Rook’s deadline: at ten, he expected an answer.

Daniel paced between the drawing room and the foot of the stairs, his steps measured and his hands clenched. When I asked about the three hundred and eighty pounds Rook had demanded, he stopped.

“It is a debt,” he said. “Not a confession.”

His voice was controlled, but anger showed in the tightness of his jaw. Rook’s ultimatum left him little time to find the money, and Daniel knew the consequences of failing. His eyes kept returning to the study door. I could not tell whether he feared Rook’s decision or what he might say before making it.

Helena stayed in the drawing room, though twice she rose and went toward the corridor. I asked whether she had recently confronted Rook in private.

“I have not confronted him,” she said. “I did not go to his study.”

She spoke firmly, but her fingers twisted her handkerchief into a narrow cord. Her agitation made the denial difficult to read: perhaps she was angry at being questioned, perhaps afraid of what Rook’s answer at ten would mean.

Agnes carried the evening dishes toward the kitchen and told me she had seen Daniel away from the study during the period in question. Her account placed him in another part of the house, though it did not establish exactly when he had been there. She offered the detail with the caution of someone who knew it might be taken as a defense. Daniel did not confirm or challenge it.

Iris was more difficult to locate. She had been comparing a dealer’s receipt with the collection inventory, sometimes in the morning room, sometimes upstairs by the cabinets. Her notebook held figures but no times. I glimpsed her at the far end of the passage, carrying no papers; when I turned back, she had gone.

The quarter-hour and half-hour chimes seemed to make the house listen. Rook had given several people reason to seek him alone, but as ten drew near, no one volunteered to knock.

Ten struck. Rook did not emerge. Daniel watched the drawing-room door without speaking.

At twenty past ten Agnes appeared with a tray. The teapot trembled against its saucer.

“He won’t answer. I’ve brought his tea.”

I followed her to the study and knocked, then called Rook’s name. There was no reply. The handle turned, but the door held fast.

Daniel came forward, Helena and Iris behind him.

“Stand aside.”

He moved as if to throw his shoulder against the door. I stopped him and sent him for the kitchen poker. With it, he broke the narrow panel beneath the handle. Through the opening I saw the interior bolt seated in its keeper. I reached in and drew it back.

Rook was slumped over his desk, blood soaking the blotter beneath his head. A bronze bookend lay beside his chair, its base dark and wet. The wound above his ear was narrow and deep. He had no pulse and was not breathing.

I kept everyone at the threshold: Daniel to my left, Helena behind him, Iris against the opposite jamb, Agnes still holding the tray. I noted their positions before allowing anyone to move.

Both windows were screwed shut. The fireplace and its narrow flue offered no passage, and there was no other exit. The bolt was solid, its keeper undamaged. On Rook’s coat near the shoulder, I saw a small fragment of dark fabric and left it undisturbed.

The doctor arrived after eleven, rain dripping from his bag. He examined Rook as thoroughly as the circumstances allowed, then warned us that the storm and the limits of his examination made precision impossible. Death might have occurred between nine and half past ten.

Helena began to weep. I looked from the wound to the door, remembering the resistance of the bolt beneath my hand.

- Story:

The storm had cut us off from the village as effectively as a locked door. The telephone line was dead, and the low road beyond the lodge had vanished beneath runoff. No constable could reach us before morning. Until then, the household’s recollections were all the evidence I had, and recollections, I knew, altered shape under fear.

I asked them to give me the hours from dinner onward, separately. The quarrel between Daniel and Rook had been witnessed by everyone: sharp words over money, then Daniel’s abrupt departure from the table. It gave him a reason to hate Rook, and the doctor’s estimate gave him time. Yet no one could place Daniel continuously after the argument. Helena had gone upstairs; Iris said she had spent part of the evening in the music room, though she could not say when she had last seen Daniel. Agnes, at first, put him in the drawing room with her.

Her account had been too neat. She sat opposite me in the morning room, hands folded so tightly that the knuckles showed pale beneath the skin. Rain whispered against the windows.

“I said that because I thought you would suspect him,” she admitted. “After what happened at dinner. I wanted him to have somewhere to be.”

“Was he with you?”

“No.”

She lowered her eyes, then made herself look at me. “I heard the study bolt slide. It was some time after half past nine. I thought Mr. Rook had shut himself in, as he often did. I told myself he was alive because the bolt had moved.”

The precision of the sound, after the uncertainty of her first account, mattered. But it did not mean what she had made it mean. The bolt had slid; that was all. Rook might have moved it before he died. Someone else might have moved it. And with the doctor placing death anywhere between nine and half past ten, the sound could not establish when the blow fell.

I returned to the study. The broken panel gaped beneath the handle, pale splinters showing against the dark wood. The bolt still ran cleanly into its keeper; there was no damage to suggest it had been forced. I examined the desk, the papers scattered near Rook’s hand, and the space around the chair without shifting anything. The bronze bookend lay where I had seen it. The small dark fragment remained caught on his coat. Nothing in the room offered an easy answer to how a murderer could have left it fastened from within.

Daniel asked to speak with me before noon. His defiance had gone out of his face, leaving a strained, watchful look.

“I wasn’t in the drawing room,” he said. “I was upstairs.”

He had been searching, secretly, for a debt acknowledgment bearing his signature. Rook had kept it among papers in an upstairs cabinet, Daniel said, and had threatened to use it. He had gone up during the very interval in which no one could account for him. He had not wanted the household to know he was in debt, nor for me to learn how much leverage Rook had over him.

“You knew what your quarrel would look like,” I said.

“I knew what it would look like if I told the truth.”

His explanation fit Agnes’s lie and filled part of the gap, but it did not account for the whole evening. Nor did it explain why he had searched for the paper then, or what he had found. He insisted the acknowledgment was missing. I had no reason yet to accept that as anything more than another claim made under pressure.

The archive occupied a narrow room beyond the upstairs landing. Its air smelled of dust, damp wool, and the faint, dry sweetness of old paper. Rook had kept his catalogues in careful order, but several folders sat unevenly on their shelves. On the table, a ledger lay open beside a stack of reproductions. I compared the listed leaves with those present. Two numbers did not agree. One reproduction carried a watermark absent from the original paper described in the catalogue; another was too clean at its edges, while the surrounding documents had browned and softened with age.

In Rook’s hand, a note beside the entries read: “Originals replaced. Resolve before Monday.” His ultimatum, then, had not been an empty threat. Someone in the household had access to the archive, and the discrepancies suggested that valuable originals might have been removed and reproductions put in their place.

I set the note back exactly where I had found it. The evidence gave the household a motive to conceal, but it did not name the person who had acted. By then the storm had eased, though the windows still trembled with rain. The locked study, the moving bolt, and the disturbed records remained separate pieces. I had to resist the comfort of fitting them together too soon.

The archive gave me a direction, not a name. I returned to the table after the others had gone quiet and laid the catalogue beside my notes. The storm had thinned to a steady rain, but the house still seemed to hold its breath around the locked study. Without outside help, I had only the household’s own accounts and the hours they left unfilled.

I set down the evening in columns: where each person claimed to have been, who had seen them, and what sound or movement could be fixed to a particular moment. The entries did not make a clean sequence. Rook had been in the study; Daniel had quarrelled with him in public; then Daniel’s whereabouts blurred. Agnes had supplied him with an alibi, and her first account had made it seem that Rook was still alive after Daniel had gone.

I asked her to sit with me at the end of the passage, away from the others. She kept her hands folded in her lap. The small muscles in her jaw moved as if she were working through words before allowing them out.

“You didn’t hear Mr. Rook speak?” I asked.

“No.”

“Then what did you hear?”

“The bolt slide.”

“And after that?”

“I thought he was in there. I thought he was alive.”

She looked down at her hands. Her original story had carried the certainty of someone trying to shield Daniel. This one carried something different: shame at having mistaken her inference for a fact.

The sound could not bear the weight she had put on it. A bolt sliding told me that the bolt had moved. It did not tell me who had moved it, from which side, or whether Rook had been alive when it happened. His death had no exact hour yet; the interval still covered more than one person’s movements. Agnes’s correction sharpened the chronology and weakened the only part of it that had seemed to place Rook beyond danger.

Daniel’s public quarrel remained the plainest motive anyone could see, and his absence gave it teeth. But when I pressed him on the missing stretch of time, he repeated what he had already admitted: he had been searching upstairs for the acknowledgment of his debt. He had hidden the search because the paper would have exposed the debt to the household. His account explained why he evaded questions and why Agnes lied for him. It did not establish when he began or finished searching, what he found, or whether he had entered the study that evening. He insisted the acknowledgment was gone. I had no independent reason to believe him.

I went back to the archive and compared the altered entries with the household’s movements. The room was not sealed off from ordinary life. Anyone called upstairs could reach it; Rook’s catalogues lay in plain view, and the folders were not kept behind a second locked door. Yet the substitutions were not careless. The reproductions matched the descriptions well enough to escape a casual glance, while the watermark and the unnaturally clean edges gave them away under comparison. Whoever had made or placed them needed time, access to the papers, and some knowledge of what the originals looked like.

I asked each person whether they had gone into the archive recently. The answers differed less in their claims than in their confidence. One remembered passing the door but not entering; another could not say when they had last handled a folder. No account placed a particular person at the shelves at the right time. Still, the discrepancies made a distinction I could use: a visitor might have learned what Rook was investigating, but someone familiar with his arrangement could alter the records more discreetly.

His note, “Resolve before Monday,” gave the theft a deadline and a consequence. Rook had discovered enough to threaten someone under his own roof. That made the archive more than a collection of old papers; it was the reason one of the household might fear what he would reveal.

I closed the catalogue and listened to the rain tapping at the windows. Daniel had opportunity, Agnes had concealed it, and someone had tampered with the records. But the bolt’s movement could not settle the time of death, and Daniel’s secret search could not yet be separated from the murder. I had narrowed the question to those who could reach the papers and feared Rook’s ultimatum. The answer was still somewhere inside the house.

The rain had thinned to a silver mist against the windows when I asked Helena to speak with me. She stood by the hearth, where the fire had sunk to a red seam beneath the ash. Her hands were clasped; one thumb pressed hard against the other.

“You asked whether I quarrelled with Mr. Rook,” she said. “I did not.”

The denial was calm, but her eyes moved to the writing desk. Its drawer remained open from my earlier search.

“Where were you between dinner and the discovery of the body?”

“In my room. I have told you.”

“And you had not seen him privately?”

“I had no reason to.”

I let the silence hold. Rainwater ticked from the eaves outside. Helena glanced toward the window, then lowered her hands.

“He demanded three hundred and eighty pounds,” she said. “He claimed I owed it to him. I refused.”

“Was that the quarrel you denied?”

Her face tightened. “Yes. We argued yesterday, before dinner. He wanted the money by Monday.”

“Why lie about it?”

“Because you would think it made me capable of killing him.” Her voice wavered, then steadied. “And because I mean to leave him.”

She crossed to the desk and drew two folded letters from beneath a stack of bills. Both were from a solicitor. One advised her to keep her intentions private until arrangements were complete.

“I hid them from Rook. He searched through everything.”

The letters made her deception intelligible, not harmless. I asked when she had last taken them out.

“After dinner. I came down for them and went back upstairs.”

Mrs. Bell had seen Helena near the lower corridor around half past eight. Helena had carried a small key and asked whether the study was occupied. The housekeeper could confirm the visit, but not where Helena went afterward. It accounted for a gap in Helena’s story without accounting for the whole evening.

She watched me put the letters back. “You see? I was protecting myself.”

“I see that you were hiding something,” I said. “I have not yet decided what it means.”

In the passage, I questioned Agnes, who had carried Rook’s evening tray to the study. At first she said she had only left the coffee. When I asked whether anyone had been with him, she looked toward the stairs.

“Miss Iris was there,” she said. “Beside the desk. She asked me to leave the tray. Mr. Rook was speaking to her.”

“Did you hear what they said?”

“No. I went out. They were still together when I left.”

Iris had told me she had not seen Rook after dinner. Agnes’s account contradicted her, though it could not establish how long Iris stayed or what passed between them.

Iris appeared on the landing a few minutes later, wearing a dark green dress with a narrow collar. I had first seen her in another. The change caught my attention, but clothes were not proof. She might have changed for an ordinary reason, and I had no means of connecting the dress to the dark scrap found near Rook’s chair.

I returned to the archive to compare the household’s accounts with the records. The reference slip I had seen after finding Rook’s body was gone. I checked the catalogue and the nearby folders, then asked who had moved it. No one admitted to touching it. The bare space beside the catalogue gave me no name, only the knowledge that something I had seen was no longer there.

In the study, the air smelled of old paper and a fire gone cold. I examined the doorjamb beneath the lamp. Its varnish was fresher than the surrounding wood, still smooth and dark. Two parallel abrasions crossed it near the bolt’s height. A short thread had caught in one of them.

The bolt might have been drawn from outside with a loop of thread. The marks made that possible, but did not prove it. I lifted the fibre free with a pencil. It was ordinary mending thread, the kind kept in every part of the household.

I set it beside the catalogue. Helena had concealed letters; Iris had concealed a visit; someone had removed the reference slip. Each act could belong to the same crime, or to a separate fear. The abrasions offered a way the study might have been locked from outside, but the thread could have come from anyone. The house had begun to surrender its secrets, yet none of them could safely be joined together.

I sent for Iris before the household had finished breakfast. She came into the morning room in a grey dress, her hands clasped loosely at her waist. The change from the green dress I had seen on the landing the night before was plain. I noted it, and nothing more. A woman might change her clothes for a dozen reasons; a changed dress was not an answer to any question I had asked.

“The servant carrying the tea tray saw you leave the study after dinner,” I said. “She says you were there alone with Rook.”

Iris looked towards the window. Rain silvered the glass, and the bare branches beyond it stirred in the wind. “I told you I hadn’t seen him.”

“You did.”

Her fingers tightened once, then relaxed. The denial had been ready; the witness’s account had made it useless. After a moment she said, “I went to speak to him. I did not stay long.”

“What passed between you?”

“An argument, though not about the archive.” She looked back at me. “He had demanded money. Three hundred and eighty pounds. I refused to give it to him.”

“And the solicitor?”

Her expression changed at that. I had not named the letters I knew she had hidden, but she understood. “I have written to a solicitor,” she said. “I mean to leave him. I did not want Rook to find out until I had made arrangements.”

The private papers she had kept from me, and the times her movements could be placed elsewhere, accounted for much of her evasiveness. They gave her reasons to lie; they did not put her beside Rook when he died.

“You left him alive?” I asked.

“Yes. He was at his desk. There was a sealed packet there, and he was reading a letter. I left both where they were.”

“What was on the packet?”

“A label. A shelf mark.” She recited it without hesitation.

It was the same mark Daniel had copied from the reference slip. I learned the rest from him and Agnes separately. Agnes had concealed that she asked Daniel to help search the archive after Rook’s body was found. During that search, before the slip disappeared, Daniel copied the mark. Agnes had said nothing because she feared the search itself would be treated as a breach of Rook’s orders. The slip was still there then. The packet, by Iris’s account, had already been on Rook’s desk the night before.

I returned to the study with the letter book. Among Rook’s pencilled notes, a draft entry recorded that he had recovered an original under the same shelf mark. In the next line he wrote that he intended to submit it for examination on Monday. The writing was hurried, as if he had meant to complete the entry later. He had not.

The two absences belonged to different intervals. The packet had disappeared between Iris’s visit and my finding Rook’s body. The reference slip had remained in the archive until after the discovery, then vanished before I looked for it again. One person might have taken both, but the timing did not require it. Nor did anyone’s ability to reach the archive prove that they knew what Rook had recovered.

I began again with the household’s routines. Who had entered the study after dinner? Who had carried the trays, crossed the passage, or been sent upstairs? The answers placed people at doors, not at the desk. Then I asked who had entered the archive after I found the body, and when. Several had been there; fewer could say what they had seen. The catalogue’s bare space and the marks left on its disturbed papers suggested someone had not merely moved the slip, but wanted its removal to pass unnoticed.

My next questions were narrower. Had Rook spoken of recovering an original? Had he mentioned the Monday examination? Had he shown anyone the packet, or only its label? Iris had seen it. Daniel had copied the mark from the slip. But the entry in Rook’s hand did not say who else had been told, and the answer mattered more than a key to the archive.

As I spoke to them, I kept seeing the paired scratches in the study’s fresh varnish and the thread caught near the bolt. They offered a possible way to draw the door shut from outside, but thread was kept throughout the house. So, too, there were many hands that might have reached the archive. I had begun with the question of who could take the records. I now needed to know who understood what the records meant.

I stopped asking who possessed a key. Access explained opportunity, not understanding. Whoever had substituted the books had relied upon appearances; I needed to know who could distinguish the replacements, and who knew that Rook had recovered an original for Monday’s examination. Daniel’s copied mark established neither fact by itself.

The packet had disappeared before I found the body. The reference slip had vanished afterwards. I ruled a line between those intervals in my notebook. Whatever connected them, I would not manufacture it by treating two disappearances as one.

Iris stood in the drawing room, listening to the rain worrying the chimney.

“Agnes saw you leaving his study,” I said. “You have heard her account. Do you still maintain that you did not go back?”

“I went to finish our conversation.”

“Then your earlier statement was untrue.”

“You make everything sound deliberate.”

“Was this not deliberate?”

Her eyes moved towards the door.

“He showed you the packet?”

“Only its wrapping.”

“And told you what Monday’s examination might establish?”

“He told me what he hoped it would establish.”

That distinction was carefully chosen. She understood the threat, whether or not she had ever touched a stolen book.

“Were you wearing your earlier dress when you went back?”

“Yes.”

“Why did you change afterwards?”

“It was uncomfortable.”

“Where is it?”

“In my room.”

She answered quickly, then pressed her lips together. I asked Agnes to show me. Leaving Iris unobserved was a mistake; I understood it when we found the chair in her room empty, and heard a door close below.

We went down the service stairs. At the turn came a smell sharper than wood smoke: scorched wool.

Iris was kneeling before the kitchen range. The fire door stood open, and she was pushing the dress into it with a poker. Its skirt had collapsed into glowing folds. As I entered, a flame travelled along a sleeve.

“Stand away.”

She thrust the poker forward instead.

I caught the sleeve with the tongs and dragged it onto the hearth. Iris sprang up and seized my arm. There was scarcely room between the range and the kitchen table; her shoulder drove me against the iron rim, and the kettle lurched on its ring.

For an instant there was only heat, her breath against my cheek, and the fear that another shove would bring the boiling water down upon us.

I braced one foot against the table leg, freed my arm, and caught her wrist as she reached for the cloth again.

“Water, Agnes.”

Agnes emptied a jug over the sleeve. Steam whitened our faces. I held Iris back until the last orange thread went black; then she ceased struggling, suddenly as still as she had been at the window.

The shoulder was ruined, but the cuff had lain outside the fiercest heat. I lifted the surviving length onto a clean plate. From my pocket I took the folded paper containing the fragment I had documented in Rook’s hand during my first examination.

I did not compare colour alone. The ragged edges met, tooth for tooth. Across the join, a torn seam resumed its course; the broken stitching aligned.

Iris watched my hands.

“It proves nothing about the books.”

“I am not asking it to.”

I folded the pieces separately and gave Agnes the plate, asking her to keep it beyond Iris’s reach. The dress had been changed after the visit Iris denied, and its missing cuff had remained in Rook’s grasp. Her attempt to burn it supplied another fact, not an explanation. I would have to distinguish those, too.

Beyond the scullery window the yard was running water; no messenger would cross the flooded road tonight. I could not yet assign every movement in the archive, nor turn scratches beside a bolt into certainty. But the concealed meeting, the change of clothes, and the torn cuff now connected Iris to a struggle independently of the missing books.

She said nothing more. I opened my notebook to set down the whole sequence, beginning with the visit she had said never took place.

I caught the dress with the stove tongs before the fire had taken the whole sleeve. The shoulder came away in a scatter of sparks, but the cuff dragged across the iron lip, dark and wet-looking where the heat had begun to eat it.

Iris seized my wrist.

“Leave it.”

Her voice was low enough that Agnes, standing in the doorway, might almost have mistaken it for an appeal. Then Iris thrust my arm towards the open fire.

The tongs struck the stove. I caught the edge of the table with my free hand, and the kettle rocked above us. Its lid chattered; a narrow stream of boiling water hissed down the iron side. In that cramped space there was nowhere to retreat without surrendering the cloth.

“Stand back,” I told her.

She reached past me.

I turned, holding the tongs away from the flames, and caught her wrist. For a moment her weight drove against mine. Her face was close enough for me to see the small pulse beating beside her jaw.

“Water, Agnes.”

Agnes emptied a jug over the sleeve. Steam rose between us. Iris pulled once more, then stopped so abruptly that I nearly lost my balance.

I laid the surviving cloth on a clean plate.

“Fetch Daniel. Ask him to come here.”

Agnes hesitated.

“Take the plate with you,” I said.

Only when she had carried it beyond the kitchen door did I release Iris.

Rain rattled against the scullery window. Behind her, the stove continued its steady domestic roar. She rubbed her wrist, looking at the whitening marks my fingers had left.

“You have hurt me.”

“You nearly overturned the kettle.”

Neither of us looked at it.

Daniel arrived with Agnes beside him. I asked him to remain at the doorway while I examined the sleeve. From my pocket I took the folded paper containing the fragment recovered from Rook’s hand. My first notes recorded where it had lain, its dimensions, and the broken stitching along one edge.

I opened the paper on the scrubbed table.

Colour alone would have meant little. Dark cloth was common enough, and fire had altered what remained of the dress. But the torn edges fitted at each uneven projection. A split in the seam continued across the join; two broken stitches found their answering ends.

I kept my hands still so that Agnes and Daniel could see.

Iris watched without approaching.

“It proves nothing about the books.”

“I am not asking it to.”

Her eyes lifted to mine then. There was anger in them, but also calculation, as though she were listening for the precise boundary of what I could establish.

I separated the pieces again and wrapped each in fresh paper, marking where it had come from. Agnes brought a tin box. I placed both packets inside and kept the key.

At the table I opened my notebook.

Agnes had seen Iris leaving the study. Iris had denied returning there. I wrote those statements separately, then the account of Rook showing her the packet and speaking of what Monday’s examination might disclose. The private meeting belonged within the interval before he was found dead.

During a struggle, Rook had caught her sleeve. That was the plainest explanation of the cloth in his hand, though it did not tell me every movement that followed. Afterwards she had changed her dress. Now she had tried to destroy it.

I left a space beneath those facts.

The two missing archive items required their own accounting. I entered each catalogue reference on a separate line, recording the listed leaves against those actually present. The substitutions established interference with the collection; they did not establish who had struck Rook. Nor could I assume both removals had occurred together.

Finally I recorded the scratches beside the interior bolt and the thread. A thread might have drawn the bolt from outside. Until I could test its course and purchase, that remained a possibility.

Across the table, Iris sat with her hands folded.

Outside, floodwater covered the road. No one could bring us help tonight.

I turned back to the page and began fixing each event to the last moment at which someone could swear they had seen it.

I brought them into the drawing room while Agnes lit the second lamp. Beyond the curtains, rain struck the glass with the persistent sound of sand being poured. Helena remained beside the mantel; Daniel stood behind a chair. Iris sat with her wrists hidden beneath her sleeves.

“I have arranged what we know in order,” I said. “But first we must separate what was witnessed from what was supposed.”

Agnes looked at the notebook in my hand.

“You heard the study bolt move?”

“Quite distinctly.”

“Did you hear Rook speak then? A footstep, a chair—anything that required him to be alive?”

Her fingers tightened upon her handkerchief. “No. I thought—”

“Naturally. But the sound proves only that the bolt moved.”

Iris watched me without blinking.

“The windows are fixed. The flue will not admit a person, and the door opens inward. Those facts exclude the escapes we imagined. They do not tell us who killed him.”

We went together to the study. The corridor smelt of wet wool and cold ashes. I asked Daniel to hold the lamp while I drew the two existing abrasions beside the bolt, noting their lengths and positions against the repaired jamb. No one touched the wood until I had finished.

The repaired jamb still allowed a narrow clearance above the bolt’s course. Its knob stood sufficiently proud for a loop to take hold of it. With the door open, I slid the bolt back and forth. The slight resistance I remembered lay at the keeper; along its mounting it travelled readily.

“Watch the thread.”

I took a length of fine thread, looped it around the exposed knob, and led both ends through that existing clearance, above the bolt’s path. Daniel stayed by the desk to reopen the door afterwards. I stepped into the corridor with the others and closed it, both loose ends in my hand.

Then I drew upon both ends together. The thread tightened obliquely towards the jamb. Through the wood came a brief scrape, followed by the familiar metallic click.

Agnes inhaled sharply.

I tried the handle. The door would not open.

“The bolt is in its keeper.”

I released one end and drew steadily upon the other. The loop slipped from the knob; the thread came back into my hand.

Nothing remained crossing the threshold.

Daniel opened the door when I called. Against my first drawing, the paired abrasions now showed fresh, pale scoring. I recorded that alteration separately.

“The thread has reproduced the marks. The remnant we found fits this account. The repaired jamb admits the strands, and its fresh varnish records their passage. Neither another exit nor a hidden mechanism is necessary.”

“Anyone might have used thread,” Iris said.

“Yes. That is precisely the distinction.”

For a moment her shoulders eased. I had feared that the ingenuity of the fastening might eclipse the simpler evidence. The cloth still required an answer.

Back in the drawing room, I set the locked tin upon the table.

“The thread explains the fastening. It does not identify its user. The cloth connects you to the attack. The fragment in Rook’s hand fits your torn cuff, down to the broken threads. Agnes saw you leaving his study; you denied returning. Afterwards you changed your dress and tried to destroy it.”

Iris looked towards the door.

“You met him before the bolt was shot. He caught your sleeve during the attack. You left him dead, closed the door, and drew the bolt from outside. What Agnes heard happened afterwards.”

“And the books?”

“The archive discrepancies explain why he threatened exposure, and why you feared Monday’s examination. They are not proof of murder. The cloth and your false account must be considered independently.”

Nobody spoke. Iris lowered her eyes to the tin, then tucked her hands beneath her arms.

Iris kept her hands tucked beneath her arms. Beyond the drawing-room windows the rain blurred the lawn, and water hurried down the panes in wavering lines. The road was still under floodwater; no one could leave for the village, and no one could bring the authorities to us. In the silence, the small click of the bolt seemed to linger in the house.

“You have shown that thread can move it,” Iris said. “You have not shown that I did.”

“No,” I said. “The thread alone does not identify you.”

I let the distinction stand. The study door opened inward; its windows were fixed, and the flue was too narrow for a person to pass. The repaired jamb had fresh varnish over the old clearance. Our trial had shown that fine thread could be looped around the bolt’s exposed knob, passed through that gap above its path, and drawn from the corridor to slide the bolt into its keeper. When one end was released, the thread could be withdrawn. The paired abrasions on the bolt were reproduced. No second exit, hidden catch, or extraordinary strength was needed.

“Agnes heard the bolt,” I continued. “She was right about the sound. But a bolt moving after you left tells us nothing about whether Rook was alive when it moved.”

Agnes sat very straight, her fingers clasped so tightly that the knuckles showed white. She had been certain the click meant the study was secured, and certainty had become a comfort to her. Now she looked down at her hands.

“You met him before that sound,” I said to Iris. “You went to his study privately, while the door was still open to you. He had told you he meant to expose the archive discrepancies at Monday’s examination. That was a reason for you to fear what he would say. It is not, by itself, proof that you killed him.”

I could not tell them precisely how the attack began, or how many times Rook tried to get away. The room had not yielded that kind of knowledge. What we could say was narrower: you were alone with him; he died during that meeting; and his hand closed on a piece of cloth torn from your cuff. The fragment fits the damaged edge and broken threads of your sleeve. It is not an inference drawn from the bolt, but evidence recovered from the struggle.

Agnes had seen you leaving the study. When asked, you said you had not seen Rook after dinner. That denial made her account more important, not less. Afterwards you changed your dress and tried to destroy the garment you had worn. Each fact has its own weight; together, they make a sequence you cannot explain by the thread.

For an instant Iris’s expression softened, as though the distinction between the archive and the murder might still offer her shelter. I closed the tin containing the separately labelled exhibits.

“The books explain the threat,” I said. “They may explain your motive. They do not prove that you removed anything, or that the two discrepancies arose at the same time. I will not use them to make the case stronger than it is. The fragment, Agnes’s sighting, and your false denial are independent of that inquiry.”

Iris said nothing. The others watched her, but no one moved to block the door. I had made no request for a confession; a confession could be denied, withdrawn, or confused by fear. The evidence had to be preserved as it was, not improved by pressure. I gave Daniel instructions to keep the cloth and thread apart, each marked and sealed, and to leave the study untouched.

Until the flood fell and the authorities could reach us, Iris would remain under observation. Daniel took his place in the corridor. The rain went on tapping at the windows, and the household stayed in the drawing room, bound together now less by uncertainty than by the duty of remembering exactly what each of us had seen.

The rain had weakened to a steady hiss against the windows, but the causeway remained under water. No one could leave Greywater House, and no one seemed eager to try. We stayed in the drawing room while the fire burned low, speaking in turns and pausing whenever a gust pressed at the shutters. The reconstruction was complete. What remained was to learn what each person had hidden from it.

Daniel spoke first. His search upstairs, he said, had been for the debt acknowledgment Rook kept among his papers. Agnes had asked him to help search the archive, but the acknowledgment was his own object: he feared what Rook might do with it, and had wanted to find it before Rook could use it against him. He had not gone upstairs to remove anything from the collection.

I wrote down his account without asking him to make it fit Agnes’s. Her first version had put Daniel with her in the drawing room. Later, she said she had found him alone in the library shortly after nine; she denied that anyone had been accompanying him, and denied that he had been with her. Those statements could not all describe the same moment. Agnes looked at the page as I read them back, her fingers laced so tightly that the knuckles had whitened.

“I lied because I thought the search would bring trouble on him,” she said. “And because I knew what it would sound like if I admitted I had asked him to help.”

She recognized that her false account might be taken as a defense of Daniel. I recorded that, too. So I recorded what she had actually seen, what she had merely assumed, and what she could not know. She had seen Daniel away from the study. She assumed Rook was alive when the bolt slid. An assumption, however sincere, could not fix the moment of death. I kept each statement in its proper column; loyalty did not make her observations worthless, nor did it make her conclusions facts.

Helena had waited through the others’ accounts with her gaze fixed on the dark window. At last she rose, went to her room, and returned with a packet of letters. Her solicitor’s correspondence described arrangements she had begun making to leave Rook. She acknowledged the financial confrontation she had concealed: Rook had discovered the plan and pressed her about the money. She had hidden both the letters and the argument because she wanted time to go, not because she wished the household to mistake her silence for proof of anything. Her voice remained even, but she held the packet as if it might still be taken from her.

Iris’s papers required no such personal explanation. I compared the archive entries with the surviving sale correspondence, checking dates, descriptions, and prices. Together they established that pieces had been substituted and that originals had been sold. The records explained the collection’s discrepancies and the urgency behind Iris’s fear of prosecution. They did not, by themselves, establish when each substitution had occurred, and they did not prove the killing. I kept those findings separate from the evidence already gathered: the preserved cuff fragment in Rook’s hand, the matching broken threads, the thread and damage at the study doorframe, Agnes’s sighting of Iris leaving the study, and Iris’s denial that she had seen Rook after dinner. Her later change of dress and attempt to destroy the garment belonged in the same account. None required a confession to have weight.

Before dawn, I labelled the archive records, the sale letters, and the household accounts, then sealed them apart from the cloth and thread exhibits. Daniel checked each label against my list. No one offered to improve their earlier testimony. That restraint mattered. Contradictions were not stains to be scrubbed away but part of the record.

By morning the storm had eased. Water still shone over the low stones, yet the causeway was passable, and the authorities arrived with the first grey light. I handed over the preserved cuff, the thread and doorframe evidence, the archive records, and the accounts. Then I watched them enter the study, carrying their cases through the doorway whose bolt had occupied so much of the night. The room had been locked. That was a fact. That a locked door made the crime impossible had only ever been an assumption.

By morning, the storm had spent itself. Rain still fell from the eaves in thin, exhausted threads, but the wind had withdrawn across the marsh, leaving Greywater House wrapped in a pale and dripping silence. Beyond the windows, the causeway showed itself again: a dark ribbon of stone beneath the retreating water.

At six, Daniel and I carried the evidence into the morning room, where the fire had burned low and the air smelled of wet wool, ash, and sealing wax. I had arranged everything in separate packets. The preserved cuff, still bearing the fragment torn from it, lay in a paper envelope marked with the time and place of recovery. The loose thread from the study doorframe occupied another. The archive papers, sale letters, and household accounts were tied in bundles, each distinguished by a different label.

Daniel checked them against my list.

“You have kept the financial papers apart,” he said.

“They explain Iris’s fear,” I replied. “They do not explain Rook’s death.”

The distinction had become more important as the night advanced. The archive established that objects from the collection had been substituted, then sold, and that Iris had altered the household accounts to conceal the losses. It explained her terror of prosecution and the urgency with which she had tried to destroy the evidence. But fraud was not murder. To combine them carelessly would weaken both conclusions.

I wrote my account in a steady hand. Daniel’s search upstairs had been for the debt acknowledgment, not for some mysterious object connected with the killing. Agnes had asked him to help search the archive, then concealed that request because she believed the concealment would protect him. Her false alibi—that she had found Daniel in the library shortly after nine—had grown from the same impulse. She had also insisted that no one had accompanied him, and that he had not been with her. Her statements were inconsistent, but their inconsistencies had a shape. They were the anxious architecture of protection, not evidence that Daniel had entered the study.

Iris’s position was different.

The cloth recovered from Rook’s hand matched the damaged sleeve of her discarded dress. The broken threads corresponded with the fibres caught in the study doorframe. Agnes had seen Iris leaving the study. Iris had denied seeing Rook after dinner, then changed her clothing and attempted to destroy the garment. Each fact stood independently. Together they formed a chain no confession was needed to complete.

At half past seven, wheels sounded beyond the windows. The authorities had reached the house.

I met them at the entrance and surrendered the packets, explaining the order in which each had been preserved. I directed them to the undisturbed doorframe before anyone crossed the study. The senior investigator listened without interruption, his wet coat shedding drops onto the flags. Behind him, two officers carried cases and measuring instruments.

When they entered the study, the bolt remained in place, and the room looked as it had throughout the night: close, orderly, and falsely self-contained.

I watched them begin their independent examination. The room had been locked. That was a fact. The conclusion that a locked room made the crime impossible had been only an assumption.
