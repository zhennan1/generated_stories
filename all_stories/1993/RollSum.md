## Chapter 1: The Telegram

The telegram arrived while I was trying to prove that a man with one leg had burned down his own tobacconist’s shop. He had lost the leg in the fire. The insurance company considered this an elaborate precaution.

I had once investigated murders. Now I charged eight pounds a week to doubt the unfortunate.

The messenger stood in my doorway, dripping onto the patch of carpet my landlord still described as Persian. Inside his envelope were a telegram, a bank draft for fifty pounds, and a slip bearing Arthur Pemberton’s name.

The telegram read:

HE IS ALIVE. COME TO BLACKWOOD PEAK. IF I AM FOUND DEAD, THE ANSWER IS IN THE WALL.

I read it twice, then opened the bottom drawer of my desk.

Pemberton had written three times that autumn. Long letters, cramped handwriting. Someone followed him. Someone entered rooms without using the doors. A face he had buried kept appearing in windows.

I had answered the first with a suggestion that he consult a physician. The other two lay beneath an unpaid gas bill.

Fifty pounds would settle the gas, the rent, and several other questions of immediate importance. I put the letters in my case.

It was not concern that got me out of the chair. That came later.

***

By the time I reached the funicular station, the mountain had disappeared.

Snow streamed past the platform lamps. Above us, two rails climbed into whiteness at an angle that made the little carriage look like a regrettable experiment.

“Last run,” the operator said.

“Until morning?”

He looked at my city shoes. “Until we can see the track.”

I climbed aboard.

The cable drew us upward with a series of iron complaints. Houses dwindled below, their windows briefly visible through the snow. Then there was nothing beyond the glass but the occasional black shoulder of rock.

At one bend I looked back. Our tracks were already vanishing.

Blackwood Peak Hotel emerged suddenly, a mass of gables and chimneys above a sheer white slope. Its lower walls rose from a stone shelf at the glacier’s edge; beneath them, blue ice showed through the wind-scoured snow. Someone had built a Victorian mansion there when money and judgment were apparently kept in separate banks.

The porter waiting at the upper station took my case before I offered it.

“Mr. Hale.”

“How did you know?”

“Mr. Pemberton said you’d come.”

He was a thin man with pale eyelashes and a scar disappearing beneath his collar. His name, he told me, was Elias.

“Where is Pemberton?”

“In his rooms.”

“Tell him I’m here.”

Elias glanced toward the house. “He left instructions he wasn’t to be disturbed.”

“That seems a curious welcome.”

“Yes, sir.”

He said it without agreement.

***

Inside, the hotel smelled of coal smoke, furniture polish, and the damp wool of people who had expected better weather. Electric bulbs glowed in fittings made for gas. Along the hall, portraits of severe men watched me surrender my coat.

The manager gave me a room in the west wing and explained that the telephone line had failed an hour earlier.

“What about the railway?”

“The lower connection has stopped running. We’re quite comfortable here, Mr. Hale.”

He spoke as though I had accused him of something.

I sent up my card to Pemberton. It came back with a message conveyed through Elias: Mr. Pemberton would see me after dinner.

“He’s well?”

“I haven’t seen him since this afternoon.”

“Who gave you the message?”

“Miss Keene.”

By then I was hungry enough to postpone indignation.

Four places were occupied in a dining room laid for twenty. The windows reflected the candles; beyond those reflections, snow struck the glass with a dry, persistent hiss.

A broad man with a red face lowered his whisky as I approached.

“Hale? Not the detective?”

“Not any longer.”

“Yes. I remember.”

Most people did.

He introduced himself as Marcus Webb, Pemberton’s business partner, and pronounced the last word as if it had become an unpleasant legal technicality.

“So Arthur’s sent for protection,” he said.

“Advice.”

“That’ll be cheaper, then.”

Across from him, a woman in black silk gave a small, tired smile.

“Don’t mind Marcus. He’s been losing arguments all afternoon.”

Celia Pemberton had a stage actress’s voice, low enough to make a man lean forward. I recognized her from theatre posters, though the posters had omitted the fine lines beside her eyes.

“Your husband invited me,” I said.

“My husband invites people when he wants something. We’re all waiting to discover what it is this time.”

“You could have declined.”

Her smile disappeared. “So could you.”

Beside her sat a neat young woman in a grey dress. She had stopped eating when I arrived.

“Miss Keene?” I asked.

“Ruth Keene. Mr. Pemberton’s secretary.”

“Is he joining us?”

“He has a headache.”

Webb made a sound into his glass.

Ruth looked at him. “He’s been under considerable strain.”

“He’s put the rest of us under it, too.”

“Arthur is frightened,” she said.

For a moment, nobody touched a knife or fork.

“Of what?” I asked.

Her eyes moved toward Celia, then back to her plate. “He’ll tell you himself.”

Elias entered to replenish the coal fire. At Pemberton’s name, his shovel paused against the scuttle.

Webb noticed. “Something to contribute?”

“No, sir.”

“Then shut the door. There’s a draught.”

Arthur Pemberton’s chair remained empty through the soup, the roast, and a pudding nobody seemed to want.

***

At ten, Ruth intercepted me beside the stairs.

“He’s taken something to help him sleep. He asks if you’ll forgive him until morning.”

“I’ve come a long way.”

“I know.” She held the banister tightly. “Please don’t leave.”

I looked toward the windows shaking in their frames.

“I doubt I could.”

In my room I unpacked Pemberton’s letters and read them properly for the first time. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves. Once, footsteps passed my door, stopped, and continued.

I lay down without undressing.

Sleep came reluctantly. The scream tore me out of it—a woman’s voice, high and breathless, somewhere in the east wing.

I was running before it stopped.

## Chapter 2: The Locked Door

The scream came from the east wing, high and breathless, then stopped as abruptly as if a hand had closed over the mouth that made it.

I was out of bed before I knew I had moved. The corridor beyond my door was dark except for two weak electric bulbs. Snow struck the windows in furious handfuls, and somewhere below us a shutter slammed.

“Mr. Hale!”

Elias came running from the stairwell. His thin face looked bloodless.

“What happened?”

He pointed toward the east wing. “Pemberton’s rooms.”

We ran.

At the turn of the corridor we nearly collided with Marcus Webb. He was in shirtsleeves and braces, a revolver in one hand. Behind him stood Celia Pemberton in a dark dressing gown, her loose hair falling over one shoulder. Ruth Keene followed, clutching her purse against her chest.

At the end of the passage stood Sheriff Calder.

I had not seen him since the courthouse steps in Manchester, three years earlier, when he had told the newspapers that Victor Hale mistook cleverness for evidence and had ruined an innocent man’s life to prove a theory.

He had grown heavier since then. His gray moustache bristled over a mouth made for disapproval. He held a lantern and was studying the door before him.

Arthur Pemberton’s door.

“It’s bolted,” he said.

I tried the handle. The brass knob turned, but the heavy oak did not move. Something solid held it fast from within.

“Arthur!” Ruth cried. “Mr. Pemberton!”

There was no answer.

Calder struck the door with his fist. “Pemberton! Sheriff’s office!”

Silence followed.

Webb swore. “Break it.”

Calder stepped aside. “Hale, you have the shoulders.”

“I’ve also got sense enough to know a door doesn’t open because you insult it.”

“Then stand aside.”

He and Webb drove themselves against it. The first impact shook the frame. The second cracked the wood. On the third, the lock plate tore loose and the door burst inward.

The room was warm and dim, lit only by the low fire.

Arthur Pemberton lay beside the hearth.

For a moment I saw only his dressing gown, his white shirt, and one hand lying palm-up on the carpet. Then the details settled into place: two dark wounds beneath the ribs and a jagged cut across his left forearm. Blood had soaked the front of his shirt and spread into the rug.

Celia made a small sound and caught Webb’s arm. Ruth stared past us, rigid with horror.

Calder entered, knelt, and pressed two fingers to Pemberton’s throat. He withdrew them almost at once.

“Dead,” he said.

The word seemed inadequate. Arthur’s face was turned toward the fire, his eyes half open, a crease fixed between his brows. He looked less frightened than annoyed, as though death had interrupted an argument.

Calder rose.

“Suicide.”

I looked at him. “Two stab wounds?”

“Men have done stranger things.”

“And the cut on his arm?”

“He may have lost his nerve.”

“That is a defensive wound.”

His gaze hardened. “You’ve been out of the force a long time.”

“Not long enough to forget what one looks like.”

He gave a humorless laugh. “You always did prefer murder. It made your reports interesting.”

“I prefer facts.”

I crouched beside the body. The wounds were close together, one slightly higher than the other. The second entered at an upward angle from below. The cut on the arm was shallow, but its position was unmistakable: Pemberton had raised it to ward off a blade.

Most of the blood had soaked into his shirt. The body had fallen almost where the attack occurred.

“Did anyone touch him?” I asked.

“No,” said Calder.

“Who found him?”

“Elias heard the scream,” Ruth said. Her voice shook. “He was in the corridor.”

“And the rest of you?”

“I was in my room,” said Celia.

“So was I,” said Webb. “I came from the lounge.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

Calder interrupted. “Later.”

I ignored him. “Who had access to this room?”

“Mr. Pemberton,” Ruth answered. “Me, when he needed me. Mr. Webb. Mrs. Pemberton.”

“And Elias?”

The porter lowered his eyes. “To clean.”

I examined the inside of the door. A small brass key hung in the lock, turned halfway. Beside it was a solid brass bolt, fully thrown into its socket. When we had broken the door, the wood around the lock had splintered, but the bolt remained undisturbed.

“Where was the key?” I asked.

“In the lock,” Calder said.

He crossed to the windows. Three tall panes faced the glacier. Frost covered them so thickly that no light entered. He tried the nearest latch. It did not move.

“Painted shut,” he said.

I tested the middle window myself. The frame gave no more than stone.

Celia looked toward the fireplace. “Could he have gone through the chimney?”

Calder lifted his lantern and examined the opening. The chimney narrowed sharply above the mantel.

“Too narrow for a child,” he said.

“Then we have a dead man in a sealed room,” I said.

“We have a man who locked himself in and killed himself.”

“No.”

Calder turned on me. “You’ve been here less than five minutes.”

“I’ve been looking.”

“And what have you found?”

I pointed to the body. “He was stabbed twice. He raised his arm against the second attack. The weapon is missing.”

“Unless it’s in the fire.”

I looked into the grate. The poker and tongs rested on their stand. The coals glowed beneath a blackened log. There was no knife, no blade, no metal edge among the ash.

“Nothing there,” I said.

“Then he threw it out the window.”

“The windows are sealed.”

“Down the chimney?”

“Too narrow.”

Calder spread his hands. “You’ve just described suicide in a locked room.”

“No,” I said. “I’ve described murder in one.”

He stared at me.

I bent over Pemberton again. The wounds were too close, too deliberate. The second was angled upward, delivered by someone standing near him. No man could have inflicted it upon himself in that position. And after the first wound, he had raised his arm against the attacker.

“No man stabs himself twice,” I said, “and then slashes his own arm in self-defense.”

For several seconds, no one spoke. Outside, the blizzard pressed against the hotel like a living thing.

I searched the carpet around the body, then the hearth, the desk, and beneath the curtains. I opened drawers and checked the washstand, wardrobe, and wastebasket. There was no bloodied paper-cutter, no broken glass, no knife hidden anywhere in the room.

Calder watched me with folded arms.

The room was sealed. The windows were frozen shut. The chimney could admit no man. The bolt had been thrown from inside.

Arthur Pemberton had been murdered.

And somehow, between the locking of that bolt and the scream in the corridor, his killer had vanished into thin air.

## Chapter 3: The Suspects

Calder had a blanket brought for Pemberton’s body, then posted the manager outside the broken door.

“No one goes in,” he said, looking at me.

“No one leaves the hotel either.”

He glanced toward a window white with driven snow. “I doubt we need your help arranging that.”

We questioned the others in the drawing room. Calder took the chair beside the hearth; I stood near the mantel. The fire gave little heat. Above it, a painted Blackwood ancestor watched us with the mild contempt of a man whose troubles had ended.

Webb came first, still carrying his revolver.

“Put that down,” Calder told him.

“There’s a murderer about.”

“Then I’d rather know where the guns are.”

Webb laid it on the table reluctantly.

He said he had been drinking in the lounge since dinner. Alone. He had heard the scream and come upstairs.

“Who served you?” I asked.

“I served myself.”

“Did anyone see you?”

“Not that I noticed.”

“Did you leave the lounge?”

“To fetch cigarettes from my room. Before eleven.”

“And visit Arthur?”

“No.”

His denial came with a glance at Calder, as though he expected protection from the question.

“What was your quarrel with him?” I asked.

“We had differences over the company.”

“Serious differences?”

Webb rubbed his broad face. “Arthur wanted to dissolve the partnership. Call in everything outstanding. He didn’t care what that would do.”

“What would it do?”

“Ruin me.”

The word came out flat. For the first time, he looked sober.

“And his death?”

“Doesn’t make the creditors disappear.”

“But it stops him calling in the money tonight.”

Webb leaned forward. “If you mean to accuse me, do it.”

“I’m trying to establish where you were.”

“I’ve told you.”

He had. Nobody could confirm it.

Celia entered with a shawl over her dressing gown. She chose the chair farthest from the fire, then seemed to regret it.

“I was in my room,” she told Calder. “I had a headache.”

“All evening?”

“Since dinner.”

“Did your husband send for you?”

“No.”

“Did you go to him?”

“No.”

I moved a chair nearer the hearth. As she rose to take it, she stumbled on the hem of her gown. I caught her hand.

It was cold enough to startle me.

“Have you been outside?”

She pulled away. “In this weather?”

“Your hands are freezing.”

“My room is freezing.”

“The west rooms are warm.”

“Then perhaps I should have married the architect.”

She delivered it neatly, but her eyes stayed on my face.

“You heard nothing before the scream?” I asked.

“Nothing.”

“No voices?”

“I may have heard Arthur speaking. Through the wall. I wasn’t listening.”

“You said you heard nothing.”

“I had a headache, Mr. Hale. Not a notebook.”

Calder asked about her marriage. She looked down at the hand I had touched.

“Arthur intended to stop my allowance,” she said. “He told me yesterday.”

“On what grounds?”

“That I ought to earn my living.”

“You were an actress.”

“I was his wife.”

For a moment the practiced composure left her.

“Where would you have gone?” I asked.

“I don’t know.”

She offered it without embellishment. It was the most convincing thing she had said.

Ruth Keene sat upright, her purse clamped between both hands.

“I was typing in the office.”

“Until when?” Calder asked.

“Until I heard the scream.”

“What were you typing?”

“Business letters.”

“May we see them?”

“They aren’t finished.”

“That won’t matter,” I said.

Her fingers tightened on the purse.

“Was there anyone with you?”

“No.”

“Did anyone pass the office?”

“I wasn’t watching the corridor.”

She had urged me to stay because Arthur was frightened. Now she seemed determined to make his last evening sound ordinary.

“Who benefits under his will?” I asked.

“I can’t discuss that.”

“Arthur can no longer object.”

Her mouth trembled.

“He made provision for me. Years of service. I have no pension.”

“Money?”

“An income.”

“Could he revoke it?”

“He could do anything he pleased.”

The bitterness surprised her as much as it surprised me. She lowered her eyes.

Calder dismissed her, instructing her to leave the letters untouched. She rose without loosening her hold on the purse.

Elias came last. Standing just inside the door, he watched Webb’s revolver on the table.

“Sit down,” Calder said.

“I’d rather stand.”

“You heard the scream?”

“Yes.”

“Where were you?”

“In the east corridor. I was taking coal upstairs.”

“Did you see anyone?”

“No.”

His answer was too careful.

“What did you see?” I asked.

He looked toward the closed drawing-room door.

“The light under Mr. Pemberton’s door. Something moved across it.”

“Someone in the corridor?”

“No. Inside. A dark bit crossed the light, then it was clear again.”

“A shadow,” Calder said.

“Yes.”

“And then the scream?”

“First he spoke.”

“What did he say?”

Elias rubbed a thumb along the scar at his mouth.

“‘You’re dead.’”

The fire settled with a soft crash.

“Are you certain?” I asked.

“I heard him plain.”

“Who was he speaking to?”

“How should I know?”

“Did you recognize another voice?”

“There wasn’t one.”

“You knew he was afraid of someone?”

Elias stared at me. “People say things when they’re frightened.”

“Is that what he sounded like?”

He hesitated. “Surprised.”

Calder questioned him twice more. Elias repeated the words exactly, but offered nothing about why they meant so little to him—or so much.

When he had gone, Calder rose.

“A frightened man talks to himself. Then he kills himself. There’s your explanation.”

“Where is the knife?”

“We’ll search again.”

“And the wound on his arm?”

“Not every cut is a defensive wound.”

“This one is.”

He faced me. “The door was bolted. The windows cannot open. You examined the chimney yourself. Those are facts, Hale.”

“So are two stab wounds and a missing weapon.”

“You built a case on certainty once before.”

I remembered the man I had sent to prison. I remembered how little his denials had troubled me.

“I won’t mistake your certainty for evidence either,” I said.

Calder ordered everyone to remain in their rooms. Outside, the telephone wires were down and the funicular tracks lay buried. No help could reach us.

I watched him leave, then looked at the revolver Webb had surrendered.

Arthur had been murdered. Until I could explain that bolted door, Calder would keep calling it suicide.

And the killer would remain among us.

## Chapter 4: The Letter

The manager was still outside Arthur’s door, sitting on a straight-backed chair with a blanket round his shoulders. He rose when he saw me.

“The sheriff said nobody.”

“The sheriff also said suicide. I want to find the knife.”

He looked past me along the empty corridor.

“I’ll tell him you went in.”

“Please do.”

He moved his chair aside. I slipped through the broken doorway, careful of the splintered oak, and heard him settle again outside.

The room had grown cold. Snow brushed the windows with a dry, persistent whisper. Calder had left a lamp burning on the desk, but its light did not reach the corners. Beside the hearth, the sheet covering Arthur had darkened where it touched his shirt.

I stood just inside the door and tried to forget what everyone had told me.

A man had died here. Someone had brought a knife into this room and taken it away. Those were facts. Everything else required examination—including my certainty that I understood the wounds.

I lifted the sheet.

Arthur’s left hand lay palm upward, the fingers slightly bent. The cut on his forearm had parted the sleeve. I pictured that arm rising between his body and a blade, but a picture was not proof. Three years earlier I had built a case out of pictures so vivid that a jury had mistaken them for things witnessed.

I lowered the sheet carefully.

His clothes hung in the wardrobe. We had searched it for a weapon, feeling behind the garments and along the floor, but nobody had emptied the pockets. His coat was good dark wool, with a silk lining worn thin beneath the arms.

There was a handkerchief in one pocket, a railway receipt in another.

In the inner breast pocket I found an envelope.

My name was written across it.

For several seconds I did nothing. The envelope was unsealed; the flap had been tucked inside. I carried it to the desk and drew out two folded sheets.

The hotel’s crest stood at the top. Beneath it Arthur had written the date and, with unusual precision, the time: half past eight that evening.

*Dear Hale,*

*Miss Keene tells me you have arrived. I meant to speak to you tonight, but I find that I cannot trust myself to say plainly what must be said. You have already thought me mad once. I should prefer you to read this before you do so again.*

I heard the chair creak in the corridor.

*The man I have seen is my brother Silas. My twin brother. He was lost in a boating accident twenty years ago, and was presumed drowned. There was no body.*

*I know what twenty years can do to a face. I know, too, what it cannot do. I have seen him close enough to be certain.*

I moved the lamp nearer.

*He is in this hotel. I do not mean that he has taken a room under another name. I mean that he is here where they say nobody can be. Yesterday I heard him moving after the corridor was empty. This afternoon I saw him at the turn beyond my sitting room. By the time I reached it, he had gone.*

*We stayed in this house as boys, before it became a hotel. He knows the old ways of this house.*

The final lines had been pressed so hard that the nib had torn the paper.

*Do not let them persuade you that I imagined him.*

*If I am found dead, the answer is in the wall.*

*Arthur Pemberton.*

I read it twice.

Then I took his earlier letters from my pocket and laid them beside it. I had brought them because they concerned a paying client, not because I believed him. The first still bore my pencil mark beside a passage about a face at a window.

*Nerves?*

I remembered writing it while my kettle boiled. I had been mildly irritated by the length of the letter.

Now Arthur lay six feet away beneath a sheet, and I knew the name of the man he had been trying to describe.

That did not make every word true. A frightened man could be mistaken. Twins could die in boating accidents; grief and guilt could survive them. But Arthur had deserved questions. I had given him a pencil mark.

I folded the letters and put them away.

Before touching the walls, I went back to the door.

The brass bolt remained thrown. I worked its knob through the full travel, examining the underside and the socket torn from the jamb. There was no thread caught around it, no scoring that suggested a wire. With the door closed before it was broken, the bolt would have been well beyond reach from the corridor.

The key offered no better answer.

I crossed to the windows and tested each catch. Paint bridged frame and sash in old, unbroken ridges. Frost obscured the glass, but the little I could see beyond it was empty air above the glacier. I pressed harder than necessary. Nothing moved.

At the fireplace I knelt and raised the lamp. The flue narrowed above the smoke shelf to a black throat scarcely wider than my hand.

Door, windows, chimney.

I had examined them all before. Repeating the examination had changed nothing.

*The answer is in the wall.*

I began beside the wardrobe, tapping the plaster with the handle of my penknife. The noise sounded foolishly loud. I worked round the room, listening, then crouched to test the dark wooden wainscoting beneath it.

Near the hearth, one panel answered differently.

I tapped its neighbour, then returned to it. A small, hollow note.

The carving concealed most of the joints, but at the right-hand edge a fine line ran against the pattern of the wood. I brought the lamp down. Dust filled the lower moulding except at one point, where a narrow scrape showed pale.

I inserted the knife blade there and pressed.

For a moment the wood resisted. Then something clicked behind it.

The panel came towards me so suddenly that I nearly dropped the lamp. Cold air touched my wrist.

I pulled it wider.

Behind the wainscoting was a narrow, dark opening, tall enough for a man.

## Chapter 5: The Priest-Hole

The opening was narrower than it had appeared from the room. I turned sideways and stepped through, my shoulders brushing both jambs. A current of cold air struck me, carrying the smell of damp stone.

I raised the candle.

The compartment rose beside the chimney in a black vertical slit, its walls made of rough stone and old timber. It was tall enough for a man to stand in, though scarcely wide enough for his shoulders. A priest-hole, I thought—a place built into an old house for concealing a hunted priest while soldiers searched the rooms.

The floor lay below the level of the room, reached by two shallow steps. Dust filmed the stones, except where it had been disturbed.

Someone had been there recently.

I crouched and lowered the candle. Two footprints showed in the dust, one complete and one blurred. They came from the back of the recess. The heel of the second had dragged, as though its owner had turned sharply or been hurried.

Near the wall lay a small object. I picked it up.

A cufflink, gold or a good imitation, with a dark green stone in its face. Black grease marked the hinge. When I held it near my nose, I caught the smell of tobacco beneath the dampness—not Webb’s sweet, heavy smoke, but something sharper and cheaper.

I examined the recess. No door showed in either side. The back wall appeared to be solid brick, mortared from floor to ceiling. I pressed my palm against it. The cold went through my hand.

A man could have hidden there while Arthur entered and bolted the door. He could have waited in the darkness until the murder was done.

But he could not have vanished.

The thought steadied me. A locked room was still a locked room. If the killer had hidden in this compartment, he must have remained in it while Calder and I forced the door. The space was too narrow to overlook, and the room had been searched before I returned. No murderer could have stayed folded in this slit while the hotel gathered around it.

I angled the candle toward the brick. The courses were old and uneven in color, but a straight vertical seam ran from floor to ceiling. The line was too regular to belong to the surrounding masonry.

I touched it. Mortar flaked beneath my nail.

“Mr. Hale?”

I turned too quickly and struck my shoulder against the frame. Ruth Keene stood just inside Arthur’s room. The door behind her was closed, though I had not heard it move.

“What are you doing here?” I asked.

Her eyes went to the open panel. For an instant, fear—or something more deliberate—crossed her face. Then she clasped her purse with both hands.

“I might ask you the same.”

“You may, after you answer me.”

“I was looking for the will.”

“Here?”

“Arthur kept papers everywhere. He told me the old will was in this room.”

“Did he tell you about this?”

“No.” The answer came too quickly. She looked again at the recess. “I mean, I never saw it open.”

“Did you come here after dinner?”

“No.”

“Did you see Arthur tonight?”

“I took him his medicine shortly after eight.”

“And was he alive?”

She looked at me directly. “Obviously.”

“People become less obvious after they are dead.”

Her fingers tightened around the purse. It was the same gesture she had made during questioning, whenever Calder mentioned Arthur’s papers or the inheritance.

“You think I killed him.”

“I think you know more than you’ve told us.”

“You think that of everyone.”

“Everyone has given me reason.”

The room creaked around us. A pipe ticked somewhere behind the walls. Ruth’s gaze kept returning to the priest-hole.

“Close it,” she said.

“Why?”

“It is indecent.”

“A hiding place is indecent?”

“Arthur was not well. He frightened himself with old stories, and now he has frightened you.”

“Did he frighten you?”

The question changed her face. Her lips parted, but no answer came.

“I came for the will,” she said at last. “I haven’t found it. I’m leaving.”

She turned toward the door.

“Miss Keene.”

She stopped.

I held out the cufflink. Her eyes fixed on it, and the color left her face.

“Have you seen this before?”

“No.”

“It has grease on it. Like the floor of the recess.”

“I said I haven’t seen it.”

“Then why are you afraid of it?”

“I am not afraid.”

She went out before I could question her further. The door closed behind her with a soft, careful click.

I listened to her footsteps recede along the corridor. They were light and even. Only when they had faded did I realize how tightly I was holding the cufflink; its edge had cut my palm.

I turned back to the opening.

The candle was burning low. Shadows climbed the walls of the narrow compartment, but the seam remained visible. I studied it again.

At first glance, the rear wall was solid masonry. At second, it was too regular. The bricks on either side were laid in opposing patterns, as if one section had been fitted into the other. It might be a door—not one made to swing, but one intended never to be noticed.

I set the cufflink on the floor and put both hands against the brick.

Cold air touched my knuckles.

Before I could find its source, a scream tore through the east wing.

It was a woman’s scream, high and breathless, close enough to make the candle flame leap.

I snatched up the cufflink and ran for the corridor.

## Chapter 6: The Shadow Under the Door

The scream came thin and high through the corridor, like a violin string breaking.

I ran.

The manager’s lantern swung ahead of me, setting the walls in motion. Doors opened as I passed. Calder emerged from Arthur’s room with one hand on his revolver. Marcus followed, red-faced and scowling.

“Where?” Calder demanded.

The scream came again, muffled now, from the west side of the east wing.

Celia.

I reached her door first. It stood open. She was pressed against the far wall in her dressing gown, one hand at her throat and the other gripping a brass candlestick. A grey shape darted across the carpet and vanished beneath a chest.

“There!” she gasped.

The manager raised his lantern. A rat’s eyes flashed in the light, then disappeared under the skirting.

Calder entered behind me. “That caused all this?”

“It ran at me.” Celia’s breath came in short pulls. “It came from beneath the wardrobe.”

Marcus laughed from the doorway. “Perhaps it has better reason to be frightened than we do.”

Celia turned on him. “Go to hell.”

I studied the room. It was warm, as I had noticed earlier. Yet her hands had been cold when I steadied her after Arthur’s murder, almost cold enough to suggest she had been outside. There was no open window and no draught. Her explanation accounted for the scream, perhaps, but not for everything.

“Did you see anything else?” I asked.

“No.”

“Before the rat?”

“No.”

“Did you hear anything?”

She hesitated. “Scratching.”

“Nothing from Arthur’s room?”

The colour left her face.

Calder saw it. “Mrs. Pemberton?”

“I told you, I had a headache. I was asleep.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“I may have heard voices earlier. Through the wall. I couldn’t make out the words.”

“Arthur’s voice?”

“I don’t know.”

A moment before she had heard nothing. Now she had heard voices. She lowered the candlestick and looked at me with an expression I could not read—pleading, perhaps, or warning.

“The rat was real,” she said.

It was. That did not make the rest of her story true.

Calder ordered the manager to search beneath the furniture. The search produced dust, two hairpins, and a dead moth. The rat had vanished into the walls. Celia was left under the manager’s watch while I went looking for Elias.

I found him in the service passage beside the coal chute, standing just beyond the lantern light. He was watching the drawing-room door. When he saw me, his shoulders tightened.

“You heard the second scream,” I said.

“Yes.”

“You know it was Mrs. Pemberton?”

“Yes.”

“Then you know what she was screaming about?”

“A rat, she says.”

“Do you believe her?”

“Rats are common in old houses.”

“So are lies.”

He said nothing.

I stepped closer. “Tell me again what happened before Arthur died. This time, leave nothing out.”

His scar tightened as he frowned. “The door was shut.”

“I know.”

“There was light beneath it, from the fire and the lamp.”

“Where were you?”

“By the servants’ stair, carrying the scuttle.”

“And then?”

“I heard movement. A scrape, not footsteps. Then a shadow crossed the light.”

“Which way?”

“Left to right.”

“Inside the room?”

His eyes met mine. “Yes.”

“What kind of shadow?”

“A man’s legs. Or the shape of them. It passed quickly.”

“Toward the door?”

“Across it.”

“Then Arthur spoke?”

Elias nodded. “‘You’re dead.’”

“Those exact words?”

“Exactly.”

“Did he sound afraid?”

“No. Surprised.”

“And after that?”

“A noise. A fall, perhaps. Then the scream.”

“Did you hear anyone answer him?”

“No.”

“Did you hear the bolt?”

He looked down the passage. “A metal sound. Yes. Before the scream.”

My thoughts moved backward through the corridor, to the strip of light under Arthur’s door and the brass bolt found driven into its socket.

I had been asking the wrong question.

I had assumed the room was empty until the murderer entered. I had imagined a killer outside, searching for some trick by which the bolt could be drawn from the corridor. But if Arthur had bolted himself in while the killer was already inside—

The shadow had moved within the room. Arthur had seen someone, recognized him, and spoken with astonishment.

You’re dead.

If he had been alone, he might have cast a shadow himself and addressed some imagined figure. But Elias had heard a change in his voice: not fear of a memory, but surprise at a presence. There had been two men in the room when the bolt was thrown.

One dead.

One hidden.

I turned toward Arthur’s suite.

“Where are you going?” Elias asked.

“To find the hiding place.”

“The sheriff sealed it.”

“Then he can arrest me afterward.”

I hurried down the corridor, the cufflink clenched in my hand. The disturbed dust, the footprints, the black grease, and the sharp tobacco smell came back to me with sudden force. I had stood before the priest-hole and seen only a dead end because I expected a passage to reveal itself as a door.

The room had not been sealed.

It had been occupied before the sealing.

The killer had hidden while Arthur entered, waited until the bolt slid home, stepped out to murder him, and then escaped by a route I had failed to see.

The only place in the room large enough to conceal a man was the narrow compartment beside the hearth.

I reached the broken doorway. The manager began to protest, but I pushed past him. Calder’s voice rose behind me. I did not stop.

The fire had sunk low. Arthur’s blood darkened the rug. Beyond it, the wainscoting stood silent beneath the trembling light.

I went straight to the priest-hole.

This time, I meant to find what was behind the wall.

## Chapter 7: The Forged Signature

I had my hand on Arthur’s broken door when I smelled burning paper.

Not the dry, dusty smell of a fire being coaxed to life. This was sharper. It came from the little study across the passage, where a line of yellow light showed beneath the door.

I let go of the splintered oak.

The study door opened without a sound. Ruth Keene knelt before the grate, pressing a folded sheet between the bars with the poker. Her purse stood open on the desk.

“Leave it.”

She jerked round. The poker struck the fender.

I caught her wrist before she could push the paper farther in, then dragged it free with the tongs. One corner had gone to lace. Flame ran along an edge; I crushed it against the hearthstone.

“That is private,” she said.

“So was the room where he died.”

I laid the sheet on the desk. It was a power of attorney, granting Ruth Keene authority to dispose of Arthur Pemberton’s securities, draw upon his accounts, and transfer property into her own name. The language was elaborate. Its purpose was not.

Arthur’s signature sat beneath it, stiff and uncertain.

I took his letter from my pocket and set it beside the document.

“Don’t,” Ruth whispered.

The signature in the letter ran freely, the final stroke falling away from the name. On the power of attorney it stopped, began again, and ended in a small blot. Faint pencil showed beneath the ink.

There was another sheet beside her purse: an old business agreement, folded so that only Arthur’s signature was exposed.

I placed the signatures together. Every loop matched. So did an accidental kink in the capital P.

“You traced it.”

She sat down abruptly.

“For how long?” I asked.

“It was tonight.”

“Before he died?”

“Yes.”

“Did he know?”

She shook her head. For a moment she seemed unable to breathe. Then the tears came, silently, which made them harder to watch.

I had seen innocent people weep under questioning and murderers remain dry-eyed. I waited.

“Marcus was taking the money,” she said at last. “Not borrowing. Taking. Arthur had begun asking for the ledgers, and Marcus kept finding reasons not to produce them.”

“So you proposed to take everything first.”

“To put it beyond his reach.”

“In your own name.”

“I couldn’t put it in Arthur’s. He would only have let Marcus persuade him again.”

“You expected a bank to accept this?”

“I expected time. A few days. Someone would have asked questions, and then Arthur would have had to listen.”

I looked at the scorched authority, the pencilled outline, the old agreement carefully folded for copying.

“You prepared it for use. Not merely to frighten him.”

“Yes.”

“Why burn it now?”

“Because he’s dead.” She rubbed at her cheeks with both hands. “Because I knew what it would look like.”

“You were looking for his will.”

“I was. He said he had made a new one. I wanted to know whether—”

“Whether you still had your income?”

Her hands dropped.

“Yes.”

It was the first answer she had given without trying to improve it.

A floorboard creaked behind me. Calder stood in the doorway, Webb at his shoulder.

“What have you found?” Calder asked.

“Forgery. Not yet murder.”

Webb came forward when he saw the paper. Ruth recoiled from him so sharply that he stopped.

“Tell him about the accounts,” she said.

His eyes narrowed. “Be careful.”

“Tell him why Arthur wanted to end the partnership.”

“Business disagreements don’t concern a secretary.”

“Apparently they concern her enough to give her control of the business,” I said.

Webb reached for the document. I put my hand flat over it.

Ruth looked at me. “He went into Arthur’s room tonight.”

Webb’s hand closed.

“When?” I asked.

“After I took up the medicine. I had gone back for my shorthand book. I saw him from the landing.”

“Before dinner?”

“Yes. A little after eight.”

“Did you see him leave?”

“No.”

“She saw nothing,” Webb said. “I went upstairs for cigarettes.”

“You said that was before eleven.”

“I fetched them twice.”

Calder glanced at him. It was a small glance, but Webb saw it.

“Ask her why she’s lying,” he said.

“I am asking everyone.”

A pale figure appeared beyond Calder. Celia had wrapped a woollen shawl over her dressing gown. She looked from Ruth to Webb and gave a short, unpleasant laugh.

“Oh, Marcus. You might at least show some gratitude.”

“Go back to your room,” he said.

“Why? Is this another matter that doesn’t concern the wives?”

Ruth stood. “Celia, please.”

That plea seemed to settle it.

“They were lovers,” Celia said. “There. Now perhaps we can stop pretending her devotion to Arthur was the only reason she stayed.”

Webb turned towards the door, then thought better of it.

“How long ago?” I asked Ruth.

“It ended last spring.”

“Did Arthur know?”

“No.”

“And the stolen money?”

“I found that afterward.”

Celia tightened the shawl at her throat. “How unfortunate for everybody.”

Ruth flinched. I could not tell whether Celia had struck at a lie or simply found an old wound.

I folded the forged authority into a clean sheet from the desk and gave it to Calder.

“Keep them apart. I want their accounts written down separately.”

He studied the packet. For once he did not argue.

Forgery, theft, a discarded lover: each gave us something worth investigating. None explained how a knife had vanished from a bolted room.

Ruth picked up her purse. As she turned, her sleeve brushed the lamplight.

“Wait.”

Along the underside of her cuff lay a black, glossy smear. I caught the fabric between finger and thumb. It was not ink. It dragged thickly beneath my nail, carrying a faint mineral smell.

The same grease had marked the priest-hole floor and the hinge of the cufflink.

Ruth watched my face.

“You told me you had never seen that compartment open.”

“I didn’t—”

She stopped before completing the denial.

I released her sleeve.

“Calder. She stays here.”

Then I crossed the passage and went back through Arthur’s shattered door.

## Chapter 8: The Boiler Room

Arthur lay where we had left him, under a sheet someone had found the decency to fetch. One hand protruded beside the hearth. In the lamplight it looked waxen and faintly impatient.

I shut what remained of the door.

Across the passage Calder was asking Ruth a question. I heard her answer rise sharply, then subside. For the moment she would stay where she was put.

The black smear beneath her cuff had brought me back here, but it did not tell me what I would find. She might have entered the recess in search of a will. She might have met someone there. Grease was evidence of contact, not murder. I had once helped send a man to prison by forgetting that distinction.

I took the lamp from Arthur’s desk and stepped down into the priest-hole.

The compartment received me like a coffin stood on end. My shoulders brushed stone on one side and timber on the other. Beyond the disturbed footprints, the rear wall showed its narrow, unnatural seam.

I pressed against it. Nothing moved.

With my penknife I scraped the crumbling mortar from the joint. The blade entered farther than it should have. I worked upward, feeling rather than seeing, until the point struck metal.

A little pressure produced a dry click.

The wall yielded beneath my other hand.

I nearly dropped the lamp. A section of brickwork swung inward, complete with its mortar lines, revealing a black opening and a breath of air that smelled of warm iron. The bricks were shallow slips fixed to a framed door. Along the hinge, black grease shone freshly against old rust.

I stood motionless, listening.

Somewhere below, machinery laboured with a slow, heavy pulse.

There was the answer to the locked room. Not yet the answer to the murder.

Before going farther, I returned to Arthur’s desk and wrote on a sheet of hotel paper:

*Calder—passage behind rear wall of recess. Leads down. I am following it. Bring Elias.*

I folded it once and carried it across the landing. The study door was closed. Rather than interrupt an answer I could hear Ruth struggling to give, I put the note into the manager’s hand as he came towards me.

“For the sheriff. Now.”

He looked at the lamp, then my knife.

“Mr Hale—”

“Give him that.”

I went back before he could ask anything else.

Beyond the false wall, three steep steps descended into a passage scarcely wider than the compartment. I left the panel open and followed the slope, carrying the lamp low.

The hotel sounded different from inside its walls. Pipes ticked beside my head. Wind worried a loose board somewhere overhead. Once I heard a muffled voice, so close that I stopped, but no words reached me.

At a turn, my light caught a fresh scrape across the plaster. Farther down lay a cigarette end, pinched flat and stained brown at the tip. The same sharp tobacco smell clung to it that I had noticed on the cufflink.

Someone had stood here and smoked in the dark.

The thought made me glance behind.

There was only the passage, empty as far as the bend.

I went on, one hand against the wall. The steps turned twice, passing a narrow wooden hatch secured by a rusty hook. A draught came through its edges, cold enough to account for the chill in the priest-hole. Ahead, however, the air grew steadily warmer.

At the bottom stood an iron door.

Its latch had been polished by recent use.

I lifted it slowly. The door opened into a cavern of brick vaults and sweating pipes. A boiler occupied the centre, its gauges trembling, its furnace throwing a red bar of light across the concrete floor. Coal lay heaped behind a wooden partition. Overhead, a belt turned with a soft, continuous slap.

The room seemed empty.

“Anyone here?”

My voice came back flattened by the machinery.

I set the lamp on a ledge and looked behind the coal partition. An alcove opened beyond it, screened from the door by a hanging sack. There was a blanket on the floor, a chipped cup, and the remains of a loaf wrapped in newspaper.

A man had been sleeping there.

A dark coat hung from a nail. Its hem was wet, and beneath it a small puddle had gathered among the coal dust. I touched the sleeve. Cold water soaked my fingers.

On a low shelf lay a parcel of oilcloth.

I unfolded one corner.

The knife was long and narrow, with a plain wooden handle. Brown-red stains darkened the blade near the guard. A strand of pale cloth adhered to it.

I did not lift it. I had already handled too much in this house.

Beside the parcel stood a faded photograph, its lower edge curled by damp. Two boys in sailor suits faced the camera on a garden terrace. Each had the same straight mouth, the same pale eyes beneath a level brow. One had his hand on the other’s shoulder.

Arthur and Silas.

A foot scraped behind me.

I turned.

He stood between me and the lamp, so thin that the coat on him seemed borrowed. A ragged beard covered his jaw, but above it was Arthur’s face, stripped of comfort and twenty years of safety.

“Silas Pemberton,” I said.

His eyes moved to the photograph.

“He kept that?”

“I found it here.”

“Yes.” His mouth twitched. “Of course you did.”

The boiler gave a deep metallic knock.

“How long have you been down here?”

“Days. There are places in this house nobody remembers.”

“Arthur remembered.”

“Not soon enough.”

His right hand was hidden by his coat. I shifted towards the lamp, trying to put the shelf between us.

He watched the movement.

“Stay where you are, Mr Hale.”

“You know my name.”

“I heard him waiting for you.”

My hand moved beneath my jacket.

He was on me before I touched the gun.

Something hard struck the side of my head. The lamp lurched in its pool of light, though I could no longer tell whether it had moved or I had. My knees hit concrete.

As I fell, I saw the open iron door behind him.

Arthur had bolted his door against the corridor. His brother had never needed it.

Then the floor rose against my cheek, and the furnace light went out.

## Chapter 9: The Twin

I woke to a knocking that seemed to come from inside my skull. Then a valve hissed above me, and I understood that some of the noise belonged to the boiler.

My wrists were tied behind a vertical pipe. Coal grit pressed through my trousers. Across the room, Silas Pemberton stood before the open furnace, his face red in its glow.

He held the knife in a fold of oilcloth.

“Steel doesn’t burn,” I said.

He looked over his shoulder. “Handles do.”

He pushed the blade into the coals with a poker. The wood caught almost at once. Then he fed in the oilcloth, a corner at a time.

My right wrist was wet. When I moved it, the cord bit deeper.

“Where’s my gun?”

“Beyond your reach.”

“So was your brother. Until tonight.”

The poker stopped.

I had no idea how long I had been unconscious, or whether the manager had delivered my note. Above us were floors of sleeping rooms, a blizzard, and people who had excellent reasons not to help me.

Silas shut the furnace door.

“Arthur was never beyond my reach,” he said. “That was what he couldn’t bear.”

“Twenty years is a long time to wait.”

“You think I spent it standing outside his window?”

He laid the poker across his knees and sat on an upturned coal box. In the lamplight the resemblance was worse. Beneath the beard and privation was the face I had seen against the hearth upstairs.

“Who pulled you out?” I asked.

“A fisherman. Miles down the coast. I had a broken shoulder and enough seawater in me to kill a horse.”

“And Arthur thought you drowned.”

“Arthur watched me drowning.”

He said it without emphasis.

“The boat overturned. There was a line hanging over the stern. I caught it. Arthur was on the rocks by then. I called to him.”

The boiler ticked behind my head.

“He pulled the line away.”

“You saw him?”

“I saw his hands. I saw his face. We were twenty-three, Mr. Hale. Our father had been buried six weeks. Half of everything was mine.”

“Unless you died.”

“Unless I died.”

He rubbed a thumb along the poker.

“For months I couldn’t lift a cup properly. By the time I could travel, Arthur had inherited, mourned me, and begun explaining to people how he had nearly died trying to save me. I had nothing but my word against his.”

“So you stayed dead.”

“At first because I was afraid. Later because I was ashamed of being afraid.” His gaze moved towards the sleeping alcove. “A man can lose twenty years without deciding to lose any one of them.”

I pulled cautiously against the cord. No give.

“What brought you here?”

“I heard he was closing the old accounts. Selling things. This place was to go eventually. I wanted him here, where he couldn’t pretend he had forgotten me.”

“You wanted to frighten him.”

“Yes.”

“And then you wanted more.”

He looked at me for a long time.

“Then he offered me money.”

Somewhere beyond the iron door came a faint scrape. Silas raised his head. I coughed, and pain burst behind my eyes.

“Was that tonight?” I asked.

“This afternoon. He saw me outside his sitting room. I let him.”

“Then you went back through the wall.”

“We used those passages as boys. You could get from the kitchens to the east rooms without meeting a servant.”

“You waited in the recess while he locked the door.”

“He had taken his medicine. He checked the corridor, threw the bolt, and stood listening.” Silas’s mouth tightened. “Listening for me outside.”

“But you were already inside.”

“Yes.”

I pictured the narrow space, the disturbed dust, a man holding his breath behind the panelling.

“You crossed towards him. Between the lamp and the door.”

“He said I was dead.”

“Elias heard him.”

“Arthur always thought saying something firmly made it true.”

“And the cut on his arm?”

“He caught at the knife. I pulled it free.”

“Before you stabbed him?”

Silas nodded.

“Twice,” I said.

“He was still standing after the first.”

There was no triumph in his voice. That made it harder to hear.

“Then back through the wall.”

“I closed both panels.”

“And left his door bolted.”

“I never touched his door.”

A quiet metallic tap came from the passage. Silas stood.

“Pipes,” I said.

He did not believe me. He picked up the poker.

“Arthur sent for you. I heard him asking whether you had arrived. I ought to have gone then.”

“You can still put that down.”

“And let you take me upstairs?”

“Let me hear the rest.”

“You’ve heard it.”

“No. I’ve heard what he did to you. I haven’t heard why you brought the photograph.”

His eyes went towards the alcove.

“The two boys,” I said. “You kept that through all those years.”

“Be quiet.”

“You didn’t come here only to kill him. Some part of you expected your brother to be glad you were alive.”

His grip shifted. For a moment he looked bewildered rather than dangerous.

The iron door struck the wall.

“Drop it!” Calder shouted.

Silas swung towards him. Elias came low from the doorway and caught his arm; the poker struck brick with a crack that sent sparks across the floor. Calder seized Silas’s other wrist. The three of them staggered against the coal partition.

I could do nothing but watch.

Then the poker fell. Calder forced Silas face down across the coal box while Elias held him. Handcuffs clicked.

“Knife in the furnace,” I said. “Don’t let anyone rake it out bare-handed.”

Calder looked at me, breathing hard. “Your note took some finding. Manager thought I was still in the study.”

Elias cut my bonds. As my hands came free, the returning blood hurt almost as much as the cord.

They brought Silas beneath the lamp. He blinked in the light, suddenly an exhausted man in a dirty coat.

Calder glanced from him to the passage.

“I was wrong, Hale.”

“So was I. I found the hiding place and decided it led nowhere.”

Above us, Arthur’s broken door still held its useless bolt. We had spent the night asking how a murderer could leave a sealed room.

Silas had waited inside it for his brother to lock him in.

## Chapter 10: Redemption

By morning the storm had spent itself. Snow lay against the boiler-room windows in white banks, and above it a strip of sky showed clear and painfully blue.

Elias brought clean towels for my wrists. Calder stood beside Silas while the engineer raked the furnace.

They recovered the blade with long tongs and laid it on an iron tray. Its wooden handle was gone. I had watched the oilcloth burn; whatever else the heat had destroyed would have to be established by someone better equipped than I was.

“Wrap it when it’s cold,” I said. “And keep the photograph dry.”

Silas watched the tray.

“Will they believe me about the boat?”

“I don’t know.”

“You believe I killed him.”

“You’ve given us evidence of that.”

He looked away. I would once have offered him certainty about everything, guilt or innocence, truth or lies. Now I had only distinctions, and the duty to preserve them.

By noon the telephone was working intermittently. Men came up the cleared track with tools, followed by two constables and a doctor. The doctor examined my head, called me fortunate in a tone usually reserved for fools, and ordered rest.

Instead, I showed the constables the passage.

In daylight, with lamps held at either end, it looked less like an impossibility than neglected workmanship. A catch, a hinge, brick slips fixed to timber. One constable measured the opening while another knelt over the disturbed dust.

The cold-air hatch opened onto an old service stair leading toward the outer boiler entrance. Snow had blown beneath its lower door. There were other ways through the house than those marked on the guests’ plan; each would now be examined.

I gave them the cufflink and showed them where I had found the cigarette.

“Those need owners,” I said. “Not guesses.”

Ruth’s forgery went into a separate envelope. She admitted she had entered the recess while searching for the will, then lied because she feared what we would think. Whether that accounted for everything was another question. Webb’s books were requested, his account of his movements recorded again. Celia acknowledged that the first scream had been hers: she had heard the fall, opened her door, and fled back into her room without trying Arthur’s handle.

There was shame in her account, but shame was not evidence of murder.

Calder took her statement without interruption.

When the repaired funicular was ready, I stood with him beneath the entrance canopy. Silas waited between the constables, wearing the wet coat we had found below. It had dried stiffly at the hem.

“Webb asked me to keep the financial business quiet,” Calder said.

“And you agreed?”

“I told him we were investigating a death.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

He rubbed a thumb along the edge of his notebook.

“No,” he said. “It wasn’t.”

The car descended slowly, its cable singing in the cold. Silas did not look back. I watched until the roof vanished below the platform.

Then I went upstairs.

Arthur’s suite had been left open. Pale sunlight lay across the rug, stopping short of the darkened patch beside the hearth. The broken door rested against the wall. Beyond the wainscoting, the exposed passage showed plainly enough to make me ashamed.

I had found the recess and decided it led nowhere.

That decision had nearly killed me.

The bolt had been real. So had the sealed windows and the narrow chimney. My error was not believing what I saw; it was believing that I had seen everything. I had made the room complete in my mind, then resisted anything that would alter it.

I knew that habit. An innocent man was still living behind a locked door because I had once exercised it with greater authority.

At the writing desk I found hotel stationery.

The first letter was addressed to Arthur.

I wrote that I had received his warnings and dismissed them. That his money had brought me where his fear should have brought me sooner. I did not ask forgiveness. There was nobody left who could give it.

I folded the sheet and put it beside his undelivered letter.

The second took longer.

I addressed it to the clerk of the court that had convicted the man whose denials I had taught myself not to hear. I identified the case, my testimony, and the conclusions I could no longer defend. I requested the record and a review of the evidence. Then I offered to make a sworn statement.

It would not open his cell that morning. It might cost me what little standing I retained. Neither fact excused another day’s silence.

Calder found me sealing the envelope.

“Another case?” he asked.

“An old one.”

He read the address and said nothing.

I handed it to him for the outgoing post, then turned back once toward the hearth.

There had never been a missing key. There had been a man already inside, and a way out I had refused to look for.

I left the room with the door open.
