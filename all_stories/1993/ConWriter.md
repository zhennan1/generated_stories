The first time I heard Adrian Vale die, I was standing outside his study with a cup of coffee going cold in my hand.

There was a crash, a scrape of furniture, and then his voice, unmistakable even through the oak.

“Put that down.”

A pistol shot cracked through the corridor.

I dropped the cup and tried the handle. Locked. At the far end of the passage, Vale’s daughter turned so sharply that her shoulder struck a picture frame. Behind her, the housekeeper stood motionless with an armful of folded linen.

“Father?” Clara called.

I put my shoulder against the door. It held.

“Get someone strong,” I told her. “And call the police.”

She ran toward the entrance hall. The housekeeper let the linen fall.

“The telephone’s there,” she said, pointing to an alcove behind me.

“Then use it.”

I had arrived at Greyhaven less than an hour earlier, hired to investigate threatening letters. Vale’s secretary had shown me into a drawing room, where Clara offered coffee and her father’s business partner, Bell, asked whether I was really necessary. Before I could answer, Vale appeared in the doorway.

“Wait here for now, detective.”

Those were the only words he had spoken directly to me. Now I was bracing my foot against his study door, listening for something beyond it.

Nothing moved.

Bell came running with Clara behind him. He was broad across the chest, still wearing the dinner jacket in which he had greeted me.

“Stand clear,” he said.

“We go together.”

The first impact shook dust from the frame. The second split the timber near the lock. On the third, the upper part of the door sprang inward, but something held it lower down.

Through the opening I saw a brass bolt, still extended into its socket.

“Again.”

The socket tore loose. We stumbled into a room smelling of burned powder and hot electrical insulation.

Vale sat behind his desk, his head tipped back against the leather chair. Blood had soaked his shirt below the breastbone. A pistol lay on the carpet near his right shoe.

I went to him while Bell stopped just inside the doorway. There was no pulse that I could find. I left his collar as I had found it and stepped back.

“Don’t touch anything.”

Clara tried to follow us in. I intercepted her before she could see past my shoulder.

“Stay with the housekeeper.”

“Is he alive?”

“I’m sorry.”

She gripped my wrist hard enough to hurt, then released it. I watched her walk back into the corridor, one hand sliding along the wall.

Only then did I examine the room.

Two tall windows overlooked the flooded garden. Both catches were shut. Rain trembled against the glass; beyond it, a sheer wall dropped to paving two floors below. There was one door, now broken, and no adjoining room that I could see. The fireplace held a fitted stove, its iron door closed and its narrow flue vanishing into masonry.

The key was in the study lock, on the inside.

Bell stared at the ruined bolt.

“He locked us out.”

“He locked the door,” I said. “We don’t yet know when.”

“We heard him.”

“Yes.”

The distinction made him look at me.

I asked him to wait outside and keep everyone away from the threshold. He hesitated, glancing once toward the pistol, then obeyed. From the corridor came the housekeeper’s low voice giving the address over the telephone.

I took out my notebook. Before private work, I had spent fourteen years learning how easily a room could be spoiled by people trying to help. I sketched the door, the desk, the chair, the pistol. Beside the gun lay a small curved piece of black material. I left it where it was.

A green-shaded lamp illuminated an empty blotter. There were no threatening letters. There was, however, a portable dictating machine on a side table, its reels turning.

The take-up reel revolved steadily. Loose tape flicked against its casing.

I leaned closer without touching it.

“Did your employer record his correspondence?” I asked.

The housekeeper appeared beyond the broken door. “Every evening.”

“What’s your name?”

“Mrs. Mercer.”

“Was he recording tonight?”

“I don’t know.”

A thin electrical hum came from the machine. Beneath the side table lay the shattered remains of a small loudspeaker. Its cable still ran up to the recorder.

I crouched.

One edge of the speaker casing bore a fresh gouge. Across the room, in the plaster beneath a framed map, was a small, dark hole.

I looked from that hole to the ruined speaker, then to Vale’s wound. They did not form one straight path.

“Mr. Bell,” I said, “when you heard the shot, where were you?”

“In the drawing room.”

“And before it—the crash, his voice?”

“Yes. What does that matter?”

I stood carefully, keeping clear of the cartridge case I had just noticed beneath the table.

“It matters because we may have heard a recording.”

Behind him, Clara lifted her face.

I looked again at the locked windows and the bolt hanging from the splintered frame. The room had seemed to give us a precise moment of death.

Now I had to establish whether Vale had been alive when I reached his door.


I put a question mark beside the dictating machine in my notebook. A machine capable of reproducing a voice was not proof that it had done so. Nor did a locked door prove that the man inside had locked it himself. I had seen investigations go wrong because someone mistook the first plausible explanation for a fact.

“Did you understand the words?” I asked Bell.

“Of course. ‘Put that down.’”

“Were those the words you remembered before I suggested a recording?”

His mouth tightened. “You heard them yourself.”

“I did. I want your recollection, not mine.”

Clara stood behind him, her arms folded tightly against her ribs. Mrs. Mercer had gathered up the fallen linen and held it against her apron. None of them looked at the body now. They looked at me, as though my next question might make what had happened less permanent.

I noted the positions of the recorder’s switches and the tape hanging from its reel. Then I bent close enough to read the lettering beside the controls. I could have stopped it with one finger. I kept my hands at my sides. Until I understood the machine, changing its condition might destroy more than letting it run.

“Has anyone touched that since we entered?”

Bell shook his head.

“Not even to turn it down?”

“No.”

I wrote his answer beneath my sketch. The electrical smell remained strongest near the side table, but I could not tell whether it came from the machine or the broken speaker. I wanted a torch, someone who knew the apparatus, and a doctor. Instead, I had rain at the windows and three witnesses close enough to hear one another’s answers.

I stepped around the splintered socket and went out into the corridor, taking my notebook with me. My shoe caught the edge of the fallen doorframe. I stopped, freed it carefully, and marked where the timber lay before moving on. Bell shifted to let me pass.

“Keep that entrance clear,” I told him. “No one goes in. Not even if the telephone rings.”

“You’re leaving it?”

“I’m going as far as the alcove.”

The coffee I had dropped was spread across the boards in a brown crescent. A piece of white china lay beneath the opposite skirting. I skirted the spill and stopped where I remembered standing before the crash. From here, through the broken doorway, I could see only a narrow strip of carpet and the corner of the desk.

I asked Mrs. Mercer to stand where she had been with the linen.

“Here?” She moved several paces back.

“Where you remember. Don’t choose a place to agree with me.”

She looked along the passage, then took one step toward the telephone alcove. “Here. I was coming from the stairs.”

“And you, Miss Vale?”

Clara returned to the picture she had struck. Its frame still hung crookedly.

I drew a rough plan of the corridor. Doors, alcove, stairs, drawing room. Beside each witness I left room for separate answers. Bell watched my pencil.

“What about me?”

“You said you were in the drawing room. Were you standing or sitting?”

“Standing.”

“Near the door?”

“Near the fireplace.”

“Was the door open?”

“Yes.”

I asked him to show me. We walked down the corridor together, leaving Clara and Mrs. Mercer in sight of the study. At the drawing-room entrance I stopped him before he crossed the carpet.

“Don’t tell me what you think happened. Tell me what reached you.”

“The crash. His voice. The shot.”

“Anything between them?”

“A scraping sound, perhaps.”

“Perhaps?”

He rubbed his jaw. “There was a scrape. I can’t say whether it was before the words or after.”

I put down exactly that. It was a smaller answer than I wanted, and more useful than certainty borrowed from my account.

Bell went to the fireplace. I remained at the doorway and spoke at an ordinary volume.

“Can you hear me clearly?”

“Yes.”

I lowered my voice. He leaned forward.

“The words?” I asked.

“Not all of them.”

It proved little with the study door broken, and I said so before he could turn the exercise into an objection. Still, the distance mattered. Later, with the police present, we could test what carried from the study and what did not. For now I had fixed where he claimed to have been.

As we returned along the passage, Clara moved toward us.

“You think we imagined his voice.”

“No. I heard it too.”

“Then why all this?”

“Because hearing a voice and seeing someone speak are different things.”

She glanced past me at the ruined door. I could not soften the distinction without lying to her.

I brought Bell back to the threshold and asked Mrs. Mercer to wait beside the telephone. Then I turned to Clara, opening a fresh page where the others could not read it.

“Start before the crash,” I said. “Tell me the last thing you actually saw.”


Clara kept her eyes on the broken study door.

“I was coming from the stairs,” she said. “I had gone up because I thought I heard my father calling me.”

“What did you hear?”

“Not words. I thought—” She stopped and pressed her thumb against the side of her hand. “No. That isn’t fair. I thought I heard movement. A chair, perhaps.”

“Before the crash?”

“Yes.”

“Where were you when you heard it?”

“By the picture.”

“Which picture?”

“The ship.” She glanced at the frame hanging crookedly behind her. “I had just reached it.”

“Did you see anyone between the stairs and that picture?”

Her gaze moved, briefly, toward Bell. He stood at the drawing-room entrance, broad shoulders blocking part of the light from that room.

“No.”

“Did you see the study door?”

“It was shut.”

“Locked?”

“I couldn’t tell.”

“Was the bolt visible?”

“The door was closed. I didn’t go close enough to see.”

I wrote her answer down, then looked at the corridor. The door had opened inward when Bell and I broke it. The bolt socket had been torn from the frame, but its brass tongue had remained extended until the impact. If Clara had seen the door from the picture, she might have seen nothing but dark wood and the strip of carpet beneath it.

“What happened next?”

“There was a scraping sound.”

“Describe it.”

“Metal against something. Or wood.” She shut her eyes for a moment, then opened them. “It came from the study.”

“Before or after your father spoke?”

“After the movement. Before the crash.”

“What crash?”

“The first one. Something heavy falling.”

I felt the scene rearrange itself by inches. Bell had placed the scraping uncertainly around Vale’s words. Clara put it earlier, before the crash and before the voice. Neither account fitted neatly with the order I had heard.

“And then?”

“His voice said, ‘Put that down.’”

“Did it sound close to the door?”

She hesitated. “It sounded as if he were angry.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“No. It sounded farther away than it should have.”

Bell shifted at the end of the corridor. Clara heard him and looked toward him.

“I didn’t say that before because I thought I was mistaken,” she said. “The door was between us, but his voice seemed to come from the room’s far side. Near the windows.”

That detail tightened something in my mind. The dictating machine sat near the side table, closer to the door than the desk and windows. A speaker might throw a voice anywhere, if it had been placed for that purpose. But Clara had not seen the machine, and I had not told her about it.

“Did you hear your father move after he spoke?”

“No.”

“Any footsteps?”

“No.”

“Then the shot?”

She nodded. Her face had gone pale, but her voice remained controlled. “The shot came almost at once.”

“And after it?”

“The silence. Then you struck the door.”

I turned to the study. “You are certain the scraping came before the voice?”

“I’m certain that is how I remember it.”

“That is different from being certain it happened that way.”

Her mouth tightened. “You keep saying that.”

“Because memory changes when people compare it. Bell heard a scrape, but he doesn’t know where it belongs. You heard one and placed it before the voice. Mrs. Mercer may have another version. I need all three before any of you borrow certainty from the others.”

Mrs. Mercer, still beside the telephone alcove, raised a hand slightly. “I heard it after.”

The words settled over the corridor.

“After the shot?” I asked.

“After the voice. Before the shot, I think.” She looked unhappy with the qualification. “It was a small sound. Like someone dragging a box.”

That made three uncertain witnesses and one locked room. I closed the notebook.

“Miss Vale, when you reached the picture, could you see under the study door?”

“A little.”

“Was there light?”

“Yes.”

“Steady or moving?”

“Steady. A line of light under the door.”

“Did anything interrupt it?”

She frowned. “For a second.”

“What interrupted it?”

“I don’t know. A shadow, perhaps.”

“Did it cross the entire gap?”

“No. Just a dark spot near the hinge.”

I glanced at the threshold. The fallen door had gouged the floor where it opened, and the remaining frame was thick enough to hide a hand or an object held low. A shadow near the hinge could have been a foot, a length of wire, or nothing more than the door settling against its bolt.

“Did you see the door move?”

“No.”

“Did you see anyone’s feet?”

“No.”

“Did you see the light go out?”

“Not before the crash.”

I made a final note. “Thank you. Stay here with Mrs. Mercer. Do not discuss the order of what you heard.”

Clara looked toward the study. “What are you going to do?”

“Test the room without disturbing it.”

Bell gave a short, humorless laugh. “How?”

“From the place you claim to have stood.”

I walked to the drawing-room entrance, and this time I crossed the threshold openly, stating what I was doing so no one mistook the movement for an attempt to leave the investigation. The room smelled of cold ash and damp wool. A lamp burned beside an empty chair; rain ticked against the tall windows at the far end.

Bell moved aside.

“Stand by the fireplace,” I told him. “Face me.”

He obeyed. I remained at the doorway and spoke in the same ordinary tone Clara had heard through the closed study door.

“Put that down.”

Bell heard the words, but not clearly. The final consonant vanished into the corridor.

I moved three paces nearer the fireplace and repeated them. This time he heard every word.

The difference gave me a measurement, not an answer. A live voice from the desk should have carried differently from a speaker near the side table. I turned back toward the corridor.

“Tomorrow—” I stopped, correcting myself before inventing a schedule. “When the police arrive, we’ll test the recorder before anyone rewinds it. Until then, Bell stays out of the study, Mrs. Mercer keeps the telephone clear, and no one touches the door, the bolt, or the machine.”

A faint click came from behind us.

We all turned.

From the study, the dictating machine spoke in Adrian Vale’s voice.

“Put that down.”


The words came again, identical in pitch and pause, but not in the way a living man repeats himself. Adrian Vale’s voice emerged from the study with a faint mechanical grain beneath it, as though each syllable had been dragged over a rough edge. The final consonant trembled, then vanished into a soft hiss.

No one moved.

Bell’s hand rose halfway toward the study opening and stopped when he saw my expression. Clara had gone rigid beside the picture. Mrs. Mercer gripped the telephone cord so tightly that the white skin showed across her knuckles.

“Stay where you are,” I said.

The machine clicked inside the study. I could hear the tape reel turning, though the broken doorway gave me only a partial view of the side table. The sound lasted several seconds, then stopped. No one had crossed the threshold. No one had touched the bolt or the recorder. I had watched all three witnesses, and the narrow strip of corridor between us and the room remained clear.

Bell swallowed. “It answered you.”

“No.” I kept my eyes on the study. “It played after me.”

“That is the same thing.”

“It isn’t.” I spoke quietly, forcing myself to separate what I had observed from what the timing suggested. “I said the phrase. Then there was a pause. Then the machine produced the phrase. That makes the sequence a fact. It does not tell us why it began.”

Clara stared at the dark opening. “It was already running.”

“Did you hear the machine before the first time?”

“No.” She drew a breath. “I heard your voice, then the click.”

“Your recollection,” I said, “is of what you heard before we knew the machine could speak. Do not change it to fit what has just happened.”

Her eyes flashed at me, frightened and angry. “I’m not changing anything.”

“I know. That is why I’m asking you to keep the two things apart.”

Mrs. Mercer looked toward the telephone alcove. “I heard a click before the shot.”

“Before the shot, or before the voice?”

She opened her mouth, then closed it. “I don’t know.”

“Good,” I said. “Leave it there until you do.”

The words felt cruel, but certainty was becoming a weapon in that corridor. Every confident answer gave the locked room another wall. I took out my notebook without stepping closer to the study and wrote: *machine playback heard after my repetition; no visible person at entrance; witnesses stationary*. Beneath it I added the exact phrase. I did not write that the machine had answered me. That was an interpretation, and interpretations had already made a tangle of the sounds.

Bell leaned toward the opening. “If the tape is running, shouldn’t it keep going?”

“It may have reached a recorded section.”

“Then someone set it to play.”

“Possibly.”

“Vale?”

The name struck the corridor harder than the shot had. Adrian was dead inside the study; that fact had not altered because his voice came from a box. I looked at the dark opening and remembered Clara’s account: steady light, then a brief interruption near the hinge. If someone had reached the machine from inside, the locked room became a problem of access. If the machine had been started before the shooting, the voice might have been part of the staging. If the playback had been triggered by some disturbance, we still had to find the trigger without disturbing the evidence.

I stepped one pace toward the corridor, not the study, and raised my hand to keep Bell from advancing. From that angle the lamp’s reflection showed on the polished edge of the fallen frame. The strip of light across the floor remained unbroken now. I watched it for several breaths. Nothing crossed it. No foot, wire, or hand appeared at the threshold.

“Bell,” I said, “describe what you heard just now. Only what you heard.”

He frowned. “Your words outside. Then a click. Then Vale saying the same words.”

“Was his voice close?”

“No. Farther in. Like before.”

“Did it sound alive?”

Bell looked ashamed of the question. “It sounded like him.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

He glanced at the machine, then back at me. “No. Not quite.”

Clara whispered, “There was a hiss.”

“Yes,” I said. “I heard it.”

“And the shot?” Mrs. Mercer asked.

The question exposed the danger in all of us. We were trying to make the new sound explain the old ones. I closed the notebook.

“We do not replay anything. We do not rewind anything. We do not enter the study. We preserve the machine exactly as it is until it can be examined properly.”

A faint scrape came from inside the study.

This time it was unmistakable: metal drawn slowly across wood.

Clara made a small sound. Bell’s shoulders tightened. I held my breath and listened. The scrape stopped, followed by three measured clicks from the dictating machine.

Then Adrian Vale’s voice said, much more softly, “Don’t let him in.”


Bell took one step before I caught his sleeve.

“Not another.”

“He said not to let him in.”

“The recording said it.” I released him when he stopped. “We don’t know when Adrian Vale spoke those words, or to whom.”

Behind the broken doorway, the hiss dwindled. I waited for another click, another scrape, anything that might give the warning a boundary. There was nothing. Rain tapped the drawing-room windows, and Mrs. Mercer’s breathing sounded painfully loud beside the telephone alcove.

I had meant to compare distances and voices. The scrape had changed what I could safely attempt. An acoustic test required people moving and speaking; now either might conceal the next sound from the study. I put my notebook away.

“No more tests. We move back, keep the entrance clear, and write separately.”

Clara looked at my hand, still raised between Bell and the room. “What if someone’s in there?”

It was the question I had avoided asking aloud. From the corridor I could see only part of the study; beyond that narrow view, furniture and darkness left room for almost anything.

“Then crowding this opening gives them an advantage,” I said. “Back toward the drawing-room. Slowly. Nobody turns their back on the study until we’re past the picture.”

I moved first, sideways along the corridor wall, keeping well clear of the splintered wood. Clara followed. Bell retreated reluctantly, his shoes dragging on the carpet. Mrs. Mercer remained by the alcove until I motioned her farther away from the study, then joined us. Each step lengthened the empty stretch before the doorway. Nothing crossed it.

At the drawing-room entrance I stopped Bell on the corridor side.

“Stand here. You can see the opening without blocking it. Don’t lean against the frame. If you hear anything, call me. If you see movement, step back and call me. You do not go in.”

“You’re leaving me to watch it?”

“I’ll be just inside this room, with the entrance clear.”

He looked toward the study, then placed his feet squarely beneath him. I waited until he nodded before crossing into the drawing-room. From the lamp table I could still see his profile and, beyond him, the pale splinters at the study entrance.

The room felt offensively ordinary. There were coasters beside the lamp, a folded newspaper on a chair, and three envelopes on a writing desk. I found blank paper in its drawer and took a pencil from the tray. My fingers were stiff enough that I dropped it. The small wooden clatter made Clara flinch.

“Sit where you cannot read each other’s pages,” I said.

Mrs. Mercer took the chair by the window. Clara remained standing beside the desk.

“I’ve already told you everything.”

“I’ve written down what you told me. This is different. Write what you remember from before we began asking questions. If something only occurred to you after a question, put it separately.”

“You think I imagined the shadow.”

“I think I asked about the light, and then you described an interruption. I need to preserve both facts.”

She looked down at the paper. “And if I can’t tell which came first?”

“Write that.”

I divided a sheet for Bell with a pencil line and carried it back to the doorway.

“Not while you’re watching,” I told him. “I’ll take your place when they’ve finished.”

He accepted the sheet without looking away from the corridor. His knuckles had lost their color.

Inside the drawing-room, I drew the same line on my own page. On one side I recorded the scrape, three clicks, and the exact warning. On the other I put the possibilities that kept pressing against those facts: a moving part, an object shifting, a person beyond our sight. None had earned the right to become an observation.

“Was the scrape the same as before?” Clara asked.

I kept my pencil still. “Don’t compare them yet. Describe each separately.”

She pressed her lips together, then began writing.

Mrs. Mercer’s pencil moved in short strokes. Once she rubbed out a word so vigorously that the paper buckled. I resisted asking what it had been. Questions could wait until she had something that belonged to her alone.

From the doorway Bell said, “Nothing.”

“Keep watching.”

I returned to him and stood on the opposite side of the drawing-room entrance. The study remained untouched. We had gained distance, not safety; I could feel that distinction in the cold sweat beneath my collar. Whatever had produced the scrape had done so while all of us were outside.

Behind me, Clara set down her pencil.

“I’ve finished.”

I collected her page face down, then Mrs. Mercer’s, without reading either aloud. I marked which was which and slipped them between clean leaves of my notebook.

“Bell. Your turn. Stay inside where I can see you.”

He passed me, taking care not to brush my shoulder. I assumed his place, feet planted well back from the wreckage, and watched the empty corridor while his pencil began to scratch.

For the first time since the warning, nobody was speaking. If the machine started again, there would be no human voice to mistake for its trigger.


For several moments the silence held. Bell’s pencil scratched behind me, stopped, then scratched again. I kept my attention on the study entrance, where the pale strip of light lay across the carpet. The broken frame cast a crooked shadow beside it, but the shadow did not move. Beyond the threshold, the recorder remained hidden from my angle. I could see only the edge of the table and a dull gleam that might have been its metal casing.

A soft click came from inside.

Bell’s pencil halted.

“Don’t speak,” I said.

The light across the floor flickered—not dimmed gradually, but vanished in a narrow interruption near the hinge. The darkness lasted less than a second. Nothing crossed the corridor. No hand appeared, no foot disturbed the carpet, and no object slid into view. Then the light returned, steady as before.

Bell came to the doorway, stopping at the place I had marked with my earlier warning. “Did you see that?”

“I saw the light interrupted.”

“By what?”

“That is what we’re determining.”

The recorder clicked twice more. This time there was no voice. I lifted my hand, holding Bell where he stood, and listened for the scrape that had followed the previous sequence. Instead I heard a faint ticking from somewhere inside the study, irregular and delicate, like a cooling mechanism. It continued for three beats, stopped, and left the corridor silent again.

I glanced back into the drawing-room. Clara stood beside the writing desk, her page folded beneath her palm. Mrs. Mercer remained by the window, watching me rather than the study. Their written recollections were face down between the leaves of my notebook, but I could already feel their importance. Separate memories might contradict one another in details; if they agreed on the position of the interruption, that agreement would matter more than any explanation either woman had offered aloud.

“Bell,” I said, “finish the sentence you were writing.”

He looked down at the page in his hand. “I was noting the clicks.”

“Then note the light. Exact order.”

He bent over the paper, though his eyes kept lifting toward the corridor. I waited until the pencil moved again. Only then did I step backward into the drawing-room, placing the entrance fully in my line of sight. The movement was deliberate: heel over heel, no turn, no contact with the wall. Once I reached the writing desk, I set my notebook beside the other pages and opened it to a clean sheet.

I wrote: *After Bell’s writing began: one click, interruption of light near hinge, two clicks, no voice, irregular ticking. No visible crossing.*

The sentence looked thin against the fear gathering around it. Still, it was something that could be tested. The timing suggested a connection between the recorder and the interruption, but suggestion was not proof. A mechanism might be reacting to vibration. A concealed line might be shifting. Or someone might be using the darkness at the hinge to move an object too small to see from our position. Each possibility required a different examination, and all of them required the study to remain untouched until we understood more.

Clara lifted her head. “Was it another warning?”

“No voice this time.”

“Then what does it mean?”

“It means the machine can make more than one sound.”

Her expression tightened. “You always say that as if it makes things better.”

“It makes them narrower.” I turned her page face down again when her fingers began to edge toward it. “We know what happened. We don’t yet know why.”

Mrs. Mercer rose from the window chair. “Should I telephone for the police?”

The question had been waiting in the room since the first shot. I looked toward the alcove, where the telephone cord hung in a loose loop, and then back to the study. Calling would bring other people into the house, along with noise, questions, and the pressure to open the locked room before the evidence had settled. But leaving the matter entirely in our hands carried its own danger.

“Not yet,” I said. “The line may be part of the arrangement, and I won’t have anyone crossing this corridor to use it.”

Her face paled. “You think the telephone is involved?”

“I think we don’t know what is involved.”

Bell returned with his page. He held it out without stepping beyond the boundary I had given him. “I wrote everything in order.”

I took the paper and read it beside the desk. His recollection was blunt: *click; light gone at hinge; light back; two clicks; ticking.* No interpretation, no claim that a person had entered. It matched my observation closely enough to trouble me.

“Did you see the light go out before or after the first click?” I asked.

“After. I’m sure.”

“Because you heard it?”

“Because I was looking at the doorway when it happened.”

I nodded and added that qualification to my notes. Then, from inside the study, the recorder gave one clean click.

All four of us looked toward the corridor.

A narrow darkness touched the hinge-side strip of light and withdrew.

This time, beneath the click, I heard something else: a faint metallic tap against the floor, followed by a whisper of movement from inside the study. I gripped the edge of the desk and kept my feet planted.

“Bell,” I said, “watch the threshold.”

“I am.”

“No. Watch the light. Whatever crosses it may not be large enough to look like a person.”


I kept my eyes on the hinge-side strip of light. “If something crosses it, don’t call out until you’ve seen where it goes.”

Bell swallowed. “How long am I supposed to wait?”

“Until I tell you.”

The recorder remained silent. That silence was worse than the clicks. It gave the study back its ordinary shape: a dead man beyond a locked door, a desk, shelves, the dark outline of furniture. Yet the last metallic tap had not belonged to any ordinary settling of the house. I had heard it too close to the floor, too deliberate in its timing.

I looked down at my notes. The entries had begun to form a pattern without yet becoming an explanation. Click. Interruption. Clicks. Ticking. Tap. Movement. The light was not failing throughout the corridor; only the narrow wedge beside the hinge disappeared. That meant the source of the interruption was either close to the doorway or close to whatever cast the light into it. I could not learn which by staring at the darkness.

“Mrs. Mercer,” I said, “where does the telephone wire run after it leaves the alcove?”

She glanced toward the instrument. “Behind the paneling, I believe. It was fitted before I came here.”

“Behind the paneling all the way to the study?”

“I don’t know.”

“Don’t guess.”

Her mouth tightened. “Then I don’t know.”

That answer helped more than confidence would have. I turned to the cord. The telephone stood on a small table in the alcove, its black receiver resting in its cradle. The cord descended behind the table and vanished through a narrow hole in the woodwork. I had noticed that arrangement earlier, but only as part of the room. Now the cord seemed to point toward every possibility at once: communication, concealment, or nothing more sinister than old household wiring.

“I’m going to examine the alcove,” I said. “I’ll remain on this side of the drawing-room. Bell, you keep the entrance in view. If the light changes, say only ‘light.’ No one moves toward the study.”

Bell nodded.

I crossed the room in a slow diagonal, making sure my shoes stayed on the carpet and that my body never blocked his view of the corridor.
