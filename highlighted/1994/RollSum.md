## Chapter 1: The Gilded Cage

The north wind worried at Thornfield Abbey from dawn until dusk, finding every crack in its ancient walls. It stirred the curtains around Lady Elara’s bed, rattled the windows of the long gallery, and carried the smell of salt into rooms that had never seen the sea.

Beyond the walls lay a world moving freely beneath the sky. Elara knew it chiefly from books.

“Elara.”

Her father sat at the far end of the breakfast table beneath a portrait of his grandfather, who had owned three estates and died without owing a grocer a penny. The present Lord Ashworth wore a coat shiny at the elbows. Beside his untouched breakfast lay a letter bearing a dark red seal.

“Viscount Marlowe has written,” he said.

Elara lowered her cup. “Does he wish to borrow something?”

Her father ignored this. “He will arrive next Thursday. He has expressed his admiration for you in terms any sensible woman would find flattering.”

“Then I am sorry to disappoint him.”

“This is not a jest.”

“No,” she said. “I imagine you have discussed it quite seriously.”

He folded the letter with unnecessary care. “His proposal deserves your consideration.”

Outside, rain blurred the windows. Elara remembered Marlowe at a winter assembly: his faultless gloves, his cool appraisal of her face, the sharpness with which he had rebuked a footman for spilling wine.

“He has never asked what I think about anything.”

“He has asked to marry you.”

“Perhaps he supposes there is no difference.”

Lord Ashworth pushed back his chair. “Marlowe has money enough to settle our debts and put this house in order. You see the roof leaking. You hear the servants asking when they will be paid. Do you imagine these things mend themselves?”

“No. But must I mend them?”

For a moment, he looked older than she had ever seen him. Then his expression hardened.

“You are my daughter. You have obligations to this family.”

“And none to myself?”

“You will receive him courteously. You will consider what I have said.”

“And if I refuse?”

His gaze held hers. “You will give me your answer before he leaves.”

He turned toward the window, dismissing her.

Elara rose and curtsied with precision. She would not weep in front of him. He would only wait for her tears to pass and repeat himself.

In the corridor, generations of Ashworths watched from darkened canvases: men with swords, women with pearls, all secure in their places. Her mother’s portrait hung beside the stairs. Dead these sixteen years, she looked scarcely older than Elara, one painted hand resting on a closed book.

Elara had never wondered whether she had been happy. Now she could think of little else.

She took the servants’ staircase to the back entrance, snatched a wool cloak from its peg, and stepped into the rain.

Beyond the formal gardens, the estate had begun to surrender to itself. Gardeners had been dismissed as the debts grew; brambles threaded the walls, and nettles crowded the orchard path. Elara passed the ruined summerhouse and went downhill toward the cliffs.

Mud spattered her hem. Her gloves grew damp. She walked faster, grateful for the cold and the effort, for anything that demanded more of her than sitting still.

A sharp cry stopped her.

It came from the bracken beneath the lower wood. She pushed aside a wet branch and saw a falcon struggling on the ground. A wire snare held its leg. Each beat of its wings drew the loop tighter; blood stained the feathers above its foot.

“Oh, be still.”

She crouched, reaching instinctively. The falcon struck at her with its beak.

“I won’t hurt you.”

“It doesn’t know that.”

Elara turned.

A young man stood behind her with a bundle of cut branches under one arm. His dark coat was worn at the cuffs, his boots thick with mud. Rain had flattened his black hair against his forehead. Beneath it, gray eyes moved from her hand to the trapped bird.

“Stand back a little,” he said.

She was unused to being addressed so plainly. Yet she moved.

He set down the branches and knelt. The falcon thrashed again. He waited, speaking softly, then drew a cloth from his pocket and laid it over the bird’s head.

“Will that quiet it?” Elara asked.

“It should.”

He held the wings against its body while he examined the snare. His hands were rough and scratched, but nothing in their movement was hurried. With a small knife he eased the wire away from the swollen leg and cut it.

Elara found she had been holding her breath.

“Will it live?”

“If the wound stays clean.”

“Can you tend it?”

“I can try.”

He wrapped the falcon securely in the cloth. A talon caught his thumb; he loosened it without flinching.

“Who set the snare?” she asked.

“Someone after rabbits, likely. They don’t always catch what they’re meant to.”

She looked at the severed wire in the mud, ashamed that she knew so little of what happened on land that bore her name.

“You are the groundskeeper?”

“Rhys.”

He offered no surname.

“Elara Ashworth.”

“I know, my lady.”

The title restored a distance she had briefly forgotten. She watched him tuck the bird against his coat.

“Where will you take it?”

“The old mews.”

“I thought they were falling down.”

“The west room is dry.”

She had passed the building a hundred times without noticing that anyone might still use it.

“May I see it?” she asked.

He glanced at her, and she could not tell whether he meant to refuse.

“Let it settle first.”

Then he rose, gathering the branches beneath his free arm.

“You ought to return,” he said. “There’s worse weather coming.”

Beyond the trees, a dark bank of cloud was swallowing the sea.

“And you?”

“I haven’t far to go.”

He gave a brief nod and walked into the wood.

Elara remained beside the broken snare until his footsteps faded. He had not admired her dress, asked after her father, or considered what advantage might come from knowing her. His attention had belonged to an injured bird; when she had asked questions, he had answered them.

Rain gathered on her lashes. She should return before her absence was noticed.

Instead, she looked once toward the hidden mews, remembering the steadiness of his hands and those gray, untroubled eyes. She pictured the falcon waking in a dry room, its leg no longer bound.

For the first time that morning, the future contained something she wanted to see.

She wondered what it might feel like to be free.

## Chapter 2: The Groundskeeper's Secret

By morning, the rain had left the lawns shining and filled the cracks in the terrace with dark water. Elara stood at her window while her maid fastened the buttons of her dress, watching the lower wood emerge from the mist.

“Your blue slippers, my lady?”

“The boots.”

“The paths will be dreadful.”

“Yes,” Elara said. “I expect they will.”

She told herself she meant only to discover whether the falcon had survived.

At the place where they had found it, the bracken still bore the marks of their kneeling. The wire was gone. Elara searched the wet leaves, absurdly disappointed, before remembering that Rhys had told her exactly where he meant to take the bird.

She had come the longer way.

The old mews stood beyond the kitchen garden, half hidden by elder trees. One end had lost its roof; through the empty rafters a rowan had grown, lifting scarlet berries where birds had once perched. At the western door, Elara hesitated.

Within, something scraped against wood.

“You may come in,” Rhys said.

He was seated beside a low table, the falcon held securely against his leather apron. Daylight from a narrow window fell across its barred breast. A strip of linen lay between his fingers.

“Is it worse?” she asked.

“No. Angry, which is better.”

As if to demonstrate, the bird opened its hooked beak. Rhys shifted his hand, avoiding the strike without tightening his hold.

Elara watched him unwind the dressing. The wound looked smaller than she remembered, though the sight of the bruised flesh made her stomach contract.

“You needn’t watch.”

“I should like to know.”

He glanced at her, then returned to his work.

“The wire cut deep, but nothing’s broken. If the wound stays clean, there’s a fair chance.”

“Only fair?”

“It wouldn’t be kindness to promise you more.”

She thought of the physicians who had attended her mother, remembered less as faces than as soft voices outside a closed door. Everyone had promised then.

Rhys rinsed the wound with water from a small earthenware bowl. Beside it stood a jar holding green leaves.

“What are those?”

“Plantain. For a poultice.”

“It grows in the gravel by the stables.”

“And most other places people tread.”

“I’ve always been told it was a weed.”

“It is.”

There was no mockery in his answer, which somehow made her smile.

He settled the falcon in a wooden enclosure screened with sacking. At once its restless movement quieted.

“Does it like the dark?”

“It likes not seeing something it can’t escape.”

Elara looked away.

The room smelled of damp stone, feathers, and the bitter freshness of crushed leaves. A narrow pallet lay beneath the window. On a shelf above it were a whetstone, several jars, and a book swollen by moisture.

“Do you sleep here?”

“Only while she needs watching.”

“You’ll make yourself ill.”

“I’ve slept in worse places.”

He spoke without emphasis. Elara could think of nothing to say that would not make the remark heavier.

Instead she asked, “Who taught you to care for birds?”

“Gideon Vale. He kept the grounds before me.”

She remembered an old man bent almost double, carrying dead branches past the drawing-room windows.

“I thought he only tended the paths.”

“He did that too.”

“Where is he now?”

“In the churchyard.”

“I’m sorry.”

Rhys folded the stained linen.

“He had a long life.”

“Was he a relation?”

“No. He took me in.”

Outside, water dripped steadily from the broken gutter.

“Where were your parents?”

“My mother died when I was small. I never knew my father.” He put the linen aside. “Gideon found work for me before I was much use at it. Said a boy could learn not to trample seedlings, if nothing else.”

“And did you?”

“Eventually.”

His brief smile changed his face so completely that she felt she had been permitted a glimpse through an opened door.

“What else did he teach you?”

Rhys looked toward the window.

“When to cut willow. Where the ground gives way after frost. Which wells go bitter in a dry summer.”

“All useful accomplishments,” she said. “I was taught the rivers of Europe.”

“Those might be useful too.”

“Not here.”

“No,” he conceded. “Not much here.”

She laughed, and the falcon stirred behind the sacking. They both fell silent, listening, then exchanged a glance.

Through the window, a pale bank of cloud gathered over the sea.

“More rain?” she whispered.

“Not before evening.”

“How can you tell?”

“The wind’s backing. Hear it in the elder?”

She listened. Until then she had heard only a general stirring; now she distinguished the dry rattle of branches against the wall.

“Gideon used to say the abbey hears a storm before the house does,” Rhys said. “There are old drains beneath this floor. With a sea wind, they sing.”

“Monks’ drains?”

“Likely. There’s a spring under the ruined orchard, too. They built the garden around it. You can find where the channels ran by the green in August.”

She had walked those ruins all her life and seen only ruins.

“Will you show me?”

His gaze returned to her. The silence lasted just long enough.

“If your ladyship wishes.”

The title closed something between them.

Elara looked at his patched cuff, the careful stitches made with thread too pale for the cloth. Her own gloves had cost more than she cared to guess. She had left them on the breakfast table because one fingertip was stained.

“You needn’t always call me that.”

He rested his hands on the table.

“What would the others call it, if I didn’t?”

She had no answer. For her, familiarity might be a small rebellion. For him, it could be an offence.

At the door, she paused.

“May I come again?”

“To see how she fares?”

“Yes,” Elara said, a little too quickly.

“I’ll be here.”

Walking back, she passed the discarded loop of wire hanging from a thorn branch, safely twisted shut. Yesterday she had wanted only to free the falcon.

Now she thought of Rhys sleeping beside it, of all he knew and all he must leave unsaid. She wanted, without yet knowing how, to give him something beyond permission.

## Chapter 3: The Wounded Falcon

The next morning, Elara returned to the old mews with a basket of bread beneath her cloak and the excuse of seeing whether the falcon had survived the night.

The excuse was poor. The bird had survived three nights already, and Rhys had said nothing suggested it would fail a fourth. Still, no one stopped her. The house was busy preparing for Viscount Marlowe’s arrival, and the servants had learned that Lady Elara’s errands were best endured in silence.

Rhys knelt beside the falcon when she entered. A small fire burned in the grate, filling the west room with smoke and damp wood. The bird watched him with one bright eye, its injured leg resting in a linen sling.

“You came early,” he said.

“Did you expect me to sleep?”

“I expected the falcon to sleep.”

She set down the basket. “I brought bread.”

“For the falcon?”

“For you.”

“I’ve eaten.”

“Then you may eat again.”

He shook his head, though a faint warmth touched his expression. “You’ll make me unfit for work.”

“You are already in danger of becoming well fed.”

“Then I must be careful.”

She laughed. The sound seemed too bright for the crumbling room. Rhys held out a strip of raw meat.

“Would you like to help?”

Elara knelt beside him. “What do I do?”

“Place it near her beak. Not too close. Let her choose.”

“She looks as though she would choose my hand.”

“She might, if you frighten her.”

“I have never frightened anyone in my life.”

His glance suggested otherwise. “Hold still.”

The falcon lowered its head. For a breath, nothing moved but the firelight. Then the bird struck, seizing the meat with a sharp click. Elara flinched, and Rhys’s hand closed around her wrist.

“Don’t pull away,” he murmured. “She’ll think you mean to take it.”

His fingers were rough and warm. When he released her, the place where he had touched her continued to burn.

“Forgive me,” he said.

“For what?”

He looked down at the bird. “Nothing.”

After that, the days acquired a secret rhythm.

Elara came whenever she could slip free from her lessons and household duties. She carried scraps of meat in a covered dish and left by the servants’ stair when no one was watching. Sometimes Rhys waited for her. Sometimes she sat beside the falcon until he returned from the orchard or lower fields.

The bird’s wound began to close. So did something in Elara that she had not known was wounded.

Rhys taught her the uses of the land as though it were a book no tutor had thought worth opening. He showed her plantain growing beside the broken wall, its leaves useful for drawing heat from a wound, and yarrow, which could staunch blood. Willow bark eased pain. Foxglove, he warned, was beautiful and deadly.

“Like certain people,” Elara said.

“Most beautiful things are dangerous if used carelessly.”

“And which are safe?”

“Daisies.”

“Daisies are dull.”

“They are dependable.”

She studied him. “You value dependability above all else.”

His knife paused over the meat. “Dependability keeps people alive.”

He taught her to read the sky, too. Beneath a crown of dark clouds, he turned her hand toward the horizon.

“See the pale seam between them?”

She saw only gray. “A little light.”

“The storm is moving east.”

“How can you know?”

“Because it always leaves a seam behind.”

His thumb rested against the inside of her wrist. Neither spoke. Then he let her go.

The next day, he called her your ladyship three times.

At the third, she set down the bowl she was holding. “Must you keep reminding me?”

“Reminding you of what?”

“That I am not welcome here.”

His expression tightened. “You are welcome.”

“As Elara?”

“Lady Elara.”

“That is not what I asked.”

He turned toward the broken window. “You should not come here so often.”

“I know.”

“Your father will notice.”

“He notices only what affects his debts.”

“He may notice me.”

The words fell quietly, but they carried weight.

Elara rose. “You speak as if you have done something wrong.”

“I am the groundskeeper.”

“And I am the daughter of a man who owns the ground. Must that make you less human than I am?”

“No.”

“Then stop looking at me as though I am something you might break by touching.”

He faced her. “You don’t know what you’re asking.”

“I am asking you to look at me.”

He did.

The silence gathered between them, filled by the distant sea and the falcon’s soft shifting. Rhys looked away first.

By the end of the week, the bird no longer needed the sling. She stood awkwardly at first, favoring the injured leg, then hopped along the low perch Rhys had built.

“She is ready,” he said one afternoon.

“For what?”

“To remember she belongs to the sky.”

Elara’s hands went cold. “Already?”

“She cannot stay here forever.”

The mews suddenly seemed too small. She looked at the falcon, then at him.

“Perhaps she does not want to leave.”

“Perhaps she is afraid to.”

“Is that so different?”

“No.” His eyes met hers. “But fear is not the same as belonging.”

The following morning, he carried the falcon into the old garden. Wind swept over the broken walls, sharp with salt. Weeds grew between the stones, and dead roses clawed at the air.

Rhys lifted the bird onto his gloved hand.

For a moment, it remained still.

Then it opened its wings.

The falcon rose, faltered, and dropped toward the grass. Elara cried out, but Rhys did not move. The bird beat its wings again, caught the wind, and climbed. It wheeled once above the mews, a dark shape against the pale morning, then vanished beyond the cliffs.

Elara watched until there was nothing left to see.

“She is free,” Rhys said.

“Yes.”

Neither moved.

He turned to her. The restraint he had worn like armor was gone from his face. She saw fear there, and longing, and the knowledge that whatever happened next could not be undone.

“Rhys,” she whispered.

He stepped back. “You should go.”

She caught his hand.

His breath stopped.

“I am not beneath you,” he said.

“I know.”

“You should not say that so easily.”

“I do not say it easily.”

The wind tugged at her cloak. His fingers tightened around hers.

“Elara—”

She rose onto her toes and kissed him.

For an instant he stood still. Then his hand came to her waist, and he kissed her back with desperate gentleness. The world fell away—the abbey, her father, the approaching viscount, every wall built by birth and duty. There was only the warmth of his mouth and the wild sea below them.

When they parted, their foreheads rested together.

“We cannot,” he whispered.

“I know.”

“We must.”

“I know that too.”

But she did not release him, and neither did he release her.

Above the cliffs, the falcon circled in the open sky, beyond every snare and wall. Its shadow passed over them, and Elara thought that some cages were made not of iron, but of promises other people demanded you keep.

## Chapter 4: The Crumbling Walls

Summer came to Thornfield in a blaze of gold.

The grass on the cliffs grew high enough to hide a man, and the sea flashed blue between the rocks. Even the abbey seemed less dead beneath the warmth. Ivy softened its broken walls, swallows nested in the empty chapel, and roses climbed over the fallen statues in the forgotten garden.

Elara found Rhys wherever the ruins offered shelter.

In the chapel, where the roof had fallen away, he showed her marks carved into the stone floor. Some were names, others prayers, and one—nearly worn smooth—was a falcon with its wings spread wide.

“Gideon said the chapel was older than the abbey,” Rhys told her. “Before the Ashworths came, there was a fishing village here. The sea took half of it.”

“And the rest?”

“Moved inland.”

“Wise people.”

His mouth tilted. “You mean cowards.”

“I mean people who knew when a place was asking them to leave.”

He looked at her, and the air changed.

They had tried to be careful after their first kiss. They did not touch in the mews, did not linger on open paths. Yet caution only sharpened the hunger it was meant to restrain. A glance held too long became a promise. The brush of a sleeve could haunt Elara all day.

Then, one afternoon, she reached across him for a sprig of foxglove, and Rhys caught her wrist.

The chapel was empty. Wind moved through the broken windows, carrying the distant thunder of waves. He lifted her hand to his mouth and rested his lips against her knuckles.

“Elara,” he whispered.

She stepped closer.

After that, there was no pretending their meetings were innocent.

They found a hollow in the cliffs where the stone curved inward like a sheltering palm. At low tide, Rhys led her down a narrow path, and there, with the sea breathing below, she lay against his shoulder while he spoke of a life he had never dared imagine.

“I’d have a place inland,” he said. “Not grand. Two rooms, perhaps. A garden, if the soil allowed it. Apples, though they take years.”

“You would wait years for apples?”

“For the right things.”

“And what else?”

“A roof that doesn’t leak. A door that locks from the inside. No one telling me what I owe them.”

Elara rested her cheek against his chest. His heart beat steadily beneath her ear.

“And me?”

His arms tightened around her. “You’d have to want it.”

“I do.”

He said nothing. His silence was not disbelief, but fear of believing her.

At night they met in the forgotten garden. Moonlight silvered the ruined fountain, and the roses gave off their heavy scent. Beneath an arch tangled with thorns, Rhys undressed her with hands that trembled only once, when he reached the fastening at her throat.

She had been touched before by dutiful hands—dressers, physicians, her mother’s maid—but never like this. Rhys touched her as though every inch of her were a question he was grateful to ask. When she drew him down, the tenderness in his face nearly broke her heart.

Afterward they lay beneath the roses while the moon crossed the torn sky.

Elara felt remade. The walls of Thornfield receded. She could breathe. She could laugh without hearing her father’s disapproval. For the first time, she could imagine that her life belonged to her.

But the world did not vanish because they had found hidden places within it.

Viscount Marlowe arrived in July with four horses, three servants, and a black carriage polished brightly enough to reflect the abbey’s cracked windows. He brought Lord Ashworth a case of wine and Elara a necklace of pale sapphires.

“They are the color of your eyes,” he said.

Elara touched the necklace without feeling its weight. “How observant of you.”

“A husband ought to observe his wife.”

From the upper gallery, Rhys saw Marlowe fasten it around her throat. His fingers closed around the basket of branches he carried until the willow frame cracked.

That evening Lord Ashworth summoned Elara to the library.

“You have been shamefully inattentive,” he said. “Marlowe has remained three days, and still you have not given him an answer.”

“I have given him every courtesy.”

“Courtesy is not a marriage.”

“No. Marriage is a bargain, and you are the one negotiating it.”

His face tightened. “You will not speak to me as if I were your enemy.”

“Then stop treating me as property.”

For a moment, only the fire spoke. Her father looked older than he had that morning, exhausted by debts and the failing estate.

“The roof over the east wing is collapsing,” he said. “The tenants are months behind. There is no dowry left, Elara. Nothing but Thornfield—and Thornfield is sinking.”

“Then let it sink.”

“You can afford that sentiment because you have never had to keep a household alive.”

“I have had to live inside the household you kept alive.”

He struck the desk with his palm. “You will marry him before Michaelmas.”

The words followed her like a chain.

She found Rhys after dark in the old potting shed, repairing a gate hinge by lantern light. He looked up and saw the change in her face.

“What happened?”

“He has set a date.”

“When?”

“Before Michaelmas.”

The lantern flame shivered in the draft.

“Then we leave before it,” Elara said.

Rhys lowered the hammer. “No.”

“You said you would find a way.”

“I said I would try.”

“Try harder.”

He stood. “Do you know what happens if your father discovers us? He can have me arrested. He can call it theft, trespass—anything he likes. He owns the constable, the magistrate, half the men in the village.”

“Then we go farther than the village.”

“And how? On foot? With no money, no names, no protection? You think the world beyond Thornfield is waiting to welcome the daughter of a lord who ran away with his groundskeeper?”

“I would not be his daughter there.”

“You would still be a woman alone with a man the law considers his servant.”

The words struck her harder than anger. She turned away, biting her lip until she tasted blood.

Rhys came toward her, then stopped before touching her.

“I love you,” he said. “That is why I cannot pretend love makes us safe.”

“It makes us something.”

“It makes us vulnerable.”

She looked at him. His gray eyes held a fury he could not spend without destroying them both.

“I would rather be vulnerable with you than protected by him.”

Rhys closed his eyes.

Outside, a bell sounded from the abbey. Once. Twice. Calling her back.

He took her hand and pressed it against his heart.

“Not yet,” he said.

Whether he meant the escape or the parting, she could not tell. She leaned into him, and he held her with desperate care while the summer wind moved through the grass, carrying the scent of roses toward the crumbling walls.

## Chapter 5: The Storm

The Storm

The storm came in from the west before sunset, a wall of iron-grey cloud rising over the sea.

Elara watched it from the upper gallery as the windows rattled in their frames and the first gulls fled inland, crying over the roofs. Below, servants hurried through the courtyard with baskets of laundry and armfuls of firewood. A stable boy ran to secure the shutters. The yew trees bent beneath the wind.

She should have gone to her room. Her father had ordered her to remain indoors until dinner, and Marlowe expected to discuss the wedding arrangements that evening.

Instead, she took her cloak from its peg and slipped through the side passage.

The lower path was already slick with rain. Wind tore at her hood and drove salt against her lips. She knew where Rhys would be. He had said he meant to inspect the western gate before the storm worsened.

She found him there, wrestling with a chain as the gate slammed against its hinges.

“Rhys!”

He turned. Alarm crossed his face when he saw her.

“What are you doing here?”

“Looking for you.”

“You must go back.”

“I will not.”

Lightning flashed over the sea. Thunder followed so quickly that the ground seemed to tremble.

Rhys crossed the mud and seized her arm. “The path will be dangerous in another few minutes.”

“Then take me somewhere safe.”

His eyes searched hers. At last he said, “There’s a cave below the headland.”

He led her along the narrow trail, keeping one hand around hers. They moved bent against the wind, sliding more than walking, until the path dropped toward the shore. The cave mouth appeared between two shelves of black rock, half-hidden by hanging grass.

Inside, the darkness smelled of wet stone and seaweed. Rhys helped her onto a dry ledge, then crouched near the entrance. Beyond him, the sea rose and collapsed beneath the clouds.

“Elara,” he said, without turning, “you cannot keep doing this.”

“Doing what?”

“Coming to me as though there is no price.”

“There is already a price.” She pulled off her soaked cloak. “My father has set the wedding date. Before Michaelmas. Marlowe is to return in a fortnight.”

Rhys’s jaw tightened. “I know.”

“And you still tell me to wait?”

“I told you not yet.”

“You tell me nothing else.” She rose, trembling with cold and fury. “You speak of danger as though I do not see it. You speak of names and money and protection as though they are worth more than the life I am being forced to live.”

“They are worth your life.”

“I would rather die than marry him.”

The words vanished into the storm, but Rhys heard them. His expression changed.

“Do not say that.”

“I mean it.”

“You do not know what death is.”

“I know what it is to be buried alive.” She crossed the cave toward him. “Every day at Thornfield, I am watched, instructed, measured. My father speaks of me as if I were a deed he might sign away. Marlowe looks at me and sees an estate with a woman attached to it.”

“And I look at you and see a woman who might lose everything because of me.”

“Then let me lose it.”

He caught her shoulders. “And when your father has me arrested? When he tells the magistrate I stole from him, or trespassed, or laid hands upon you? You think the truth will save me?”

“No.”

Her honesty stopped him.

She laid her hands over his. “Nothing will save us unless we choose it ourselves.”

The cave shook with thunder. Water rushed over the stones, and somewhere beyond the entrance a wave broke with the force of splitting earth.

Rhys bent his head until his forehead touched hers.

“I have tried to be sensible,” he whispered.

“You have been miserable.”

“Yes.”

“Then stop.”

She kissed him, and all the restraint between them gave way.

He held her as though the storm might tear her from his arms. Her fingers found the damp wool of his coat, then the rough skin beneath it. They stumbled onto the sheltered ledge, laughing once, breathlessly, before laughter dissolved into kisses.

There was no room in the darkness for rank or duty, no father’s voice, no approaching wedding. There was only the heat of him, the salt on his lips, and the certainty that for those hours she belonged nowhere but there.

When they lay still, Elara rested against his chest and listened to his heart.

“I meant what I said,” she murmured.

“I know.”

“I will not stand beside Marlowe.”

“You may have no choice.”

“Then I will make one.”

He was silent until she lifted her head.

“What are you thinking?”

“That I have been a coward.”

“You have been afraid.”

“It is the same thing when fear makes the choices.” He looked at her. “We will leave.”

Her heart seemed to stop. “When?”

“Soon. I need money, a place beyond your father’s reach, some way of keeping you from being dragged back.” His thumb touched her cheek. “I cannot promise it will be easy. I cannot promise we will be safe.”

“I do not need safe.”

“I know.” A broken smile touched his mouth. “But I will find a way.”

The storm began to pass before dawn. Clouds lifted, leaving the sea bruised and restless beneath a paling sky. Rhys helped Elara climb the path, and they returned to the grounds by a route hidden from the house.

They knew something was wrong before reaching the courtyard.

Lanterns burned beneath the archway. Two stable hands stood near the steps, speaking in low voices. A groom hurried toward the east wing. From inside the abbey came the ringing of a bell and Lord Ashworth’s raised voice.

Elara stopped.

Rhys’s fingers closed around hers once, then released them.

“What has happened?” she asked a passing maid.

The girl curtsied too quickly. “His lordship has been looking for you all night, my lady.”

“Why?”

The maid glanced toward the groundskeeper’s cottage. “There was a rumor someone saw you near there yesterday. Mrs. Vale says she saw your cloak by the garden wall.”

Elara felt the warmth leave her body.

From the corridor, her father shouted her name.

Rhys had already stepped back into the shadows, but not before she saw the knowledge settle over his face: the fragile world they had hidden was no longer hidden.

The storm had passed.

The danger had not.

## Chapter 6: The Reckoning

By breakfast, the storm had passed, though Thornfield Abbey still seemed to tremble beneath it. Water streamed from the gutters, and the sea beyond the cliffs rolled beneath a sky of beaten pewter. Servants moved through the corridors with lowered eyes.

At eight o’clock, a footman came to Elara’s chamber.

“His lordship requires you in the study.”

She dressed without answering. Her hair still smelled of salt and rain, and Rhys’s warmth remained upon her skin like a secret her body refused to surrender. Fear had followed her from the cave into the abbey, but she held fast to the memory of his promise.

They would leave.

She entered the study alone.

Lord Ashworth stood with his back to the fire. His coat was damp, his silver hair disordered, but his face was composed. On the desk lay a leather purse, several papers, and a brass key.

“You sent for me.”

“Where were you last night?”

“You know where I was.”

“I know where you were seen.” His eyes moved over her wet boots and unbound hair. “That is not the same thing.”

She said nothing.

“Mrs. Vale saw your cloak by the garden wall three nights ago. A stable boy saw you walking toward the groundskeeper’s cottage yesterday. Then you disappeared during the storm and returned at dawn by the old drainage path.”

Elara’s heart struck hard against her ribs.

“Was Rhys seen?”

Her father’s expression shifted, almost imperceptibly. He had been waiting for the name.

“There,” he said. “At last.”

“He has done nothing wrong.”

“You have been meeting him.”

It was not a question.

“He is not what you think.”

“I think he is a servant who has forgotten his place. He has trespassed on private grounds, encouraged your disobedience, neglected his duties, and presumed to touch my daughter.”

“He presumed nothing. I went to him.”

“You crawled through drains and hid in ruins with a man who cannot sign his own name.”

“He knows more of this land than you ever have. He has kept Thornfield alive while you sold its furniture to pay your creditors.”

The words escaped before she could stop them. Her father went pale with fury.

“You know nothing of preserving a family.”

“I know you mean to sell me to Viscount Marlowe.”

“A daughter is not sold when she performs her duty. She is placed where she may be of use.”

“And if I refuse?”

“Then Marlowe withdraws his offer. The bank calls in its loans. The servants go unpaid, and Thornfield is sold stone by stone.”

“I would rather see it fall.”

“Yes,” he said. “You would.”

He turned to the desk and opened the purse. A silver snuffbox, two rings, and a ruby brooch lay within it.

Elara recognized them at once. They had belonged to her mother.

“Where did you get those?”

“They were taken from the locked cabinet in my room.”

“By whom?”

“By Rhys.”

The lie entered the room like smoke.

Elara understood. The objects had been planted; the papers beside them were meant to give the accusation the appearance of truth.

“He would never steal them.”

“It will not matter what he would do. He had access to the house and was seen near my rooms. A servant is easily believed guilty when his master wishes him guilty.”

“You would accuse him of theft?”

“I would have him arrested before sunset.”

“And then?”

His eyes remained on hers.

“If you do not marry Viscount Marlowe, he will hang.”

For a moment, she heard only the rain against the window.

“You cannot mean to do this.”

“I can, and I will.”

“I will tell everyone. I will go to the magistrate.”

“And say what? That you spent the night in a cave with the groundskeeper? That you have met him in the chapel and the gardens? You may save his life, perhaps, but you will lose your name. Marlowe will withdraw. The bank will take Thornfield. And Rhys will still hang, because no one will believe a disgraced lord’s daughter against the evidence.”

Elara crossed the room and seized his sleeve.

“You are my father.”

“I am Lord Ashworth.”

“You are supposed to protect me.”

“I am protecting this family.”

“From poverty? From shame? You would murder him rather than admit you have failed.”

His face hardened.

“Be careful.”

She let go of him. Her strength had begun to leave her limbs.

“Please.”

The word seemed to disgust him.

“I have obeyed you all my life,” she said. “I have endured Marlowe’s hand upon my arm and his eyes upon me. I have listened while you called me selfish for wanting one choice of my own. Take the estate. Take my mother’s jewels. Take everything. Only leave him alive.”

“You mistake sentiment for bargaining.”

“I will marry him.”

Her father did not move.

“I will marry Viscount Marlowe,” she continued. “I will stand beside him and speak whatever vows you require. I will become Lady Marlowe and never speak against you again.”

At last, his shoulders eased.

“But Rhys will be spared. You will not arrest him, accuse him, or lay one hand upon him. You will send him away alive.”

Lord Ashworth watched her with cold satisfaction.

“And if you refuse?”

“I will tell Marlowe everything on the day of the wedding. I will ruin you before I ruin myself.”

A small smile touched his mouth.

“You have your mother’s talent for melodrama. Very well. The groundskeeper will be spared.”

“Swear it.”

“On my honor.”

“You have none.”

“Then on the future of this family.”

The smile remained. He had won, and they both knew it.

A bell began to ring somewhere in the abbey, calling the household to breakfast. Lord Ashworth gathered the papers and returned the jewels to the purse.

“Preparations will begin at once. You will write to the viscount this morning.”

He left without another word.

Elara remained before the desk, listening to his footsteps fade.

Beyond the window, the cliffs disappeared into mist. Somewhere out there Rhys was waiting, believing that the promise they had made in the cave might still carry them beyond Thornfield.

She pressed both hands over her mouth to keep from crying out.

Then she rose and went to write the letter that would save his life and end their own.

## Chapter 7: The Impossible Choice

By dusk, Thornfield had become a machine built for Elara’s surrender.

Servants hurried through the corridors with bolts of ivory silk, baskets of flowers, and silver combs that had belonged to her mother. From the kitchen came the scent of sugared fruit. Every door stood open. Everyone knew the house was preparing a bride.

Elara sat at her writing desk with a half-finished letter beneath her hand.

My lord,

She could write no farther.

Rhys was waiting beyond the abbey. He believed their promise still lived: that they would leave before dawn, carrying what little money they had and following the old coach road inland. He did not know her father had shown her the papers bearing his name, or that a constable had been summoned to the village. He did not know a rope had been ordered from the bailiff.

Elara rose.

She slipped through a passage behind the tapestry of Saint Michael and descended the narrow stair hidden in the wall. At its foot, a rusted door opened onto the orchard. She crossed the weeds and bare trees, moving toward the spring where Rhys had promised to meet her.

He stood beside the stones, his dark coat blending with the branches. When he saw her, his face brightened. Then he read the truth in hers.

“What has happened?”

She seized his coat. “We must go tonight.”

His hands closed around her shoulders. “Elara—”

“Tonight. Now. We can take the north road, sleep in barns, sell my jewels. We can go to the coast and find a ship. I don’t care where.”

His expression hardened with dread. “What did he do?”

“My father knows.” The words rushed out. “He knows about us. He has accused you of stealing my mother’s jewels. He has papers, false witnesses—everything. He means to have you arrested. If I marry Marlowe, he says you will be sent away. If I refuse, he will hang you.”

The wind stirred the branches above them.

Rhys released her. “When?”

“He said before sunset. But he has not done it.”

“Because he expects you to obey.”

“He swore he would spare you.”

Rhys gave a bitter laugh. “Your father swore?”

“I forced him to promise. I told him I would expose everything at the wedding if he harmed you.”

“And you believe that will protect me?”

“I don’t know. That is why we must leave.”

He looked toward the abbey. A few windows glowed above the trees. Men had been posted near the stable yard, strangers with muskets beneath the archway.

“We cannot reach the road,” he said.

“Then we will use the cliffs.”

“They are watched.”

“The tunnels.”

“They know the grounds now. Your father will have men at the cottage, the gate, the chapel. If we are caught, they will not wait for a trial.”

“I don’t care what they do to me.”

“I do.”

She pushed him. “You cannot tell me to marry him and call that caring.”

His face tightened. “Do you think I don’t know what I am asking?”

“You are asking me to stand beside a man I despise while you disappear like a criminal.”

“I am asking you to live.”

“What kind of life?”

“The kind where you are breathing.”

She laughed, and the sound broke into a sob. “You speak as though breath is enough.”

“It may be all we can save.”

“No.” She stepped between him and the abbey. “There is still a way. We can tell Marlowe the truth. We can go to the magistrate, the bishop, anyone. My father cannot own me.”

“He owns the roof over your head, the servants who will testify against me, and every magistrate nearby who owes him money.”

“Then let him lose everything.”

“And you with it?”

“I would rather lose everything than lose you.”

Rhys looked at her for a long moment. “If we run, they will hunt us. They will call you abducted, mad, or dishonored. They will find us eventually. If they find us together, they will kill me first.”

“I can bear that.”

“No, you cannot.”

“I can bear anything with you.”

“That is what frightens me.”

He reached for her. She struck his chest with both hands, once, twice, but he caught her wrists and held them against him.

“You promised,” she whispered. “You promised we would leave.”

“I promised to find a way.”

“You found one. You found a way to give me to him.”

His grip loosened. “I am trying to keep you alive.”

“By killing what is alive in me?”

His eyes shone. “If your father believes you have submitted, he will let me go. I can leave before the wedding. I can go north and find work. Years from now, perhaps—”

“Do not give me years from now.”

“Elara—”

“Do not make a future of scraps. Do not tell me we might meet again as though I could endure this marriage by pretending it is temporary.”

He pulled her against him. She resisted only a heartbeat before collapsing into his arms.

“I hate you,” she said.

“I know.”

“I hate you for making me do this.”

“I know.”

“And I love you.”

His arms tightened. “I know.”

They stood beside the spring while darkness gathered. At last Rhys kissed her forehead, her eyelids, and then her mouth. The kiss was desperate and brief.

“You must go back,” he murmured.

“No.”

“You must.”

“Take me with you.”

“If I take you, I lose you.”

“You will lose me anyway.”

“Not entirely.” His voice trembled. “As long as you live, some part of me remains in the world. That must be enough.”

“It is not enough.”

“No. But it is what we have.”

He released her and stepped into the trees. The darkness swallowed his shoulders, his face, and finally the sound of his footsteps.

Elara remained by the spring until cold entered her bones.

When she returned to Thornfield, the housekeeper waited at the servants’ stair with warm water and an expression emptied of pity.

“Your ladyship,” she said, “the dressmaker is waiting.”

Elara looked toward the hall. Beneath her mother’s portrait lay the wedding veil, folded like winter sky.

“Very well.”

She climbed to her room. Strangers undressed her, washed her hair, and measured her for the life chosen in her name. When they placed her mother’s ruby ring upon her finger, Elara did not pull away.

She had saved Rhys’s life.

It was the only victory left to her, and it felt indistinguishable from defeat.

## Chapter 8: The Sacrifice

Before dawn, the bells began.

Elara heard them beneath the hands of the women dressing her. The ivory silk tightened around her waist; pearl buttons closed along her spine. Her mother’s gown had been altered until scarcely anything of its former shape remained.

Mrs. Vale stood behind her with the veil. Their eyes met in the mirror, and the old woman’s mouth trembled.

“Please,” Elara whispered. “Do not pity me. I cannot bear it this morning.”

Mrs. Vale lowered her gaze and settled the lace over Elara’s hair.

Her mother’s ruby ring lay beside the bridal jewels. Elara put it on. When Lord Ashworth entered, she was sitting upright, her hands folded so neatly that he paused before speaking.

“You look beautiful.”

“Where is Rhys?”

His glance went toward the servants. Mrs. Vale ushered the others out, then reluctantly followed.

“He is alive,” her father said.

“Is the constable gone?”

“The complaint has been withdrawn. Your groundskeeper will leave Thornfield today.”

“Without being followed?”

“I have kept our agreement.”

She studied his face for something she could trust. There was only impatience.

“If I hear otherwise before the vows—”

“You have made your threat sufficiently clear.”

He offered his arm. Beyond the door, footsteps hurried toward the chapel, and someone called for more candles.

Elara rose.

The chapel smelled of wax and damp stone. White roses stood beside the altar, bought with money that had been unavailable for servants’ wages. Marlowe waited beneath them in a dark blue coat, composed and handsome. He smiled as though her arrival fulfilled an ordinary expectation.

Her father led her between the pews. Neighbors turned to watch; creditors bowed their heads with unfamiliar reverence. She fixed her eyes upon a loose thread at Marlowe’s cuff.

Then wind stirred her veil.

A narrow window beside the aisle stood open. Through it she saw villagers gathered beyond the chapel wall, and, at the edge of the crowd, a man in a worn wool jacket.

Rhys.

His hair was blown across his forehead. He stood very still, his hands empty at his sides. When he lifted his face, she saw that he had not slept.

Her step faltered.

For an instant she could feel the spring water cooling her fingers, his hand closing over hers beneath the orchard trees. There had been so little they wanted: work, a garden, a door of their own.

Her father tightened his grip.

Rhys made no movement toward her. Only his eyes followed her, grief plain enough to reach her through the glass.

He was alive. She had needed proof, and now the sight of him was almost unbearable.

She walked on.

At the altar, Marlowe took her hand. Lord Ashworth stepped away.

The vicar opened his book. Elara watched his finger move down the page while he spoke of marriage, duty, and the obligations she was about to undertake. Behind her came the rustle of someone settling into a pew.

Then he asked his question.

She remembered the papers on her father’s desk, the stolen jewels Rhys had never touched, the rope ordered from the bailiff.

“I will.”

Marlowe answered clearly. His ring slid onto her finger beside her mother’s ruby.

When he bent to kiss her, she could not make herself lift her mouth. His lips touched her cheek. His fingers tightened briefly around hers, but his smile remained in place as he turned toward the congregation.

The bells began again.

Outside, Elara searched the faces along the chapel wall.

Rhys had gone.

At the wedding breakfast, she sat beneath the portraits of dead Ashworths while guests congratulated her father. Marlowe accepted their good wishes and occasionally rested a hand upon her chair.

“You are very quiet,” he said.

“I have nothing to add.”

“You will find married life less disagreeable if you do not begin by resisting every kindness.”

She looked down at his hand. He removed it.

Before evening, she caught her father alone near the dining-room door.

“He has been paid and dismissed,” he said before she could speak. “He crossed the boundary before sunset.”

“And the accusation?”

“Finished.”

She had obtained everything he had promised. There was nothing left to ask him for.

Their bridal chamber occupied the south wing, overlooking the cliffs. A fire had been lit, but the room remained cold beyond its immediate warmth. Elara stood at the window while Marlowe poured wine.

“We shall stay here until the estate business is settled,” he said. “Afterward, you may prefer my house inland.”

She had once imagined going inland without knowing a single village there. Now the word belonged to an itinerary her husband would arrange.

“Come away from the glass,” he said.

“Not yet.”

He watched her for a moment.

“I do not require affection tonight, Elara. I do require civility.”

She inclined her head. It was the only answer she could manage.

Eventually he extinguished the candles and went to bed. She remained beside the last glow of the hearth until his breathing deepened.

Only then did she loosen the veil still pinned to her hair.

One pin caught. She struggled with it, suddenly clumsy, and began to cry.

She lay at the far edge of the bed, pressing the lace against her mouth so that Marlowe would not wake.

Beyond the abbey boundary, Rhys climbed the old cliff path. The steward had forbidden him the grounds, but this strip of rough grass lay outside the estate wall.

From there he could see the south wing.

He knew the room. He had repaired its shutters one spring while Elara was away, never imagining he would remember the shape of its window like this.

He stood with his wages untouched in his pocket and watched the light.

At last it went out.

Rhys stayed. Behind him lay the north road; below him the sea broke against the rocks. Before him, Thornfield’s darkened windows gave him nothing more to watch, but he could not yet turn away.

## Chapter 9: The Last Desperate Act

By November, Elara had learned how little a person needed to say.

“Yes, my lord,” answered most questions. “As you wish,” answered the rest. At dinner she could pass an hour by asking whether the roads would hold through winter, then listening while Marlowe spoke of drainage, rents, and the expense of keeping Thornfield habitable.

They had not gone inland. Her father’s debts proved more tangled than Marlowe had expected, and he preferred to supervise the disentangling himself. Men arrived with ledgers; furniture departed beneath tarpaulins. Locks were changed. Two of Marlowe’s men took turns watching the courtyard after someone stole firewood from the stores.

Elara watched autumn strip the orchard.

Sometimes, while her maid fastened a necklace, she remembered Rhys bending over the falcon, his fingers steady beneath its trembling breast. The memory could be so exact that she expected to see his hands instead of her own reflected in the glass.

She had heard nothing of him.

Once she asked Mrs. Vale whether there had been news from the north.

“No, my lady.”

The older woman’s eyes filled, and Elara did not ask again.

***

Rhys had reached the north road three days after the wedding. He had followed it as far as a market town where a wheelwright needed help, and for six weeks he had worked until exhaustion ought to have made sleep inevitable.

Instead, he lay awake wondering whether she had believed him when he said he loved her.

He returned south with less money than he had taken.

For a fortnight he lodged above a fisherman’s shed in the village beyond Thornfield’s western boundary. From there he learned that the viscount remained at the abbey, that Lady Marlowe seldom went out, and that no one had seen her smile.

These were scraps of servants’ gossip. He knew that. Yet he carried them about all day, turning each one over.

He told himself he would only see her. Across the chapel, perhaps, or through a lighted window. Then he would go far enough that returning became impossible.

On the first clear night after days of rain, he crossed the orchard wall.

The ruined drain beneath the north terrace was narrower than he remembered. Water soaked his elbows; roots caught his coat. Beyond it lay the disused cellar, then the service stair that joined the passage behind Saint Michael.

He had repaired its lower steps with Gideon when he was fourteen.

Now he climbed them as though they belonged to another life.

***

Elara was returning from her father’s study when she heard her name.

Not *Lady Marlowe*. Not the careful title that had replaced her wherever she went.

“Elara.”

She stopped beside the tapestry.

For a moment she could not move. Then its edge stirred, and she saw him standing in the narrow darkness.

He was thinner. There was mud on his sleeve.

“You went north,” she whispered.

“Yes.”

“You were supposed to stay there.”

“I know.”

She reached for him before she knew she had lifted her hands. He drew her into the passage, and she pressed her face against his coat. It smelled of rain and woodsmoke, of a world in which people still walked where they wished.

Neither spoke. His arms tightened once, painfully, then loosened as though he remembered she might have bruises.

“Has he hurt you?”

She shook her head against his chest.

“I thought you dead,” she said. “Some days I thought my father had lied.”

“I should have found a way to send word.”

“Yes.”

He bowed his head. She felt his breath against her hair.

“Come away,” she whispered.

His hand stilled on her back.

The pause was answer enough.

“I have nothing ready,” he said. “No horses. No place—”

“Then why have you come?”

“I meant only to see you.”

She drew back far enough to look at him. There was no defence in his face, only the terrible admission of what he had risked for so little.

From the corridor came the scrape of a shoe.

“Who’s there?”

A lantern shone through the tapestry’s worn embroidery.

Rhys released her.

“Go to your room.”

“No.”

“If they find you here, everything begins again.”

The lantern moved nearer. Someone caught the tapestry’s edge.

Rhys pushed the little door inward behind him and ran down the stair.

Elara stepped into the corridor.

The watchman stared at her, then at the swinging tapestry.

“My lady?”

Before she could speak, footsteps sounded below. The man shouted toward the courtyard and hurried past her with his lantern.

She followed, but at the stairhead another guard caught her arm.

“Please return upstairs, my lady.”

“There is no thief.”

He glanced at her face. His grip became more careful, not less firm.

From below came Marlowe’s voice, sharp with questions.

Elara pulled free and ran toward the south wing.

***

Rhys cleared the orchard gate before the first lantern reached the terrace.

He could hear men behind him, boots striking the wet path. The inland track led past the courtyard; the lower gate was locked. He turned toward the cliffs.

He knew that path. In darkness, in storms, carrying tools or a sack of feed, he had trusted it beneath his feet.

But the autumn rains had eaten into its seaward edge.

“Stop!”

He ran harder.

Moonlight lay across the grass in broken silver. Beyond the headland he could descend toward the fishermen’s steps, lose them among the rocks.

Mist lifted from the water and veiled the turn ahead.

His right foot landed where the path had been.

Earth broke beneath his heel. He flung himself toward the bank, caught a fistful of grass, felt it tear loose in his hand.

***

Elara reached her window and thrust it open.

Below, lanterns moved along the cliff. One stopped; another swung wildly, throwing a brief yellow arc across the mist.

Then she heard him cry out.

Not a word. Only his voice, stripped of breath and distance, followed by the sound of falling stones.

She gripped the window frame.

For one instant she waited for another sound: a shout from below, a guard calling that he had caught a root, anything.

The sea struck the rocks.

She knew.

Behind her, the chamber door opened, but she did not turn. Her hands remained on the cold stone, holding the place from which she had heard him last.

## Chapter 10: The Ghost of Thornfield

They searched for nine days.

Men went down with ropes at low tide. Boats passed beneath the cliffs, their crews studying the mouths of caves, the weed caught on the rocks. On the fourth morning a fisherman brought back a strip of dark wool. Elara held it until someone explained that it might have come from any coat.

Marlowe paid the men. She never thanked him.

On the night of the fall, he had found her beside the open window, rain blowing across the sill.

“Who was he?” he asked.

For months she had guarded Rhys’s name as though silence could shelter him. There was nothing left for silence to do.

“The man I love.”

Marlowe stood very still. Then he closed the window.

Afterward they spoke only when necessity required it. He took her south before Christmas, away from the search and the servants who lowered their eyes. Her father wrote that she must consider her future. She burned the letter without answering.

No body was ever found.

For years this absence tormented her. Whenever a door opened unexpectedly, she looked up. Once, in a crowded market, she followed a man with Rhys’s gait through three narrow streets before he turned and showed a stranger’s face.

At last she ceased looking at doors. She did not cease listening to the sea.

***

When she returned to Thornfield, she was forty-seven.

Marlowe had died in February after an illness during which she had sat beside his bed and measured out his medicines. They had learned, over time, a careful civility. He had never asked her again about the man at the window.

Her father was long buried. Thornfield, preserved by Marlowe’s money and seldom visited, had passed to her under the settlements she had once been required to sign without reading.

She arrived in April with two trunks and a housekeeper who had been warned that most of the abbey would remain shut.

In the entrance hall, she took off her gloves. The banister still bore the shallow notch her ring had made when, as a girl, she had struck it in anger.

“Which rooms shall we open, my lady?”

“The east bedroom. The little parlour.”

“And the rest?”

“Leave them.”

She went through the kitchen garden alone. Beyond it, the groundskeeper’s cottage stood empty, its door swollen against the frame. Someone had removed the bench beneath the window. Nettles filled the place where it had stood.

She had thought she remembered everything.

It was the missing bench that made her weep.

***

The villagers called her the ghost of Thornfield, though never within her hearing.

They saw a tall woman in plain grey walking the cliff paths in every weather, her hair silver beneath a loosely pinned veil. She bought little, received no visitors, and sometimes stopped in the middle of a conversation as though she had heard someone calling from far away.

But when a shepherd’s boy found a falcon beneath a broken fence, it was to her that he brought it.

“I thought you might know what to do.”

The bird lay wrapped in his jacket, one bright eye watching them. Elara knelt beside the kitchen table.

“Fetch clean linen,” she told the housekeeper. “And send for the surgeon.”

“Will he come for a bird?”

“I shall pay him.”

When everyone had gone, she rested her hands on either side of the falcon without touching it. She remembered another pair of hands, patient above a trembling breast.

“Quietly,” she whispered.

She could not remember whether Rhys had spoken that word to the bird or to her.

The old mews were repaired that summer. She had the fallen stones cleared and the roof made sound, but would not allow the narrow windows to be glazed. Air moved through them, carrying salt and the scent of wet grass.

Other birds came: a hawk struck by a cart, a young falcon with fishing line wound about its leg. Some healed. Some died despite everything she could do. She buried those beneath the thorn hedge, each in a piece of clean linen.

While she worked, she spoke to them.

“Rhys would know how to hold you.”

Or simply, “Rhys,” trying the sound in the quiet room.

***

One September morning, the first falcon was ready.

Elara carried it beyond the ruined chapel to the open ground above the sea. Its feet gripped her leather glove. For a moment it remained there, turning its head against the wind.

Then it sprang.

She felt the thrust through her wrist. The bird dropped toward the cliff, caught the rising air, and climbed until she had to lift her face to follow it.

Years ago, she would have turned to Rhys then. Even now her body began the movement before she remembered.

There was only the grass bending beside her.

She walked farther along the path, keeping well back from the edge. The place where he had fallen had changed. Whole shelves of earth were gone; rain and frost had widened the wound beyond recognition. She had once been angry with the cliff for failing to preserve it.

Now she sat on a flat stone and unfastened the glove.

She could still recall the small scar on his thumb, the weight of his coat around her shoulders, his embarrassment when she praised him. These returned more readily than the sound of his voice. She no longer tried to force that memory. When it came, it came unexpectedly, while she bent over a bird or opened a door.

Above her, the falcon dwindled to a dark mark against the sky.

Elara watched until she could no longer distinguish it. Then she lowered her eyes to the horizon and stayed where she was, alone on the wind-scoured rocks, while below her the waves entered the hollow places of the cliff and withdrew.
