Story:

The first warning was not the alarm bell but the pipe.

Tessa Rill stopped halfway through tightening a coupling. Beneath the Foundry Ward’s morning racket—forge hammers, cart wheels, voices raised against the river wind—the return pipe knocked three times, paused, then knocked once more. The last tap came too soon.

“Shut the drive,” she told the pump-house attendant.

He looked at the gauge, its needle trembling near the red mark. “The lower crew’s still in the access run.”

“That’s why.”

The pump shuddered. Its casing groaned, the needle jumped, and a bell began ringing in the street. Water had reached the lower passage.

Tessa ran to the flood gate. Residents crowded the iron stair, where brown water frothed through the grate. Beyond it, the gate struck its rails with each surge. Someone pounded on the access hatch below.

“Seven workers,” Ada Fen called, pushing through the crowd with a tally board under her arm. She counted names as they were shouted to her, then marked each one. “Who has the hand controls?”

The attendant raised his hand.

“East wheel for you. Mara, take the west. I’ll call the turns and keep the count. No one leaves a control without a relief.” Ada looked at Tessa. “What’s wrong?”

Tessa pressed her palm to the return pipe. Its vibration stumbled beneath her skin. “Blocked intake. The pump’s drawing against it, then throwing pressure back through the return. Restarting will hit the gate harder.”

“We can’t hold the channel with the pump off,” the attendant said.

“We won’t hold anything if the gate splits.”

She found the manual release behind its rusted guard. The bypass wheel was built to bleed pressure into a drainage channel, but its safety stop had caught against the shaft. Tessa recognized the bend in the metal. She had worked on this mechanism last month; the wrench had slipped, and she had left the stop slightly out of line, telling herself it would last until the next service day.

The hatch boomed. People on the stair fell silent.

“Can you open it?” Ada asked.

“I can give them a way out.”

Ada studied her face, then turned back to the crew. “Listen for my count. Half a turn on the east wheel when I call. West wheel holds. If I say stop, you stop.”

Tessa gripped the release spokes. It would not move. The gate slammed again, shaking rust from its guide. She leaned into the wheel.

“Now,” Ada called.

The hand wheels turned. Pressure eased, barely. Tessa forced the release. The safety stop caught, and the wheel scraped against it.

She could leave it there and trust the narrow bypass to drain slowly while the water climbed the stair. Or she could force it farther and risk the mechanism.

“Tessa,” Ada said.

“I know.”

Tessa set her shoulder against the spokes and drove. The stop groaned, then the wheel jumped past it. The release cracked open. Water thundered through the bypass, and the gate’s next impact landed softer.

“Hold!” Ada shouted.

The residents locked the hand wheels in place. At the hatch, the pounding changed to three blows, a pause, then three more.

“They’re at the ladder,” someone called.

“Keep the channel steady,” Tessa said. A quick rattle came from inside the release housing. She did not look at it. “East wheel, ease a quarter turn when I say. No more.”

Ada relayed the order, and the residents shifted their grips. The gauge trembled down from red. The hatch lifted, caught, then rose again. The first worker dragged himself onto the landing, coughing water onto the stones. Ada counted him in. Six followed: soaked, shivering, alive. The youngest could barely stand, and a woman took his weight as he reached the stair.

Ada kept everyone at the controls. The floodwater still pressed against the gate, and the release housing continued its light, uneven rattle. Tessa felt the wheel shiver under her palms. It was holding, but she could not tell what the surge had done to it—or how much damage she had caused by forcing it open.

The university inspector arrived once the workers were clear. His dark coat was dry, his badge polished, a brass instrument in his hand. He examined the gauge and the flowing bypass.

“Stand clear,” he ordered.

“The channel is settling,” Ada said, staying at her wheel.

The inspector placed his instrument against the gauge plate and recited the formal hydraulic measures. Its engraved marks brightened. The rough current drew into a smoother flow; the flood gate steadied in its rails. A low hum replaced the shudder beneath their feet.

Relief passed through the crowd. Someone clapped.

“Central oversight prevents a local emergency from becoming a ward-wide disaster,” the inspector announced. “The crews’ assistance was timely.”

Tessa looked at the bent stop and the open bypass. She could tell him the release had caught, that she had forced it, and that the mechanism needed repair. But the workers were safe, and the inspector was already shaping the rescue into proof of the university’s protection. Tessa did not know whether her shortcut had damaged the stop further. An admission now could discredit the hands that had brought the workers out.

She said nothing.

When the inspector turned to his clerk, Ada came beside her. “How bad?”

“The release needs a new stop. The housing needs inspection.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“I don’t know what the surge did to it.”

Ada’s jaw tightened. “Then we keep a watch. The pump stays off until the intake is clear and the release is checked.”

“The ward can’t run long without it.”

“Nor can it run if we break the machinery trying to prove we can.” Ada glanced toward the rescued crew. “No dramatic fixes, Tessa. Not with people below us.”

Tessa nodded. Residents took turns at the controls, passing Ada’s tally board between them. They would stay until the channel ran quiet; the pipes and wheels were theirs to tend, whether or not the university named their work.

The inspector explained the gauge readings to his clerk. His badge caught the light. In the street, the story of the rescue was already taking shape: the university had steadied the water, the residents had assisted, and seven workers had survived.

Tessa returned to the pump-house wall and rested her palm against the iron return pipe. The knocking had stopped. She listened anyway, while the ward kept watch over the machinery that kept it alive.

The renewal hall had been a boiler shed. Old heat lingered in its brickwork, and morning light came through the high windows in a thin, smoky band. Residents queued beneath it with their work ledgers: pump tending, pipe inspection, flood watch, street drainage. The university called these service obligations. In the Foundry Ward, they were the work that kept the water out.

Ten days remained before Convocation. Tessa Rill recognized the names as they passed across the clerk’s table. Jori from the east pump signed with his cramped left hand. The Venn sisters renewed their night watches at the lower culvert. Two rescue volunteers, still pale from last week’s flood, committed to another season. They signed because the ward needed them, and because refusing could cost access to heat, medicine, or the machinery they maintained.

Among the renewal notices lay a sheet headed PROPOSED ENERGY ALLOCATION, FLOOD SEASON. Tessa read the figures, then checked the heading again. The ward’s allotment would fall by nearly a third during the high-water months. The university districts would receive the difference.

“This is wrong,” she said.

The clerk glanced at the next person in line. “It’s proposed. Comments go to the Allocation Office.”

“After we renew?”

“Renewals are separate.”

Separate, Tessa thought, was what they called taking a person’s labor with one hand and cutting the power needed to do it with the other.

She carried the schedule to the slate on the wall, where the pumps’ minimum operating loads were recorded. She knew the figures without checking: the east pump needed a steady current to lift water from the lower streets. The drainage wheels could be turned by hand in an emergency, but not through a night of rising water, let alone a season.

The recent failure had left little room for doubt. The intake had clogged; the release stop was bent, and its housing still needed inspection. Tessa remembered the wheel shuddering under her shoulder as she forced it. The workers had got out, but she could not tell whether her effort had worsened the damage. If the ward lost power when the river rose, a worse failure might leave no time to find out.

She tapped the schedule. “If we interrupt the shared supply, they’ll have to answer.”

Ada Fen set down a stack of signed renewals. “Not the pumps.”

“They’re cutting power from the ward that maintains the system.”

“And if we stop it, the lower streets flood first.” Ada’s voice stayed quiet, but her gaze moved over the residents nearby. “The people who signed this morning would be standing in that water.”

Tessa felt the anger in her tighten, demanding a gesture large enough to be noticed. Ada had a way of making the cost visible before the gesture could become a plan. “So we do nothing?”

“We keep every pump staffed and running. Then we make a case they can’t dismiss.” Ada glanced toward the door, where the pump crews were already gathering their tools. “No dramatic fixes. Not with people below us.”

Tessa folded the schedule along its crease. She disliked the answer because it was right. “We make the case.”

A young man beside the notice board had been listening. His university coat was new at the shoulders, and his Convocation pin still shone. Tessa knew him from his student visits to the ward: Lio Marr, who had once stood beside the intake taking notes while workers hauled out silt.

“You’re assigned to enforce this?” she asked.

“Once it’s approved,” he said. “I’m carrying the district schedules and explaining the limits.”

“Can you change ours?”

“No. The allocation is set centrally.” He hesitated. “I can record objections.”

“Record that these hours don’t meet the pumps’ minimum load.”

Lio opened his form. “If you give me the figures, I can attach them.”

“I just did.”

He wrote carefully, with the stiff concentration of someone aware that his handwriting might be the only part of the decision he controlled. Tessa could not tell whether he would follow through. At least, unlike the clerk, he had not pretended the numbers were irrelevant.

Professor Iven Saye stood in the adjoining corridor beneath a framed map of the city’s water lines. Tessa had once worked under him, checking pressure readings and copying the observations he later discussed in lectures. She went to his table before she could talk herself out of it.

“You knew the east release was failing,” she said. “We sent the readings.”

Saye’s attention settled on her. “There were several reports from the ward. Some were incomplete.”

“The stop was bent. The intake was fouled. We warned your office that the gate was slow.”

“I remember concerns about maintenance.”

“Did you send them forward?”

He straightened the papers on his table, aligning their edges. “I sent the information available to me through the proper channels.”

There it was again: an answer without an actor. Tessa felt suspicion harden, but it could not become proof. She had copies of the readings and gaps where replies should have been. She did not know whether Saye had buried the warnings, or whether the university’s channels had swallowed them without his help.

A bell called the hall to order. Chancellor Pell stood on the shallow platform beneath the city map and spoke about the coming flood season. Power, he said, must be distributed where it could be monitored. Local control invited dangerous misuse; central oversight let trained operators protect the city as a whole.

Tessa looked at the folded schedule in her hand. She had forced the release without credentials or certainty. The intervention had helped bring six workers out, but she still did not know what the surge had done to the mechanism. If officials learned that her repair might have worsened the failure, Pell could offer it as proof that the ward could not be trusted with its own machinery.

The address ended in polite applause from the university delegates. Around Tessa, residents gathered their ledgers and returned to their shifts. Ada was already assigning pump watches, making sure every control had someone beside it.

Tessa took the schedule to the pump house. She laid it beside the inspection log and opened the drawer containing pressure readings, maintenance requests, and carbon copies of unanswered letters. Her hands were unsteady, though she could not tell whether from cold or anger. She sorted the records by date and marked the measurements that showed what the ward required to survive the flood season. Pipes clicked in the walls as the machinery settled. Tessa bent over the pages and began assembling a case the city would have to read.

By morning, Tessa had arranged the ward’s case across three boards: pressure readings on the first, unanswered maintenance requests on the second, and allocation schedules on the third. Together they showed what no single document admitted. The university had reduced pumping power while the river rose, then cited the pumps’ declining performance as evidence that the ward needed closer supervision.

She carried the boards to the station’s public entrance. Rain silvered the brickwork; water stood in the hollows between the cobbles.

“We want enough power to hold the lower channels,” she told the allocation clerk, “and access to the operating records. Not another assurance that someone has reviewed them.”

The clerk stayed behind the wicket. “The allocation is authorized.”

“By whom?”

“The appropriate faculty.”

Ada, beside Tessa, tapped a maintenance request dated six weeks earlier. “Then send someone who can answer for this.”

The clerk took their written demand. At noon he returned it with a stamped refusal. Operating records were restricted to credentialed personnel. Allocation appeals would be heard at the next Convocation.

Through the station windows, Tessa could see the river gauge climbing its painted scale.

“Then we’ll wait here,” she said.

They occupied the station without stopping a pump.

Ada posted watches, kept the aisles clear, and sent anyone without a task to the dry gallery above the machinery. Residents brought bread, blankets, and copies of flooded-cellar claims. Tessa tied their demands to the entrance rail.

“No one touches a working control without Ada’s agreement,” she told them. “We’re here because this service matters. We don’t interrupt it.”

She meant every word. That made what followed harder to remember afterward.

For several hours, the occupation accomplished little beyond drawing a crowd. The clerk departed through a rear passage. No faculty representative came. Inside, the pumps labored beneath a low ceiling furred with condensation, their steady strokes interrupted by a knock Tessa had noticed since the earlier release.

Ada checked the bearing housing. “We need the internal pressure record.”

“And the governor settings,” Tessa said.

Both lay behind the same credential cabinet.

Its glass panel displayed columns of figures, but the detailed register remained shuttered. Below it, a brass plate carried the university seal and a narrow contact strip where a degree-holder could establish authority. Tessa had repaired wiring beneath similar cabinets. She knew where their ordinary supply entered.

“They’ve put the evidence behind a diploma,” she said.

Ada looked up. “Leave that until we’ve someone who knows it.”

“We’ve asked.”

“That doesn’t make us know it.”

Tessa heard caution, but also the old humiliation: wait outside while someone qualified decided whether your flooded home warranted attention. She knelt and removed the cabinet’s lower cover.

She intended only to release the record shutter. Beyond the wires, a small linked assembly connected the credential contact to the control shaft. It looked like a retaining catch. She eased a screwdriver beneath it.

The resistance surprised her.

Then the catch broke.

A sharp click traveled through the cabinet. The shutter stayed closed. Across the chamber, the familiar knock became a continuous hammering.

Ada turned at once.

“What did you move?”

Tessa stared at the severed brass tongue. “I thought—”

“Which part?”

She pointed.

Ada looked from the cabinet to the rising pressure needle. “Everyone off the lower floor. Now. Behind the brick division.”

Workers hesitated until she shouted again.

Tessa tried to press the tongue back into place. It slipped beneath her fingers, hot now. Somewhere inside the pump, the relief mechanism strained without opening.

She had expected a barrier between herself and information. Instead she had touched machinery she could neither see nor name.

The west door opened. Lio entered with two university attendants, rain running from his coat.

“This station must be returned to—”

“Relief won’t engage,” Ada cut in. “Pressure’s still climbing.”

He stopped looking at the residents.

Lio crossed to the gauge, followed the trembling linkage with his eyes, and swore. He ordered the attendants back and climbed the service step beside the main wheel.

The wheel had jammed halfway through its governing turn. Each pump stroke drove a shudder into its spokes.

“Clear of the casing,” he said.

Tessa stayed by the open cabinet.

“Not there, Tessa. Clear.”

She moved.

Lio drew his mechanics diploma from the inner pocket of his coat. Its folded metal edge caught the lamplight as he pressed it against the housing. With his other hand he touched the wheel’s hub, then the heavy flywheel turning beside it.

For a moment nothing happened. His gaze moved between the shafts, measuring their motion.

Then the air gave a hard, hollow crack.

The jammed wheel lurched backward. Momentum passed into the flywheel, which accelerated with a shriek that set Tessa’s teeth on edge. Lio staggered against the rail but kept his hand in place. The governing linkage traveled the last inch.

The relief valve opened.

Water thundered into the return channel. Beneath Tessa’s boots, the floor shook; then the pressure needle began to fall.

No one spoke until Ada called the first watch back to its post.

Lio descended slowly, his hand trembling.

“You promised to keep them running,” he said.

“I was opening the records.”

“You were interfering with a live control.”

“It should have been labeled.”

“Yes,” he said. “And you should have stopped when you didn’t understand it.”

She wanted him to deny the university’s fault, to give her something she could fight. Instead he glanced toward the workers sheltering behind the brick division.

“They trusted your promise.”

Tessa looked at Ada, who was listening to the damaged pump with her head tilted. Shame moved through Tessa with an almost physical heat.

“Your degree doesn’t give the university the right to starve us of power.”

“No. But knowing where that force would go kept this casing whole.”

The pump knocked again, unevenly.

Lio looked toward the entrance. Saye stood just inside, taking in the exposed cabinet and the wet floor.

“He needs to inspect it,” Lio said.

Tessa’s suspicion had not lessened. Neither had the pressure beneath the gauge.

She stepped away from the cabinet.

“Tell us what you find,” she said to Saye. “All of it.”

Saye stopped at the edge of the spreading water.

“Which circuit is still carrying the ward?”

“The eastern pair,” Ada said. “Half speed. This one’s disconnected from the drive, but the return line is live.”

“Then nobody opens the inspection plate yet.”

Ada looked at him for a moment, then nodded to the crew. “You heard him.”

Tessa remained beside the broken cabinet. The pump’s uneven knocking traveled through the soles of her boots. Every fourth stroke came harder than the others, like a fist striking from inside the iron.

Saye set his leather case on a dry stair and took out a lamp, a narrow mirror, and a jointed measuring rod. He did not touch the credential plate. Instead, he crouched below it and followed the exposed linkage into the housing.

“Show me exactly what you moved.”

Tessa pointed. “That latch. Then the brass tongue behind it.”

“With what?”

She handed him her screwdriver.

He laid its flattened tip beside a fresh gouge.

“The latch releases the records drawer,” he said. “The tongue is part of the governor interlock. They share a mounting because the operating record must register any change to the governor setting.”

“So you cannot alter the pressure without leaving a record.”

“You should not be able to.”

He angled the mirror beneath the broken bracket. Lamplight flashed across a bent strip of steel.

“Your tool pushed the tongue past its stop. That held the relief linkage shut while the drive continued feeding pressure into the casing.”

Tessa looked toward the wheel where Lio had stood.

“I thought the tongue was another catch.”

“I can see why. The markings are on the inside cover.”

“The cover they wouldn’t let us open.”

“Yes.”

His agreement gave her no relief.

Saye asked Ada to lower the eastern pair another fraction. She sent a worker to the controls, waited for his signal, then watched the return gauge until its needle settled.

Only then did she let Saye loosen the inspection plate.

The air that escaped smelled of hot metal and river mud. Beneath the plate, a little curved arm trembled against its stop.

“There,” Saye said. “The bracket has twisted. The arm is catching on every return.”

Tessa reached for her spanner.

“Hold the lamp first.”

Her hand stopped. For an instant she heard an insult in the request. Then she saw how little clearance there was between the arm and the shaft.

She took the lamp.

Saye worked slowly, asking Ada to confirm each change in the sound. He eased the bracket outward and fitted a temporary spacer beneath its foot. The knocking softened, though it did not disappear.

“This will carry reduced service,” he said. “The bracket needs replacing before the pump takes full load.”

“I can make that,” Tessa said.

“Can you keep the mounting faces parallel?”

“To half a line.”

“It needs a quarter.”

She swallowed. “Ada can. I can cut the blank.”

Ada held out her hand for the measurements.

The admission ought to have been small. Tessa had spent years passing work to whoever did it best. Yet here, with Saye’s precise instruments spread on the stair, it felt as though she were surrendering something larger than a bracket.

She tightened the inspection cover after he showed her which bolts carried the seal.

“I damaged it,” she said. “The crew needs to hear that from me.”

Ada glanced up. “They do.”

“But I still want the records open. And people here trained to operate what keeps their houses dry.”

Saye wiped his measuring rod.

“The lock protects more than university authority.”

“It protects both,” Tessa said. “You’ve shown me one reason for it. That doesn’t answer the other.”

Outside, someone dragged a crate across the entrance. The occupation continued around them: workers changing watches, a kettle beginning to hiss, wet coats steaming against the warm brick.

Tessa lowered her voice.

“There are warnings in those records. Unanswered ones. Your knowing how to mend this doesn’t make them disappear.”

Saye’s cloth stopped moving.

“No,” he said.

“Then tell me why Ada can keep these pumps alive for twenty years and still need permission to touch their controls.”

“She needs a recognized qualification.”

“Where is she supposed to get one? Leave the ward? Pay university fees while somebody else does her shifts?”

“There is another provision.”

Ada set down her pencil.

Saye looked toward the entrance before continuing.

“Under the older charter, a qualified teacher may confer a degree by practical examination, publicly witnessed. Attendance at the university is not a condition.”

Tessa waited, suspicious of how quickly hope could take hold.

“What makes it a degree?”

“An authorized matriculation press. There is one in the faculty keep here in the ward. The examiner enters the discipline and attests the result. With power supplied, the press communicates with the Registry and authenticates the diploma.”

“A piece of paper opens the lock?”

“The authenticated diploma establishes the holder’s standing. The authority follows the discipline recorded on it.”

“Enough to do what Lio did?”

“For someone qualified in mechanics, perhaps. A degree in drainage would grant a different scope. Neither would supply knowledge the holder lacked.”

Ada folded her arms. “What does the candidate have to do?”

“Explain the system. Demonstrate its operation. Recognize a fault and respond safely. Answer questions that establish understanding beyond familiar routine.”

“And the witnesses?”

“Must be able to observe the examination and hear the attestation. Their names enter the record.”

Tessa pictured the keep’s barred entrance, then the crew gathered around an open machine, listening.

“We could write down what everyone knows,” she said. “Every repair. Every watch. Find where the gaps are.”

“And record the gaps,” Ada said. “No dressing up something learned by habit as something understood.”

Tessa nodded. On the stair lay a scrap of packing paper. She turned it over and smoothed it against her knee.

“The press needs power,” she said. “We need access to the keep. And a teacher qualified to examine.”

Saye closed his case without answering.

Tessa wrote the three requirements down, leaving space beneath each.

Tessa left the three requirements on the packing paper and tucked it inside her coat. The stair beneath them trembled with the pump crew’s labor. Water worried at the ward below, pressing through cracks and joints with a steady insistence that made every pause feel borrowed.

“The service route,” Lio said. He stood at the stair’s foot, one hand braced on the wall, his face pale from the effort of securing the way ahead. “It’s still clear. Mostly.”

“Mostly is what we have,” Tessa replied.

She led them down.

The passage narrowed behind the old laundry rooms, where pipes crossed overhead in a confusion that only looked like confusion. Tessa knew which lines carried clean water, which returned it, and which had been abandoned when the university altered the keep decades ago. She had studied the layout from smudged maintenance plates and learned its truth from years of listening: a hollow knock at one elbow, a warm shiver in a return line, the pitch a valve made when it was almost shut.

“Keep left at the brass collar,” she whispered. “The right-hand branch feeds the ward’s upper cistern. If we close it, the pumps will have to fight the whole building.”

A witness behind her glanced at the pipe. “You can tell that from here?”

“I can tell it’s carrying water,” Tessa said. “The rest is on the plate.”

There was no pride in her answer, but she felt the familiar tightening in her chest. Knowing the system did not make it hers. The university owned the plates, the keep, the press, and the keys. The ward supplied the hands that kept them all from failing.

At the passage’s first bend, a bell rang above them: two sharp strokes followed by a longer one. The evacuation signal.

Pell’s officials had reached the keep.

The witnesses shifted uneasily. Some had come prepared to see an examination, not to defy an order in a flooded building. Tessa turned back toward them.

“Ada’s coming when the crew can spare her,” she said. “Not before. If we leave the pumps without enough hands, there may be no ward left to examine anyone in.”

No one answered. Their silence might have been agreement, or fear. Tessa could not afford to decide which.

They reached the pump chamber’s service door. Beyond it, the crew worked around two handwheels and a counterweight assembly, sleeves rolled, faces wet with spray. The nearest pump thudded unevenly. Ada was crouched beside its return pipe, listening with her cheek near the casing while a young worker held a lamp for her.

“Backpressure’s climbing,” Ada said, not looking up. “We can spare me when the west gate is holding steady.”

Tessa wanted to argue. She heard the uselessness of it before she opened her mouth. The pump’s rhythm had a stumble in it, slight but worsening. Ada heard it too.

“How long?” Tessa asked.

“Until I know the line won’t buck when I leave.” Ada straightened and wiped water from her brow with the back of her wrist. Her attention moved across the crew, counting hands and tasks. “Take the witnesses. I’ll come as soon as I can.”

Tessa nodded. She disliked leaving Ada among the machinery that made their case possible, but it would have been worse to turn the ward’s survival into a stage prop. She moved on.

The service route climbed toward the faculty keep. The stone changed underfoot from rough utility blocks to fitted slabs, and the pipes gave way to brass conduits set behind narrow inspection doors. Here, the old hydraulic layout doubled back beneath the examination rooms. Tessa steered them around a pressure chamber and through a narrow arch rather than the direct corridor.

“That corridor’s faster,” one of the witnesses murmured.

“It’s also where the old bypass meets the main return,” Tessa said. “We pass it without touching anything, or the pump crew takes the surge.”

From the other side of the keep came the clatter of boots and a man’s voice ordering doors opened. Pell’s officials were clearing rooms as if the danger came from the people in them.

They entered the lower hall just as a security gate began to descend across the passage ahead. Its iron lattice rattled in its tracks. A warning lamp flashed red above it.

“Move!” Tessa shouted.

The first witnesses ducked under. The gate dropped another foot. Lio stepped forward, teeth set, and raised both hands. The iron stopped with a wrenching clang, its weight hanging above the crew.

“Go,” he said.

His arms shook. The gate’s counterweight groaned in the wall, pulling against him. Tessa saw the strain gather in his jaw and the fine tremor in his fingers. This was not a force he could hold for long.

The witnesses hurried beneath the lattice. Tessa remained to guide the last two around a puddle where a floor plate had lifted. Behind them, the pump crew’s young worker appeared, one hand on Ada’s shoulder. Ada had left the pump running under the crew’s watch, but she looked back once toward the chamber before crossing the threshold.

“West gate is steady?” Tessa asked.

“For now,” Ada said. “They know the sound to watch for.”

Then she ducked under the security gate. Lio let it rise a fraction, enough to clear her, and released his hold. The gate slammed down behind them. He staggered against the wall and drew a ragged breath.

No one thanked him. There was no time, and he seemed to prefer it that way.

The matriculation room stood beyond a pair of carved doors, its high windows clouded by rain and rising mist. The press occupied the far end: a black iron frame bolted to a stone plinth, its engraved plates dull beneath the lamps. Saye opened his case and checked the Registry contacts. Tessa watched the indicator needle tremble.

“Is it enough?” she asked.

“It has power,” Saye said. “Not steady power.”

The needle dipped. Somewhere beneath them, a pump gave a heavy, uneven beat. Water lapped against the bottom steps of the hall outside.

A fist struck the doors. “By order of Provost Pell, clear the keep!”

The witnesses looked toward Tessa. Ada’s hands were still damp from the pump room; she flexed them once, as if the tools remained in them. Lio leaned against the wall, breathing carefully. The press clicked, and its indicator went dark.

Then the needle twitched back.

Tessa felt the floor shiver under her boots. The water was still rising, and the press had not yet held a steady charge.

The lamps dimmed again. Beyond the carved doors, water slid over another stair with a persistent hiss. Tessa pictured the lower galleries filling: first the cable trenches, then the valve sockets. Once those sockets drowned, a worker could put his whole weight on a wheel without knowing whether the gate beneath it had moved.

She pulled open the service cupboard beside the windows. Inside, three handwheels projected from a wall dark with condensation. Their pipes disappeared beneath the matriculation room.

“You two. Here.”

The ward workers came reluctantly away from the press.

“Hold the return at its chalk mark,” she told one. “If it kicks, call me. Don’t fight it blind.” To the other: “Watch the balance gauge. Tell me every change.”

Saye joined her with his notebook open. He traced the connections against a diagram, then watched the paired needles while Tessa eased the return wheel a fraction.

“North return responds,” he said. “The lower bypass doesn’t.”

“Seized?”

“Or its indicator is flooded. We cannot distinguish from here.”

Tessa chalked a cross beside that wheel. No one would touch it merely because it remained within reach.

The knocking became a sustained pounding.

“Let him speak,” Saye said.

Tessa opened one door. Pell stood beyond it with two officials and a porter pushing a low iron cart. Long spanners lay across its bed.

Her eyes went from the tools to the bolts securing the press.

“No.”

“The keep is under evacuation,” Pell said. Rain glistened on his collar. “The lower galleries are taking water. This room must be cleared, and the matriculation apparatus secured.”

Lio straightened from the wall, then caught himself against a chair. His face had gone grey. Tessa put out a hand without looking at him.

“Stay there.”

Pell followed the gesture.

“You have already exhausted one licensed operator forcing entry. Must someone drown before you acknowledge the danger?”

For a moment Tessa wanted to bar the doors and refuse him every word. But water shone on the stairs behind his men. The danger was real; pretending otherwise would make everything else she said worthless.

She stepped aside, leaving the porter’s cart blocked by her boot.

“Professor. Show him the supply reading.”

Pell entered. One official moved towards the press, spanner in hand. Ada stood in his path. She said nothing, but her damp fingers closed around the edge of the iron frame.

“Touch those bolts,” Saye told the official, “and I will record who ordered their removal during an examination.”

“You have not begun an examination,” Pell said.

“Then allow me to begin.”

Tessa tapped the glass of the supply gauge. Its needle climbed, faltered, and climbed again, never reaching the old safety mark. Below it, behind a sealed cover, the university’s allocation setting remained fixed.

“Your cut is still in force.”

“Emergency distribution,” Pell replied. “The university has obligations beyond this ward.”

“Your public finding,” Tessa said to Saye. “What supply did these pumps require under flood load?”

“At least the marked minimum. With all three working.”

“And we’re getting?”

“At best, two-thirds of it.”

Pell looked at the professor. “Your calculation assumed an intact system.”

“Yes,” Saye said. “Damage does not make the required supply smaller.”

A thin metallic chatter came from the service cupboard. The balance gauge trembled.

Tessa crossed the room before the worker could turn his wheel.

“Hold. Let me see.”

She watched the return needle, listened to the uneven beat beneath the floor, then signalled a small adjustment. The chatter stopped. The needle did not fall; it merely ceased its dangerous flutter.

At the speaking tube beside the cupboard, she lifted the cap and called for the pump crew.

The answer arrived broken by distance.

“Lower gallery floor’s covered. Bearings dry. Speed’s down again.”

“Since the allocation changed?”

“Aye. No jam. We’ve checked.”

Tessa repeated the report aloud.

“Less power, fewer strokes, rising return pressure. The machinery hasn’t stopped needing energy because your office stopped allotting it.”

“The occupation obstructs evacuation,” Pell said. “That remains true.”

“Then keep the stair clear. Send people out by the upper passage. But taking these workers off the controls will flood the route you want them to use.”

One witness shifted towards the door. Tessa saw his glance at the wet stair and did not blame him.

“Can you keep it open?” he asked.

“For now. We’ll watch it. If the return passes that second mark, we move you.”

“And the examination?”

“Comes after keeping you alive.”

He looked at the worker braced against the wheel, then returned to his place beside the witness table. Another witness opened her book and wrote down the gauge readings.

Pell noticed.

“I require my objection entered,” he said.

She dipped her pen.

“I have entered it.”

He turned to his officials. “Keep the doorway clear.”

The porter drew the cart back, but only as far as the hall. Pell had not withdrawn his order. Tessa could still see the spanners through the open door.

Saye returned to the press. Its indicator flickered weakly while he checked the Registry contacts, then laid his notebook where the witnesses could see it.

“Ada.”

She released the frame. Rust had marked her palm.

“You are being examined for competence in these connected pumping and drainage works,” he said. “Not for sympathy with your ward, nor courage in reaching this room. I will state the standard before each task and give my reasons for accepting or rejecting your performance.”

Ada swallowed. Tessa saw the effort it took her not to glance towards the failing supply needle.

“If you cannot demonstrate the required competence,” Saye continued, “I will not authorize the press.”

“I understand.”

Tessa wanted to reassure her. Instead she checked the return gauge. Ada needed an honest examination, not another promise no one could guarantee.

Saye drew a pressure slate towards him.

“First, diagnosis. You must distinguish a fault from a change in operating conditions, using observations that others can verify.”

Beneath their feet, the pump missed a beat.

“Listen,” he said. “Then tell us what you would measure.”

“The inlet pressure first,” Ada said. “Before the station controls. Then the discharge pressure and the height in the return.”

Saye waited while the pump stumbled beneath them.

“If the inlet has fallen, opening our throttle cannot restore what isn’t being supplied,” she continued. “If it hasn’t, we look for a restriction here. And we keep someone at the return gauge while we look.”

Tessa held the lamp as Ada checked the readings. Its light trembled across wet brass. The inlet confirmed the reduced supply; the return was still climbing, though more slowly now.

“Your observations support that distinction,” Saye said, writing. “Next: show how you would keep the lower channels protected if this pump stopped.”

Before Ada touched a valve, he stated the requirement: she must not relieve pressure in one branch by driving water into another occupied street.

Ada traced the connections aloud. She stationed a resident at the lower gauge, required him to repeat her instructions, and tested the relief wheel in small increments. Twice she stopped to compare readings. Tessa, impatient for movement, watched the water creep towards the second mark and understood why stopping was part of the task.

When Saye asked her to demonstrate a safe restart, Ada refused to use the damaged starter.

“The interlock cannot be trusted,” she said. “We isolate it. No automatic restart until it has been repaired and tested.”

Saye examined the isolation himself. He then required her to calculate the remaining safe load and explain how she would recognize a failing seal. Each task, its standard, and his reason for accepting her work went into the witness book.

At last he signed.

“Competent within the scope examined.”

The press shuddered through its short cycle. Ada’s credential emerged with one corner imperfectly inked. She studied the name and authorization, then handed it to a witness to check.

“Read it back,” she said.

By morning, the water had begun to withdraw.

It left a greasy border on the station walls and pale islands of silt beneath the benches. Outside, residents stood in doorways watching familiar paving stones reappear. Somewhere down the street, a loose shutter knocked steadily against its frame.

Ada divided the inspection work before anyone started a pump.

“Check the footings and pipe supports first. Mark anything that has shifted. Don’t tighten a coupling to pull two lengths back into line.”

She sent pairs along the lower channels and kept one person at each working gauge. A pump whose housing had taken grit remained stopped. Another returned to service only after its bearings had been cleaned and its discharge checked. Every improvement seemed painfully small until the chalk marks showed what those small improvements had accomplished.

Tessa carried tools and scraped fouled packing from a flange. Usually she would have reached past someone working too slowly. Now she made herself ask what they had found.

At the witness table, the public account grew heavier.

The allocation cut was copied from the supply register, with its time and recorded authorization. Beside it went Saye’s signed confession, the examination standards and results, and the successive readings from the defense of the station. Residents added who had held each wheel, which channels had remained open, and when the lower streets had begun to drain.

When Tessa’s turn came, she stood with both hands flat on the table.

“I tried to bypass the credential lock,” she said. “I damaged the starter interlock. That made a restart unsafe.”

She could hear the qualification forming in her mouth: that the lock should never have barred them, that the supply should never have been cut. Both were true. Neither repaired what she had broken.

“The risk from that damage was mine,” she finished. “Put it in.”

A woman who had worked beside her at the return wheel looked up.

“You stayed when we needed you.”

Tessa nodded, unable to answer.

“And Ada was right to stop you starting it again,” the woman added.

“Yes.”

Ada gave Tessa the replacement assembly to clean, but assigned someone else to inspect the repair.

Lio’s account took longer than Tessa expected. He kept correcting the clerk when a sentence became broader than what he had seen.

He described taking residents from the lower passage to the upper landing, going back for a man who could not manage the steps, and asking for help at the stair. The officials he approached had said they were waiting for instructions. He had seen the tool cart remain in the hall, ready for work on the press.

“Were they aware of the danger?” the clerk asked.

“I told them water was coming in. I don’t know what else they knew.”

Pell’s representatives arrived while wet bedding was being carried upstairs to dry. One listened to the testimony with his gloves still on.

“The occupation created an emergency in essential works,” he said. “Unauthorized interference cannot be separated from these consequences.”

“No,” Ada said. “It should be separated carefully.”

She laid out the inlet readings, the record of the allocation cut, and the changing return levels. Tessa’s damaged interlock had its own entry. So did the decision to isolate it. Witnesses identified the measures that had kept the lower channels open.

The representative challenged two times. One witness corrected hers. The clerk amended the record without scraping away the original.

Tessa watched him do it. She disliked having her fault preserved there, available to anyone who wanted to use it against them. Yet the same pages held the cut, the evacuation, and the readings the officials could not explain away.

Across the room, a resident called out the next gauge figure.

Ada repeated it before the witness wrote it down. Then she sent Tessa to fetch the tools for another inspection.

By the time Pell came down to the station, the flood had left a brown line across the meeting-room wall. Below it, the plaster blistered. Two chairs stood in the yard to dry; everyone at the negotiating table would have to stand.

Ada would not bring the pump crews off duty for the conference. Their voices reached the room through the stairwell, calling readings over the steady drive of the machinery. Tessa stood beside the ledger, resisting the urge to turn it away from the page recording her damaged interlock.

Pell brought a clerk and two faculty masters. One master set his papers on a folded cloth, clear of the damp tabletop.

“The university remains responsible for a connected system,” Pell said. “Central oversight is necessary. A ward cannot make decisions for itself while disregarding the consequences beyond its boundaries.”

Both masters nodded.

“Then write down our obligations,” Ada said. “Beside yours.”

Pell looked at the ledger. The allocation cut, Saye’s confession, the examination, and the measures that had held the lower channels were indexed with their witnesses. His representatives’ objections were there too. Nothing had been removed to make the ward’s account easier to defend.

They began with the matriculation press.

Pell proposed university supervision with resident participation. Tessa heard how little the phrase required of him.

“Who holds the operating credentials?” she asked.

“Properly qualified persons.”

“Selected by whom?”

The discussion lasted until the clerk replaced the sentence. The ward would control the press and hold its operating credentials, appointing operators qualified under the agreed standards. University inspectors would retain access for scheduled safety examinations, but inspection would not confer authority to take possession.

Tessa read the clause twice. It gave them control without pretending that control made competence unnecessary.

The utility allocation took longer.

Saye crossed out the word *adequate*.

“Put in the figures,” he said.

“Conditions vary,” one master objected. “You cannot bind a supply system to yesterday’s weather.”

Ada spread the inlet records across his draft.

“Then account for changing conditions. Here are the levels. Here is the power required to keep every pump running against them. Your own instruments gave us these readings.”

The master bent over the figures. For several minutes, only the machinery and the scratching of his pencil disturbed the room.

They attached a schedule guaranteeing a metered allocation to the ward, with a protected flood-safety minimum based on the recorded operating requirements. Any proposed reduction had to be entered in the public record and reviewed against those standards before authorization. An unavoidable emergency interruption required an immediate entry stating its cause, followed by the same safety review.

“Who sees the meter?” Saye asked.

“Authorized inspectors,” Pell said.

“Residents,” Ada said. “And your inspectors.”

That, too, went into the agreement. Readings would be posted each shift, and the meter and operating records would be open to inspection under the station’s safety rules. Neither party could quietly replace an inconvenient figure.

When they reached examinations, the faculty masters recovered some of their confidence.

“Standards cannot be lowered to ratify an occupation,” one said.

Tessa felt heat climb her neck. Her hands were still rough from cleaning the replacement assembly; someone else had inspected it afterward. She had resented that for half a morning before recognizing how badly she needed the check.

“Use the published standard for me,” she said. “If I fail, record why.”

Examinations would be announced openly, with requirements published in advance. Observers could attend. Questions, practical tasks, results, and reasons for decisions would be documented. The faculty retained its examiners, but not the right to conceal the standard or change it during a test.

Pell insisted that neighboring districts could not depend upon the ward’s goodwill alone.

Ada agreed. Essential drainage and utility service would continue across ward boundaries, with duties and maintenance schedules entered beside the allocation figures. The ward would not be entitled to withhold those services during a dispute.

“We would be flooding people who had no hand in this,” she said.

Pell studied her, then instructed the clerk to retain the sentence establishing that obligation.

By the time the copies were ready, the light had shifted from the tabletop to the wall. One faculty master still objected to the inspection provisions. Pell heard him out without reopening them.

He signed first.

Ada and the appointed ward representatives signed beneath him. Saye checked every attached schedule against the figures they had negotiated. Tessa checked that the press clause had survived the copying unchanged.

No one cheered. From below came a request for another pair of hands, and Ada sent a resident who had finished witnessing the signatures.

One copy went under glass beside the pump-station entrance. Tessa accompanied Saye to the faculty keep with the other. The porter examined Pell’s signature before allowing them to post it on the public notice board. Students passed behind them beneath the stone arch; a faculty master stopped to read, his mouth tightening at the inspection clause.

The university still controlled the main supply. Its examiners would still judge candidates. Those powers had not vanished with a signature.

Back at the station, a resident was comparing the latest meter reading with the posted schedule. Tessa waited while he found the protected minimum.

For now, the supply stood above it.

She entered the time beside the number and handed him the pencil to witness it.

The resident took the pencil and set his mark beside the reading. Above the pump-station door, the signed accord rested under glass; below it, the first shift gathered around the board to learn what its figures required of them.

No one treated the minimum as a promise that the water would behave. They checked the meter against the posted allocation, then checked the check against the channel gauge. When the readings disagreed, the crew took them again. Ada Fen stood with them, sleeves rolled to her elbows, listening as the mechanics described a change in the western gate’s vibration.

Her degree gave her authority over the connected channels: she could order a gate adjusted when its movement threatened the whole system, and direct water through routes maintained by more than one district. It did not give her a better ear for a failing bearing, or make a stubborn valve obey. She asked the crew to show her what they had heard.

The oldest mechanic tapped the housing with two knuckles. A dull note answered, low and uneven. Ada leaned close, then nodded. They reduced the gate’s draw by a fraction while another worker watched the downstream gauge. The number steadied. Ada entered the adjustment in the log; the mechanic entered his observation beside it and signed.

For the first days, service returned by increments. Crews resumed regular shifts, clearing silt from drainage runs and testing the utility lines that carried water across the ward boundaries. At each change of watch, the outgoing crew explained not only what had been done, but what remained uncertain. The accord required maintenance schedules and duties to be recorded. The workers made those records more useful than a list of completed tasks: they noted strain, delay, and the odd sound that might become a break if left alone.

Ada moved between the station and the connected channels, carrying the same ledger the crews used. When a reading changed, she asked who had taken it and under what conditions. She never asked a worker to leave the controls so that she could stand in his place. In the flood, the ward had been saved by a system of knowledge assembled from many kinds of attention. She would not make its return depend on one person’s authority.

The first examination standards were posted a week later beside the accord. Ada had helped write them, but the pages bore more than her name. Candidates would be expected to explain the principles governing flow and pressure, then demonstrate that they could take a reliable reading, identify a faulty instrument, and respond to a changing channel without endangering connected services. The standards specified what an examiner must disclose, how a result could be challenged, and where the public record would be kept.

Under them hung the crews’ practical account of the flood defense. It described the order in which the gates had been adjusted, the minimum flow maintained for neighboring districts, and the points where workers had found the gauges misleading. Beside each instruction stood the conditions under which it had worked. No one had written that the same measures would answer every flood.

Tessa read the account slowly. She knew which entries came from hurried hands during the worst hours and which had been amended once there was light enough to see the marks. She found one omitted time and brought it to Ada, who added it without complaint. That evening, people stopped to read the pages on their way to and from the pumps. A young worker traced the route on a maintenance map with one finger.

At the inquiry, Saye sat before the panel with his hands flat on the table. He explained that he had altered findings to make the danger appear less certain than it was. He spoke of pressure from the faculty and of his own fear of losing his position, but neither explanation softened the fact that his changes had concealed what the measurements showed. The panel asked when he had understood the consequences.

“Before the flood,” he said. “Not soon enough to act honestly. Soon enough that I knew I was choosing not to.”

His work on the accord was entered into the record: he had checked the schedules against the figures and helped establish terms the university could enforce. So was the damage his earlier deception had done. No one called the ward’s recovery proof of his innocence. When the hearing ended, Saye left by the side door, and the panel kept his findings for further review.

Lio remained a mechanics officer. He submitted his challenge to the evacuation order through the proper register, attaching the crew’s readings and the times they had been taken. His argument was narrow and difficult to dismiss: the order had not accounted for the protected minimum, and the recorded conditions did not justify sending every resident away from the pumps at the moment the ward most needed trained hands.

He did not claim that evacuation was never necessary. He asked that future orders state who had assessed the readings, what evidence supported the decision, and how crews could report a contradiction without being treated as insubordinate. When a clerk asked whether he meant to challenge his superiors, Lio looked down at the signed log.

“I mean to challenge the order,” he said. “That is why I put my name under it.”

Pell had powerful faculty supporters, and he spoke to them in the upper hall with the accord’s pages spread across a table. The agreement, he insisted, was temporary: an emergency arrangement that could not settle the university’s lasting authority over supply and examination. His supporters nodded at the word temporary.

Below, two residents from neighboring districts waited by the notice board. They had brought a written request for public examinations of their own. Ada read it, then called over the workers who had maintained the boundary channels. The renewed service was holding. The standards had been published. The questions had begun to travel beyond the ward, where the answers would have to be tested in public.

By the time the first requests from neighboring districts reached the notice board, Foundry Ward had become a precedent the university could not ignore. Its pumps were running, its boundary channels holding, and its safety standards available to anyone who entered the ward office. Residents of Coil Street, the North Quays, and the low houses beyond the tannery asked how their own examinations might be opened to public inspection. The requests differed in spelling and urgency, but each asked the same question: who would be allowed to judge the people whose work kept its water moving?

Pell came to Tessa on a gray morning when rain pressed soot against the windows and turned the yard to black glass. He carried a narrow case.

“The university wishes to recognize your service,” he said.

“How?”

“With an honorary degree in hydraulics. It would restore your standing and bring your work under the university’s proper authority.”

Tessa did not touch the case. “Would it qualify me to sit an examination?”

“An honorary degree is not a qualification earned by examination. It is a distinction.”

The distinction exposed the fastening beneath his courtesy. The university could call her a scholar without testing her knowledge, praise her labor, and draw it back under the authority that had once treated her unfinished studies as proof of incapacity.

“I will not accept a degree for work I have not examined,” she said. “I left my study before completing it. If I return, I will return as a student.”

Pell looked toward the pump house. Its pistons struck in a steady rhythm through the brick. “You are choosing difficulty.”

“I am choosing the ordinary road.”

He left the case unopened. Tessa returned it that afternoon.

The refusal settled nothing, but it clarified what she would do. For several nights she copied old course lists, searched the ward library for hydraulics manuals, and wrote to the university registrar. She secured a place in the evening lectures under the ordinary student register. She would pay the fee, submit written work, attend practical demonstrations, and sit the examinations without exemption. A clerk entered her name without praise beside younger students’ names. The lack of ceremony steadied her.

By day she kept repairing the ward’s pumps.

The next public examination took place in the covered loading court, where rain drummed on the roof and coal smoke mingled with wet iron. Ada had posted the standards behind glass. They specified the required calculations, the permitted error in pressure readings, the handling of emergency gates, and the conditions requiring an examiner to repeat a test.

Before the candidates arrived, Tessa inspected the brass gauges, compared the water columns with their marked levels, and tested the certified weights on the balance. One calibration seal had worn thin.

“Record that defect,” she told Ada. “Observers must be able to verify the readings. They cannot be asked to trust us because our faces are familiar.”

Ada called two residents from the machine crews to witness the check. Tessa opened each instrument case, read the serial numbers aloud, and explained which marks belonged to the last calibration. When a university examiner objected to recording the procedure, she answered calmly.

“The standards permit observation. They do not permit private instruments.”

She did not examine the candidates. When a young mechanic from the North Quays asked whether his solution to a flow problem was correct, she directed him to the examiner’s table.

“I can tell you whether the gauge works,” she said. “The judgment is theirs.”

At noon, Ada went to the main pump and pressed her ear to its casing. The metal was warm. Beneath the yard’s clatter, she found the piston’s even beat.

“The channel is stable,” she reported.

Using her degree authority, she confirmed staffing for every connected pump. No station would be left unattended while the examination continued.

Representatives from other districts watched through the open gate. They saw the standards, the instruments, and the signatures entered in the register. They could not yet control the university’s main supply, whose gates remained under faculty seal, and their requests for similar examinations still required approval.

That limit mattered to Tessa. The accord had not transferred authority everywhere; it had made authority answerable in one ward. Knowledge could still be guarded as property, but here the people who depended on the pumps could inspect how competence was measured. She returned that evening to her hydraulics text with oil beneath her fingernails and the ward’s machinery beating steadily through the walls.
