The first clock stopped while I was apologizing to it.

Its brass foot had caught my sleeve, and I had knocked it against the workbench. “Sorry,” I whispered, although the shop was empty and the clock could hardly object. At twenty-two, I still apologized to furniture more easily than I spoke to customers. I steadied its little lion-shaped feet, straightened the cuff that hid my bitten nails, and reached for the winding key.

Every clock in the shop struck once.

Then their hands began moving backward.

Beyond the window, rain climbed from the pavement into the clouds. A woman outside lifted an umbrella that folded itself into a bud. Behind me, the kettle swallowed its own whistle.

I kept hold of the key. It was a small, ordinary object, and I needed one.

Something knocked from inside the mirror above the workbench.

My reflection remained where it was: narrow shoulders, dark braid, the brown dress I wore because nobody remembered brown. But another figure stood behind it, visible only in the glass. He looked like a man assembled from damp paper. Water threaded down his pale coat without reaching the floor.

“Do not turn around,” he said. “It is easier if you let me keep a shape.”

I turned anyway. There was nobody behind me.

When I looked back, he smiled.

“You have been difficult to find, Death.”

“That isn’t my name.”

“No. Your mother called you Mara. A sensible precaution.”

The key slipped from my fingers. Before it struck the floor, it stopped beside my knee. All around it, dust hung motionless, each mote holding a tiny, impossible landscape: a bed beneath white sheets, a wrecked boat, a fox curled under snow.

I backed against the bench.

“What are those?”

“The departures you have refused to see.”

The dust opened.

Souls streamed through the shop in pale spirals, passing through shelves and clock faces. Some retained hands or mouths; others were only a particular blue, the smell of bread, the pressure of someone waiting beside a door. They circled me until my braid lifted from my back. Their sorrow pressed against my teeth.

Underneath it lay a recognition so deep it frightened me more than the stranger.

I knew where they wanted to go.

“Stop,” I whispered.

The spirals halted.

The paper man bowed.

I shook my head, but something in me had already answered. I could feel an opening beyond the walls, vast and quiet, and the souls leaning toward it. I had spent my life edging around people, afraid of being noticed. Now every dead thing in the room was waiting for me.

A moth lay beneath the lamp, where I had found it that morning. Its crumpled wings stirred without moving. Above its body trembled a scrap of silver light.

Without knowing why, I cupped my hand beneath it.

“You can go,” I said.

The silver thread passed through my palm. For an instant I felt summer darkness, warm brick, a flower opening somewhere unseen. Then it slipped into the quiet beyond the room.

The certainty left me shaking.

“I’m Death.”

“Yes,” said the spirit. “And mankind needs you.”

He touched the mirror. Its surface cleared, revealing the narrow passage beside the shop. A little girl crouched beneath the awning, wrapping a stray cat in her scarf. I recognized her. She sometimes brought broken watches nobody had asked her to mend.

“Her name is Elsie,” he said. “She must die before the clocks resume.”

I stared at him.

“She hasn’t done anything.”

“Her innocence is the requirement. One unspent life to mend the breach. Otherwise the souls you see will remain here, and every living soul will be torn loose to join them.”

In the mirror, the girl rubbed the cat’s ears.

“No.”

The word barely reached the glass.

The spirit leaned closer. “Refuse, and you will lose your borrowed body first. No hands. No voice. Only the hunger of your office, forever.”

I gripped my elbows. I wanted my room above the shop, its chipped basin, the blanket still shaped around where I had slept. I wanted breakfast and the chance to grow less timid. How quickly those small wants became reasons to consider something monstrous.

“What do I have to do?” I asked, and hated the relief that crossed his face.

“Touch her throat. Invite her out.”

A soul drifted between us: an elderly woman with a bright thread trailing from her chest. The spirit caught it and pushed it behind his coat. Her soundless mouth opened.

I looked down at his hand.

“You said they were trapped.”

“They are.”

“But you can hold them.”

His smile thinned. “Do not confuse delay with mercy.”

I reached toward the woman. The spirit recoiled before my fingers touched him.

The clocks shuddered. Somewhere outside, a single raindrop fell.

He was afraid of my hand.

I drew it back slowly, keeping myself between him and the silver faces.

“I won’t touch the child,” I said. My voice shook badly enough that I had to repeat it. “Show me the breach.”

Behind his coat, the woman’s bright thread strained toward me. I held out my palm.


The woman’s bright thread brushed my palm, then pulled taut. The spirit had wound its other end around two fingers.

“Let her go,” I said.

“You asked to see the breach.”

“I’m still asking.”

For a moment neither of us moved. My arm ached from holding it out. I was accustomed to surrendering in smaller contests: stepping aside on stairs, accepting the wrong change, laughing when someone mistook my silence for agreement. Now I kept my hand raised while the woman trembled between us, her face folding into the face of a girl and back again.

The spirit loosened his fingers.

She flowed toward me, cold as a wet sleeve. I could feel the quiet place waiting beyond the walls, but when I tried to guide her toward it, something tightened across my wrist. Her thread led past my hand, into the mirror.

The moth had been small enough to slip through. This woman was caught.

I stopped pulling.

“There,” the spirit said. “Even you cannot release them. The child is the price.”

I cradled the trembling light against my apron. Behind the glass, his mouth had become a thin crease.

“Then show me what I would be paying.”

He raised one papery hand. In the mirror, Elsie and the cat vanished beneath a bloom of darkness. A red seam appeared, wavering down the glass.

I stepped closer. Souls revolved above my shoulders, shedding scraps of things I almost recognized: a wedding veil, a burnt crust, the sharp green scent of cut stems. Within their turning, the shop changed around me. New varnish gleamed on the counter, then peeled. A chair collapsed into worm-eaten sticks and stood whole again. The clocks stayed silent through it all.

The red seam did not move.

I shifted sideways.

Neither did it.

“That isn’t inside the reflection,” I said.

The spirit lowered his hand. “Distance has little meaning here.”

But the seam crossed a tiny blemish in the silvering. I knew that blemish; I had polished around it often enough. Whatever he had shown me lay against the back of the mirror.

I put the woman’s thread gently over my wrist and reached for the frame.

“Don’t.”

His voice cracked like a ruler across a desk. I flinched. My fingers struck the wood instead of taking hold, and shame warmed my face.

Then he added, softly, “You would not survive what is behind me, Mara.”

My mother’s name for me hurt worse in his mouth than Death had.

I wanted to believe him. Obedience offered such a narrow, simple road: go outside, touch a child, keep my hands and my voice and the small life I understood. The horror would come afterward. For one cowardly instant, afterward seemed very far away.

Outside the window, Elsie remained beneath the awning. The cat’s paw rested against her wrist.

I fetched a screwdriver from the bench.

The spirit watched me cross the shop. “There are more lives at stake than hers.”

“I heard you.”

“And your own.”

“I heard that too.”

The screws fastening the mirror’s lower edge resisted. I had to brace the handle against my hip, an awkward, ordinary effort that steadied me. When the first screw came free, the spirit’s coat fluttered though there was no wind. The trapped woman tightened around my wrist.

I left the upper brackets in place. The mirror was too heavy to lift alone, and I would not risk breaking it with her caught inside.

Instead, I took the screwdriver and the work lamp through the narrow doorway beside the shelves, into the rear storeroom. Its brick wall was the back of the wall that held the mirror. I had stacked packing crates there myself. Now I dragged them aside, scraping my knuckles on a nail.

The lamp cast no light until I gripped its cold shade. Then a weak yellow circle spread over the bricks.

A split ran down the plaster.

Red light seeped through it, matching the seam in the glass. Near the floor, a strip of pale material had been pushed deep into the opening. I crouched. There were folds in it, precise as the folds at the spirit’s cuffs.

His voice came through the wall.

“Old repairs. Leave them.”

I touched the strip with the screwdriver.

From the shop came a dry, involuntary gasp.

I drew back immediately. The woman’s thread trembled, but remained intact. Behind the plaster I heard a multitude shifting, like people crowded on the other side of a locked door.

“You’re in it,” I whispered.

Silence.

I set the lamp down, carefully, so my shaking hand would not overturn it. I did not know whether he had made the breach or merely lodged himself there. But he had shown me a wound while hiding his own grip upon it.

I unwound the woman’s thread from my wrist and held it loosely between two fingers.

“I’m going to look,” I told her.

Then I slid the screwdriver beneath the edge of the plaster, keeping its point well clear of the pale strip, and began to lift.


The plaster lifted in a curved flake, exposing a darkness that the lamp could not enter. I caught the fragment against my knee before it fell. The woman’s thread rested across my other hand, trembling with each scrape of the screwdriver. I waited until it steadied before I set the broken plaster on the floor.

Beneath it, the pale strip crossed the red opening like a bandage pulled too tight. It was not pressed into the crack at random. One edge disappeared through the brickwork; the other divided into narrow folds that gripped the back of the mirror. Between them, silver threads crowded together.

I lowered the screwdriver.

“Those aren’t repairs.”

“They are what stands between you and a catastrophe,” the spirit said through the wall.

I leaned nearer without touching anything. Inside the split, souls turned in a slow, constricted spiral. A shoulder became a cloud of white seeds; a face opened into a staircase upon which tiny figures climbed without advancing. Their movement made my eyes ache. Around them, the red light swelled and thinned, revealing brick, then uncut stone, then the roots of a tree that had never grown through this storeroom. The pale folds remained unchanged.

The woman beside me made a small sound. Her thread ran through a crease near the bottom.

I had nearly tried to widen the opening by pulling away its edge. Now I saw what that would do: tighten the fold around her.

I put the screwdriver down.

“Tell me how to loosen it.”

“Kill the child. Elsie must die before the clocks resume. I have told you the price.”

“You told me she would mend the breach.”

“And she will.”

“Then why is this holding the woman?”

No answer came. The lamp hummed beneath my hand, an ordinary electrical sound so welcome that I almost cried. Beyond the storeroom doorway, none of the clocks ticked. I tried to imagine the silence spreading beyond the shop: kitchens, railway platforms, bedsides. Perhaps he had told the truth about that much. Perhaps every moment I spent distrusting him endangered people I would never see.

My knuckles had begun to lose their color.

I turned my hand toward the lamp. Through the scraped skin I could see a faint yellow glow.

“There,” he whispered. “Your body is already failing.”

I clenched my fist. The light disappeared, though whether because my flesh had returned or because my fingers hid it, I could not tell. I wanted to run upstairs and wrap myself in my blanket until I could feel warm again. Instead I crouched beside the thread, ashamed of how fiercely I wanted to live.

“If you know how to save me,” I said, “you can answer a question.”

“You cannot bargain with necessity.”

“I’m asking you.”

My voice dwindled on the last word. I let it. He had heard me.

I moved the lamp sideways, dragging its cord around the crate I had shifted earlier. From the new angle, a shadow appeared beneath the lowest fold. The material did not lie flat against the mirror. There was space behind it, scarcely wide enough for a fingernail.

I remembered the spirit recoiling from my hand.

Keeping the woman’s thread loose, I brought one finger toward the fold.

“Stop.”

I stopped just short of touching it. Through the wall came a faint dragging sound. The fold withdrew a fraction, and the woman’s thread slipped lower.

I pulled my hand back. The material settled again.

“You can move it,” I said.

“You are hurting me.”

“I haven’t touched you.”

The silence afterward felt different from his earlier refusals. I stared at the pale strip until my eyes watered. It had the same tiny ridges as his cuffs, the same damp sheen. Whatever else lay in the breach, part of what held these souls belonged to him.

I could not simply tear it free. The woman’s thread was caught beneath it, and dozens more shone behind hers.

I took a thin wooden packing slat from the open crate. Its end was rough, so I rubbed it against the floor until the splinters wore away. Then I slid its smooth tip into the small space beneath the fold—not lifting, only keeping that space from closing.

The spirit gasped.

The woman’s thread slackened across my palm.

I held still. Her face sharpened beside my shoulder: deeply lined, mouth parted, eyes fixed on the opening. She was not free, but she was no longer being pulled against my hand.

“I’m leaving that there,” I told him.

“You have no idea what you are doing.”

“No,” I said. “So I’m going to look from the other side before I do anything else.”

I set the lamp where it illuminated the slat and rose carefully, carrying the woman’s loose thread. At the doorway I looked back to make certain the wood had not shifted. Then I stepped from the storeroom into the shop.

The mirror still hung from its upper brackets. The spirit stood within it, one cuff drawn tight against his wrist. I approached the workbench, keeping clear of the suspended winding key.

“Move your hand,” I said.

His fingers closed over the cuff.


His fingers closed over the cuff.

I had expected defiance, but the spirit’s hand blurred at the edges, as if it were being pulled through deep water. The cuff tightened. In the rear storeroom, the wooden slat gave a small wooden creak.

The woman’s thread jerked across my palm.

I caught it before it could draw taut. “Move your hand,” I repeated.

“You think a scrap of wood makes you powerful?”

“No.” I swallowed. “I think it proves you’re afraid of something.”

The mirror clouded around him. Behind his shoulder, the reflection of the shop stretched into a corridor lined with clocks. Their hands spun in opposite directions, racing toward noon and midnight at once. None made a sound. At the corridor’s far end, a pale figure stood with its back turned. It was too tall to be Elsie and too narrow to be human, though its head tilted as if listening.

The spirit pulled his cuff free.

In the storeroom, the slat snapped.

The woman’s thread went rigid. I stumbled against the workbench, and the suspended winding key swung inches from my cheek. A gust of cold passed through the shop, carrying the smell of wet earth and extinguished candles. Every loose paper lifted from the counter and hung in the air. Souls poured from the mirror’s blackened edge, not escaping but circling: hundreds of translucent bodies turning around one another like fish inside a glass planet.

I pressed the woman’s thread against my wrist. “Hold on.”

Her answer was a pressure, faint but deliberate.

The spirit raised both hands. “You are making this worse.”

“You moved the fold.”

“I moved nothing.”

“Then why did it break when you pulled away?”

His reflection smiled, but the expression arrived a moment too late. I saw the mouth remain still while the smile crossed it afterward, like ink spreading through water.

“Because the breach is hungry,” he said. “It consumes whatever steadies it.”

“And you are what steadies it?”

“I am the one preventing it from opening.”

The words seemed to settle into the walls. Behind me, the storeroom bricks groaned. A red glow brightened in the storeroom doorway, and the trapped souls crowded toward it. Their faces flashed in the swirling mass—old, young, laughing, terrified—then dissolved into ribbons of silver. The shop lengthened. The workbench slid away from me without moving, its legs multiplying across the floor. For one breath I stood in the store as it had been before I inherited it, with my mother’s blue coat hanging beside the door. In the next, the ceiling sagged under years that had not yet happened.

I shut my eyes.

When I opened them, the workbench was close again.

The clocks had not resumed.

That fact was the only solid thing left.

“Elsie,” I said. “You claim she has to die before time can start. If she is the price, why show her to me as a shadow? Why not bring her in here?”

“Because you would hesitate.”

“I am hesitating now.”

“You are Death. Hesitation is a luxury for the living.”

The title struck through me. I had been trying not to remember the first time I discovered what lived in my hands: the dead bird under the hedge, the thread rising from its breast; the old man on the pavement whose last breath had turned into a door; the way every soul in the world seemed to recognize me before I recognized myself. I had thought Death would feel like a crown or a weapon. Instead it felt like being the only person in a crowded room who knew the floor was collapsing.

I stepped around the bench and took the mirror’s lower frame. The wood was cold enough to sting.

“I won’t kill an innocent because you say the world will end.”

The spirit’s face lost its borrowed softness. “Then the world will end because you wanted to remain a girl.”

The accusation found its mark. My hand tightened on the frame. Somewhere outside, under the awning, Elsie might still be waiting with the cat beside her. I pictured her narrow shoulders and the way she had looked at me—not with trust exactly, but with the frightened patience of someone offering another person the chance to be kind.

The mirror shuddered.

A hairline crack spread from the blemish in its silvering. Through it, I saw not the spirit but a room turned upside down. Pale folds covered the ceiling. Threads dangled from them, each ending in a soul caught mid-motion. At the center, a dark knot pulsed like a heart.

The spirit lunged toward the crack.

I seized the winding key before he could reach it and held it against the glass. The key began to turn by itself. A single click sounded.

Not a clock restarting. A lock giving way.

The spirit recoiled. In the storeroom, the woman’s thread pulled taut again, but remained intact.

I kept the key pressed to the mirror, though my fingers had begun to glow through the skin.

“Tell me what’s behind the glass,” I said.

The spirit stared at me.

“Tell me,” I whispered, “or I open it without you.”

“It is a holding chamber,” the spirit said.

The answer came so quietly that I nearly lost it beneath the groaning bricks. I kept the winding key against the glass. Through its open bow, I could see my fingers shining like candle wax held before a flame.

“Holding whom?”

“Those who cannot pass while you refuse your office.”

The woman’s thread cut a shallow groove across my wrist. I eased my hand toward the mirror, following the pull rather than resisting it, and the pressure lessened. Behind the crack, one of the suspended souls turned. Her face appeared upside down among the pale folds: the same lined cheeks, the same parted mouth.

“You mean the people caught in your sleeves.”

His cuff twitched.

I wanted him to deny it convincingly. I wanted someone older, someone certain, to take the key from me and explain that there was a proper procedure. Even now, with his lie visible between us, I dreaded sounding foolish more readily than I understood how to be Death.

“Move aside,” I said.

“If you enter, you may not come back.”

My hand stopped. He watched it stop.

“You still have a body,” he said. “There is still something here to save. Give me Elsie, and you need never see what becomes of you without it.”

For a moment I imagined agreeing. The shame of it was physical, hot beneath my ribs. I could go upstairs. I could have cold feet and an unpaid bill and a door I could close. Beyond that small, ordinary future stood a child whose death I would have chosen.

I looked toward the shop window.

The suspended papers obscured most of it. I bent until I could see beneath a curled invoice. Through the lower pane, under the awning, Elsie sat against the wall with her knees drawn up. The cat’s paw rested against her wrist. Neither moved, but they had not vanished into some invented shadow. A raindrop hung from the awning above them, swollen and still.

“She’s outside,” I said, mostly to myself.

“Alive,” the spirit answered. “For now. Think of everyone else.”

I turned back. “I am.”

The key shifted beneath my fingers. The crack widened without breaking further, its edges drawing apart like lips reluctant to speak. Cold air touched my face. It smelled of the storeroom’s damp mortar, not of some distant world. Whatever the chamber was, it lay against the shop closely enough to pull the woman through its walls.

I could not drag her thread across the threshold blind.

Keeping the key in place, I lowered myself beside the bench and followed the shining strand with my free hand. It passed toward the storeroom door, then disappeared through the wall at ankle height. Inside the mirror, its continuation descended from a pale crease near the opening. Between those points, it made an impossible bend.

I drew a little slack toward me. The woman’s suspended face lifted.

“There,” I whispered. “I’m still holding you.”

A small answering pressure touched my palm.

The spirit reached toward the key.

I flinched, but did not surrender it. Light spread across my knuckles, and he withdrew with a hiss. Behind him, a strip of pale material tightened across the chamber ceiling. The woman’s thread jerked.

“Keep your hands where I can see them.”

“You cannot command this place.”

“Then stop obeying.”

He went very still.

I took a broad brass tray from the workbench and set it beneath the thread, lifting its smooth rim until the strand lay supported instead of dragging against the mirror’s fractured edge. It was an absurdly domestic precaution. I tested it twice anyway. When I moved my wrist, the thread slid freely.

Only then did I turn the key another fraction.

The mirror opened inward.

Beyond it, the inverted room slowly rolled until its floor met the shop floor. Souls swung overhead in widening circles, their silver wakes twisting together. Around one turning face, a summer orchard flowered and shed its fruit; around another, a bed grew empty, filled, empty again. Those little seasons whirled without advancing a single clock behind me.

The dark knot remained at the center.

Pale bands entered it from every direction. One stretched toward the spirit’s cuff.

I watched him pull his wrist close to his chest. The band drew tight at the same instant.

“That is what I asked you about,” I said.

“The thing that will tear mankind apart if you fail.”

It was another claim. I had no way yet to measure it against the trembling floor.

I pushed the tray forward until its edge rested inside the chamber, then carried the woman’s slack thread over it. My shoe crossed the sill. The floor held. I shifted my weight, brought my other foot through, and stood within the mirror, one hand still gripping the key behind me.

The spirit stayed beside the opening.

I could still see Elsie through the shop window beyond his shoulder. I could still feel the woman answering through the strand.

The dark knot pulsed. This close, I saw a loop buried beneath its outer bands—a place where the threads entered but did not emerge.

I steadied my trembling knees and moved nearer.


The next step pulled my arm straight behind me. I stopped before the key could wrench from my fingers. The chamber was larger than it had looked through the glass, and I could not reach its center while clinging to the lock.

I glanced back. The spirit stood beyond the sill, his hands raised where I had told him to keep them.

“Does it shut if I take the key?”

“You should have asked before you entered.”

I turned the key a fraction backward. The mirror’s edge shifted toward the brass tray. Immediately I turned it forward again, and the edge retreated. The woman’s thread slid safely over the rim, but my stomach clenched. I had nearly pinched it merely by asking the mechanism a question.

Beyond the spirit, Elsie remained beneath the awning. The cat’s paw still rested against her wrist. I fixed that small, unmoving picture in my mind, then examined the lock instead of his face.

A notch held the key’s shaft when I pressed it upward. I eased my grip. It stayed. Only after testing it twice did I let go.

“You are leaving your only way out unattended,” he said.

“Yes,” I whispered. I hated that he could hear how frightened I was.

I crouched inside the threshold and pushed the tray farther across the floor, paying out the slack I had gathered. Keeping the suspended woman’s strand against my palm, I advanced beside it on my knees. The stone felt wet, although my skirt remained dry. Beneath its translucent surface, staircases descended into rooms no larger than teeth.

Overhead, souls revolved through each other’s unfinished moments. A man opened his mouth, and snow poured silently from it; a girl’s plait unraveled into a procession of autumn trees. Their silver wakes brushed my hair. Each touch brought an urge to rise and follow, to shed the troublesome weight of knees and lungs.

Was that what fulfilling my office meant? Losing the young woman who still apologized when strangers stepped on her feet?

I pressed my free hand against the floor until its cold hurt.

At the knot, the woman’s thread divided around a ridge of dark material. Not divided, I realized, leaning closer. Doubled back. The strand entered the buried loop, curved around something inside, and returned beneath itself, so closely laid that I had mistaken two lengths for one.

“I won’t pull,” I told her.

“You must not touch that,” the spirit said.

“Put your hand back.”

“The chamber is failing. You feel it. Every moment you spend here brings the breach closer to the living world.”

“Then help me loosen this.”

“There is no loosening it. Elsie’s life will close it. One innocent death, freely chosen by Death, against all mankind. Must I keep teaching you arithmetic?”

My fingers went numb. He made it sound so simple, and I wanted simple with an ugliness that frightened me. One act, and perhaps the floor would become stone again. Perhaps I could return to the shop and never hear a soul caught between breaths.

“You haven’t shown me how her death closes anything.”

“You cannot expect to understand before you act.”

“I can expect you not to move your wrist.”

He stopped. The band stopped with him.

The pale strip crossed the seam without entering it. I traced its course with my eyes, careful not to disturb either it or the woman’s thread.

The spirit’s band passed through the buried loop.

Then it returned toward his cuff.

“You’re drawing it shut,” I said.

“I am holding it together.”

“Those aren’t necessarily the same thing.”

The spirit stepped toward the opening.

“Stay there.”

My voice cracked, but I kept my hand steady. I shifted onto one knee beside the knot, close enough now to see the lip of the buried loop. A hard edge lay under the bands, separating the threads from the split plaster. I slipped my smallest finger beneath that edge.

The souls overhead slowed.

At the doorway, the spirit’s cuff drew tight against his wrist.

I held the tiny space I had made and looked directly at him.

“Now open your hand.”


His thumb lifted first. The other fingers remained bent, their shadows hooked against the doorway.

“All of it,” I said.

The words sounded rude. Even there, with a woman hanging above me by a thread and the floor full of impossible staircases, I felt the old impulse to soften them. Please, perhaps. If you wouldn’t mind. My smallest finger ached beneath the buried edge. I let the ache keep me silent.

He spread his hand.

“No,” I said. “It lets me look.”

Behind the bands, the plaster had bulged outward in a shallow blister. A flake had already lifted from the wall. I touched its corner with my thumbnail. The woman’s strand had been caught between that rim and the band.

I swallowed. I had been holding her thread away from a pinch point, not holding the chamber together. That was all I knew. It was enough to change where I put my weight.

“It anchors what would otherwise escape.”

“The souls?”

“The breach.”

His answer left the two impossibly tangled. I wanted to sit back and make him explain until one clean truth emerged. But the woman’s strand trembled against my skin, and my cramped finger was beginning to slip.

Overhead, the souls gathered speed. Their wakes wound around me, silver ribbons carrying the smell of extinguished candles. My hand looked briefly transparent. Beyond it I saw my own wrist as a narrow shape of winter light, without bone or blood.

I could let go of being a frightened young woman. I felt how easy it might be to rise among them and become only the thing they recognized.

Then my cramped knee throbbed. I clung to that small, undignified pain.

“You are exhausting the body you wish to keep,” the spirit said. “Elsie need not suffer. Choose her, and mankind survives. You survive.”

My mouth filled with saliva. I hated that the offer still reached me. I had discovered I was Death, but no knowledge had arrived to tell me whether refusal was courage or cowardice.

I looked back through the opening. Beyond the shop, Elsie remained beneath the awning, the cat’s paw against her wrist.

“Show me the connection,” I said. “Not another promise.”

“The connection is beyond your understanding.”

“Then you haven’t given me a reason to kill her.”

He lowered his open hand slightly. I followed it with my eyes until he stopped.

Using my thumb, I eased the woman’s thread toward the widest part of the iron eye. It moved a little, then caught. I stopped at once. Pulling would only tighten its return around the hidden band.

Instead, I leaned closer to the exposed plaster and traced the route without touching it. The band entered the iron eye, passed behind the pale strip, and disappeared into the black seam. Its return lay just beneath it, stretched toward the spirit’s cuff.

There was space beside those two lengths. Barely enough for a fingertip.

I shifted my supporting palm beneath the woman’s thread and settled both knees firmly on the stone. The iron rim remained lifted. Her thread remained intact.

“Keep your hand open,” I told him.

Then I set my free fingertip beside the seam, where I could begin to feel which way the hidden band ran.


The seam was colder than the stone around it. I pressed my fingertip beside the hidden band and felt a pulse travel through the plaster—not a heartbeat, but a repetition of one. The chamber shivered, then returned to stillness. Above me, the souls lurched in their silver spiral. Their faces blurred as if time had become water and someone had stirred it with a long spoon.

The band ran downward.

I followed it with my eyes, not my hand. It vanished beneath the iron fitting, emerged below the eye, and descended toward the floor in a narrow black line. I had mistaken the line for a crack because it held no reflection. When I leaned nearer, I saw tiny marks stamped along its edge: loops, hooks, and short bars arranged like the teeth of a language.

The spirit’s open hand twitched.

“Do not touch the seam,” he said.

“Why?”

“It is carrying the pressure.”

“From where?”

He did not answer. His silence traveled through the chamber more distinctly than his voice had. I looked toward the doorway. Behind him, the key still held the entrance open. Through the shifting rectangle of glass, Elsie stood under the awning with the cat’s paw resting against her wrist. The shop beyond them wavered between several arrangements: shelves crowded with clocks, shelves bare, shelves drowned beneath black water. Elsie remained in every version.

The band trembled again.

This time, the suspended woman cried out. The sound came from above and below at once. Her thread tightened across my palm, and the doubled length tried to slide back into the iron eye.

“I need more slack,” I said.

“You have enough.”

“You’re pulling it.”

“I am preventing collapse.”

I glanced at his cuff. The band connected to him visibly now, crossing the threshold in a taut diagonal. It entered the chamber through the doorway, passed around the iron fitting, and returned along the floor toward the black seam. The spirit was not merely holding the chamber together. He was part of the loop.

I shifted my weight and reached for the tray with my heel. Its brass edge lay just beyond my ankle. I hooked it and drew it closer by inches. The loose thread pooled into the tray, pale as moonlit hair. Each movement made the trapped woman descend slightly. I kept my palm beneath her strand so it never touched the stone.

The spirit’s fingers began to close.

“Open your hand.”

His thumb pressed against his palm. The band tightened. The iron rim bit into my little finger, and my vision filled with white sparks.

“Open it,” I said again, louder.

For one frightened instant he obeyed. The pressure eased enough for me to pull the tray beneath the doubled thread. Then he curled his fingers once more.

The chamber folded.

Not physically. The doorway stretched away, and the floor beneath my knees became a long corridor lined with doors. Each door opened onto the same instant: Elsie beneath the awning, the cat beside her, rain suspended in the air. In one version Elsie turned toward the shop. In another she stepped into the street. In another she was an empty coat hanging beneath the awning.

I shut my eyes. The images were choices being offered, not futures being shown.

“Stop,” I whispered.

The corridor vanished. My forehead touched the stone. I breathed through the nausea, through the terrible temptation to accept the spirit’s arithmetic simply because it had a beginning and an end.

“You see what happens when you delay,” he said.

“No. I see what you can make me see.”

I lifted my head. “If Elsie is the price, why is her thread outside the loop?”

He went still.

That was answer enough to make my chest tighten.

I looked back at the fitting. The trapped woman’s thread entered the iron eye, but Elsie’s did not. Elsie’s pale line ran from the doorway along the outer wall, untouched by the mechanism. The spirit had pointed to her because she was visible, because fear made visible things seem connected.

I slid my finger beneath the lower black band. It resisted, then lifted with a faint metallic scrape. Behind it lay a second strip, nearly white, pressed flat against the plaster. It carried hundreds of tiny branching threads—souls, I realized, each one caught at a different point in time. The spirit’s dark band crossed over them all like a hand closing around a swarm of moths.

“This is not an anchor,” I said. “It’s a clamp.”

His face changed. Not much: the corners of his mouth lost their patience, and the shadows beneath his eyes deepened. But the change was real.

“If you release it, the dead will pour through.”

“Then I’ll release them one at a time.”

I eased the lower band upward and wedged the brass edge of the tray beneath it. The pressure shifted from my finger into the tray. One silver thread slipped free. Somewhere overhead, a soul stopped spinning.

The suspended woman descended another inch, and her thread warmed against my palm.

The spirit stepped across the threshold.

I caught the iron rim with both hands and held it open. “Stay where you are.”

“You cannot control what comes next.”

“No,” I said, my voice shaking. “But I can choose what I refuse to do.”

Behind me, the first freed thread drifted toward the ceiling, carrying a whisper of bells. The remaining souls tightened around the dark band. I bent closer, searching for the next point of release, while the spirit’s shadow crossed the chamber floor and reached for my knees.


The spirit’s shadow touched my knees and stopped there, as if it had met an invisible wall. I felt its cold climb through the fabric of my trousers, searching for a seam in me. I pressed harder on the iron rim. The metal groaned beneath my hands. The freed thread above us trembled like a harp string, then rose through the whirl of souls and vanished into a ceiling that had become transparent. Beyond it, I saw a sky full of unopened doors.

“One,” I said, though no one had asked me to count. “One released.”

The spirit’s fingers flexed at his sides. “And the chamber is already weakening.”

“Then help me.”

“I have been helping you.”

“No. You’ve been holding the knot closed and calling that help.” My voice shook, but it carried farther than I expected. “If you want the chamber stable, keep the loop loose. If you want Elsie dead, keep pretending these are the same thing.”

At the doorway, the glass shone with a pale reflection of Elsie beneath the awning. The image wavered, but she did not. The cat’s paw remained against her wrist. Rain hung around them in separate beads, each bead containing a miniature street and a miniature sky. I could not hear what Elsie said, if she said anything. I saw only her lift her free hand toward the shop window, toward me perhaps, and then lower it when the reflection failed to answer.

The sight pulled at me more sharply than the spirit’s shadow. I wanted to stand. I wanted to cross the chamber, cross the shop, and return to the small life I had inhabited before the dead began recognizing me. I wanted to be the woman who apologized when strangers bumped into her, who kept receipts folded in her coat pocket, who feared making phone calls. That woman had been so defenseless that I had mistaken defenselessness for innocence.

The souls tightened around the clamp.

I moved my right hand from the rim to the brass tray. The shift made the iron lift a fraction less, and the trapped woman’s thread drew taut across my palm. I froze until the pressure eased. Then I slid the tray two inches deeper beneath the dark band. Brass scraped iron. The sound ran through the chamber and returned as a chorus of distant clocks.

The spirit took one step nearer.

I turned my head. “You stay there.”

“You are in no position to command me.”

“Your shadow is in my position.”

For the first time, his gaze dropped to the black shape pooled against my knees. It withdrew by a finger’s width. That small retreat steadied me. He could be resisted. Perhaps not defeated, perhaps not even harmed, but resisted. The discovery felt less like courage than finding a handrail in a stairwell.

I bent over the exposed mechanism. The freed thread had left a narrow gap in the white strip. Beside it, three smaller strands quivered together, braided at their ends. They were not merely caught; they had been tied into a loop that fed back toward the dark seam. I touched the first with the pad of my finger. A rush of sensations filled my hand: warm bread torn open, a child’s red mitten, sunlight on a hospital curtain. None belonged to me, yet grief rose in my throat with the force of memory.

“Those three are joined,” I said.

“Then leave them joined.”

“Why?”

“Because separation will make them fall out of sequence.”

The phrase struck me. “Out of sequence into what?”

The spirit’s silence lengthened.

I drew the tray farther under the clamp, keeping the iron rim lifted with my left hand. The three strands twisted, and the chamber lurched. Above us, the souls reversed direction. Their silver spiral unwound, faces turning young, then old, then faceless. For a breath I saw the suspended woman standing on the floor, free and smiling. Then the image collapsed, and she hung where she had been, her hair drifting upward.

“Tell me,” I demanded.

“Into lives that have already ended.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the only warning you are capable of understanding.”

I almost laughed. The sound came out thin. “You keep mistaking fear for obedience.”

I slid the tray’s corner beneath the first of the three braided threads. It resisted, vibrating against the brass. The spirit’s hand closed halfway.

“No.”

The pressure struck at once. The black band dropped, and the iron rim crushed my little finger. Pain flashed through me, clean and ordinary. I cried out, but I did not release the woman’s thread. With my free hand I seized the spirit’s shadow where it crossed my knee. It felt like wet smoke, cold enough to burn.

“Open your hand,” I said.

He stared at me.

I tightened my grip on the shadow. It writhed between my fingers, connected to him by a narrow dark cord. “Or I pull this through the seam and find out what you are attached to.”

The chamber went still. Even the souls paused in their turning.

Slowly, the spirit opened his hand.

The clamp rose. My finger came free. The brass tray slid beneath the braided strands, and the first one loosened with a soft, bright note. Somewhere beyond the doorway, a clock began to strike without sound. I shifted my knees backward, inch by inch, dragging the tray and the freed strand with me toward the chamber’s inner wall. The stone behind my shoulders unfolded into a narrow passage, its floor marked by the same hooked symbols as the black band.

I did not step through yet. I only moved far enough to see where the symbols led.

They formed an arrow pointing away from the clamp.

Toward the spirit.


The arrowhead pointed at the spirit’s chest.

I kept one hand on the iron rim and the other around the loosened strand. My injured finger throbbed beneath the blood, but the pain helped me measure the present. The passage behind me was no illusion. Its stones had edges. Dust lay in the grooves. The hooked symbols continued down its walls, their marks shining whenever the souls above shifted in their spiral.

The spirit looked past me into the passage. “Do not follow it.”

“Then you know where it goes.”

“It goes where all pressure goes.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the answer you need.”

His shadow had not returned to his feet. It remained caught between my fingers, a thin black cord pulled taut from the floor to his wrist. I could feel something traveling through it—not blood, not breath, but a procession of moments. A hand closing around a child’s, a door opening onto snow, a woman turning toward a voice that had already stopped calling. The sensations vanished before I could grasp them.

I drew the brass tray another inch. The loosened strand slid into it, and the suspended woman dropped a fraction. Her thread warmed sharply against my palm. I tightened my grip beneath it, keeping it from touching the stone.

“I’m moving the braid,” I said. “You’re going to keep the rim lifted.”

The spirit’s mouth tightened. “You presume cooperation.”

“I presume you don’t want the chamber to collapse.”

That struck more deeply than the threat had. He raised his hand, fingers spread. The iron rim lifted from the fitting with a groan, and the pressure in the woman’s thread eased. Above us, the suspended souls began turning again, though their spiral now contained gaps. Through each gap I saw different pieces of the chamber: the floor drowned in seawater, the ceiling covered in clocks, my own body kneeling here with its eyes shut.

I stepped backward into the passage.

The change was immediate. The air lost its stale cold and became crowded with whispers. The walls leaned inward, then corrected themselves. Behind me, the chamber narrowed to a bright wound, the spirit framed within it. I had crossed only two paces, but the doorway already seemed impossibly far away.

The hooked symbols pulsed beneath my boots. Each pulse tugged at the strand in the tray. I knelt and examined the nearest mark. It was not carved into the stone; it had been pressed there from the other side, as if a finger made of darkness had pushed through the wall.

The strand answered it.

I set the tray down and touched the symbol with my uninjured hand. A vision opened—not the spirit’s shifting corridor, but a single memory held perfectly still. The spirit stood in an older chamber, his body thin and bright, while hundreds of soul-threads passed through him. He was not holding the clamp closed. The clamp was holding him in place.

I pulled my hand back. The vision vanished.

“You’re caught,” I said.

The spirit’s face became very still. “I am containing what is caught.”

“No. You’re the knot.”

The chamber behind him buckled. The iron rim dipped. I heard the trapped woman’s thread draw tight, and the three braided strands in the tray began to twist out of sequence. One carried the scent of bread; another flashed with hospital light; the third filled my palm with the cold weight of a red mitten. Their ends started slipping toward different points in the wall.

I seized all three.

The force nearly pulled me off my knees. The passage stretched, its far end multiplying into a forest of doorways. In one, Elsie stood beneath the awning, alive and watching the rain. In another, the shop was empty. In a third, she reached toward the mirror and her hand passed through it as though the glass were water.

“No,” I whispered.

The temptation came quietly: release one braid, let one life fall out of sequence, and use the freed pressure to reach the spirit. A sacrifice smaller than Elsie. A stranger’s death instead of hers. My body leaned toward the choice before I understood I was moving.

Then the red mitten tightened in my palm.

I remembered nothing of the child who had worn it, yet the thread carried the shape of being held. I planted my knees against the stone and pulled the three strands back into alignment. “Not you,” I said. “None of you.”

The symbols flared. A crack opened beneath the spirit’s feet, revealing not darkness but a vast turning field of souls. His shadow cord plunged into it. He staggered, and the iron rim fell another inch.

I dragged the tray forward, using my shoulder against the passage wall, and hooked its brass edge beneath the nearest row of symbols. The marks peeled away from the stone like black thorns. Beneath them lay a pale thread running from the spirit’s chest to the clamp.

Physical evidence.

I wrapped the loosened strand around my wrist and reached for that thread. The spirit shouted—not in anger, but pain.

The sound shook dust from the ceiling. I gripped the pale line and pulled.

The passage opened behind me, and the chamber’s trapped souls began to pour toward the gap. I did not let go. I held the line, held the three braided strands, held the suspended woman’s thread above the stone, and forced the spirit’s attachment into the light.

“Now,” I said, breathless, “we find out what you’ve been saving mankind from.”
